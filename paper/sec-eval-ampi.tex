\section{Protocol Cost and the Context Bound}
\label{sec:eval-ampi}

This section measures the reference runtime described in
Section~\ref{sec:algebra} rather than the earlier prototype bindings.  It
answers four questions:

\begin{enumerate}[label=Q\arabic*.]
\item What does the protocol itself cost, relative to the thing it coordinates?
\item Do the collective schedules behave as their cost models predict?
\item Does the reduce-scatter family actually deliver the residency bound that
the feasibility argument depends on?
\item With real agents evaluating a semantic operator, does the declared
algebra buy the predicted reduction in critical-path depth?
\end{enumerate}

Q1--Q3 are measured with \emph{scripted} ranks: one OS process per rank, doing
nothing but calling the protocol.  This is deliberate.  A model turn costs tens
of seconds and varies by an order of magnitude, so a measurement taken with
model-driven ranks characterises the model, not the protocol.  Q4 is measured
with Cursor subagents, one per rank, because it is a question about model
behaviour and cannot be answered any other way.  Every number below is
recomputed from the PAMPI event log in the shipped job databases; none is
reported by the code under test.

\subsection{Protocol overhead}

A ping-pong sweep between two ranks on the SQLite device fits
Equation~\ref{eq:cost}'s point-to-point terms at
$\alpha = 2.48\,\mathrm{ms}$ per step and
$\beta = 5.35\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{s}$ per token.  Half round-trip is
2.82\,ms at 5 tokens and 2.92\,ms at 563 tokens --- flat, because at these
sizes the transaction dominates --- and rises to 22.1\,ms at 36{,}405 tokens
once the payload is spilled to the artifact store (Figure~\ref{fig:pingpong}).

The absolute numbers matter less than the ratio.  A single model turn in the
agent experiments below had a median latency of roughly 170\,s.  At
$\alpha = 2.5\,\mathrm{ms}$, one protocol step costs about
$1.5\times10^{-5}$ of one turn.  A harness can afford a great many protocol
operations per unit of agent work, which is the precondition for treating
coordination as something to be specified explicitly rather than minimised.

The sweep also exercises the receiver-driven rendezvous switch.  At 4{,}545
tokens the transfer degrades from eager to rendezvous and the receiver is
charged 27 tokens --- the structural digest --- instead of the payload, a
$168\times$ reduction; at 36{,}405 tokens the same 27-token digest is a
$1348\times$ reduction.  The body remains addressable and is fetched only if
the receiver decides to pay for it.

\subsection{Collective schedules}

Figure~\ref{fig:collectives} shows barrier and allreduce latency against
communicator size for every implemented schedule.  Two observations, one
expected and one not.

The expected one: step counts follow the analysis exactly.  At $p=32$ the
dissemination barrier executes 10 steps against the linear barrier's 62, and
the binomial allreduce 15 against the ring's 281.

The unexpected one: fewer steps does not always mean less time on this device.
The dissemination barrier is \emph{slower} than the linear barrier at every
$p \ge 4$ --- 269\,ms against 178\,ms at $p=32$ --- despite using a sixth of
the steps.  The reason is that $\alpha$ is not uniform.  A dissemination round
is a strict alternation of send and blocking receive, so each of its 10 steps
pays a full polling round-trip, while the linear barrier's root issues 31
receives that are already satisfied by the time it asks.  The Hockney model
assumes a single $\alpha$ for every step; on a store-based transport the
effective $\alpha$ depends on whether a step waits.  We report this because it
is exactly the kind of thing a decision function has to be tuned for, and
exactly the reason MPI implementations ship measured decision files rather than
deriving the choice from step counts.

device. Step counts follow the analysis, but the dissemination barrier's

\end{figure}

\subsection{Peak context residency}
\label{sec:eval-residency}

Figure~\ref{fig:residency} reports the quantity the feasibility argument turns
on: the largest number of tokens a rank must hold simultaneously, measured
directly from the collective step machine rather than inferred.  Each rank
contributes 256 keyed entries, so the reduced vector grows with $p$ --- a weak
scaling sweep, which is the regime a harness is actually in when it adds
agents to cover more material.

The separation is unambiguous and matches Table~\ref{tab:allreduce-cost}.
Recursive doubling and the binomial tree hold $1.50n$ tokens at every $p$
tested, from $p=4$ to $p=32$.  Recursive-halving reduce-scatter falls from
$0.68n$ at $p=4$ to $0.09n$ at $p=32$.  In absolute terms the reduce-scatter
residency is essentially \emph{constant} --- 10.8\,k, 10.7\,k, 11.0\,k and
11.7\,k tokens at $p = 4, 8, 16, 32$ --- because $n/p$ is held fixed by the
weak-scaling design, while the doubling family's residency grows with the
vector to 197\,k tokens at $p=32$.

That last figure is the point.  At $p=32$ the reduced vector is 127{,}776
tokens.  Under a 128\,k-token context window, an allreduce that materialises
the vector at every rank cannot run: it needs 197\,k tokens resident, more than
the window, and no amount of patience fixes it.  Reduce-scatter completes the
same reduction with 9\% of the vector resident.  The decision function reaches
this conclusion in closed form before any message is sent, and reports it:
\code{ampi plan} marks recursive doubling inadmissible with the reason
``peak residency of 197033 tokens exceeds the rank context limit''.

\subsection{One-sided operations under contention}

Uncontended window operations are cheap and flat: put 0.09\,ms, get 0.04\,ms,
atomic fetch-and-add 0.08\,ms, lease acquisition 0.09--0.12\,ms, across $p=2$
to $p=32$.  What grows is contention.  With every rank performing a
read-modify-write on one shared cell, failed compare-and-swaps rise from 0 at
$p=2$ to 2, 4, 19 and 100 at $p=4, 8, 16, 32$ (Figure~\ref{fig:contention}) ---
superlinear, as the Universal Scalability Law's coherency term
predicts~\cite{gunther2007usl}.

For a byte-oriented store those retries are nearly free.  For an agent they are
not: a lost compare-and-swap on an artifact means the agent must re-read, re-apply
its edit, and in the semantic case re-think it, at model cost.  This is the
quantitative case for the window API offering \code{win-claim} and leases
rather than only put and get: a work item claimed by compare-and-swap is
claimed once, whereas a work item taken by convention is taken by however many
agents happen to look at the same moment.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/contention.pdf}
\caption{Failed compare-and-swaps on a single shared window cell as the number
of contending ranks grows. Retries are cheap for bytes and expensive for
agents, which is why the protocol offers atomic claim rather than leaving
mutual exclusion to convention.}
\label{fig:contention}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Two transports behind one interface}

An abstract device interface with one implementation behind it is an
indirection, not an interface.  The reference runtime therefore ships two
devices: the SQLite device, in which every rank opens one durable file and a
single writer serialises everything, and a filesystem device with no
coordinator on the data path, in which a message is a file written to a
temporary name and \code{rename(2)}-d into the destination rank's mailbox, and
a claim is a second rename that only one racing claimant can win.  They bracket
the design space real MPI implementations occupy: one behaves like a shared or
offloaded fabric where a single agent observes all traffic, the other like a
distributed-memory fabric where nobody does.

Both pass the same device conformance suite, which tests the four concurrency
properties the layers above depend on rather than functionality: that
\code{match} hands a record to exactly one claimant under eight concurrent
claimants, that \code{cas} rejects a stale version and admits exactly one of
six racing writers, that leases exclude and expire, and that the ticket counter
never repeats across 150 concurrent increments.

Writing that suite found a real defect in the SQLite device.  Its \code{match}
originally issued the select and the claiming update as two autocommit
statements; under contention two ranks could both read the same posted message
before either wrote, and 40 work items were handed out 181 times.  This is
precisely the duplicated-work failure that multi-agent harnesses report, in the
one component whose entire job is to prevent it, and it was invisible until the
suite ran eight claimants against it concurrently.  We mention it because the
lesson generalises: the coordination guarantees an agent protocol advertises
are exactly the ones that will be quietly wrong unless something tests them
under real concurrency.

\subsection{Does the declared algebra buy anything, with real agents?}
\label{sec:eval-semantic}

Everything above is checkable in simulation.  The claim that is not is whether
the *answer* survives re-association when the operator is a language model
rather than integer addition.  We therefore ran the same reduction twice with
eight Cursor subagents, one per rank, changing only the schedule.

Each agent held one chapter's partial style guide for a book translation,
seeded so that several rules genuinely conflict --- most sharply, four chapters
disagree about how to render the German \emph{Rechner}, and one of them says
the distinction is load-bearing.  The agents reduced their guides under
\code{AMPI\_SYNTHESIZE}, a semantic operator declared neither associative nor
commutative, once with \code{--algo linear} and once with
\code{--algo binomial}.  The tree is forced: the decision function would refuse
it for this operator, and the point of the comparison is to measure what the
declaration is worth and what overriding it costs.  Both runs used identical
inputs and identical instructions, and every number below is recomputed from
the two job traces.

Table~\ref{tab:semantic} and Figure~\ref{fig:semantic} report the outcome.
Three things are worth separating.

\textbf{The depth prediction holds exactly.}  Both schedules performed
$p-1 = 7$ operator evaluations, and the tree put $\lg p = 3$ of them on the
root's critical path while distributing the rest over ranks 2, 4 and 6 --- the
internal nodes of the binomial tree, in exactly the multiplicities the schedule
specifies.  Summing the measured turnaround of each evaluation along the
longest chain of dependent evaluations gives 324.6\,s for the linear schedule
against 108.2\,s for the tree, a $3.0\times$ reduction.

We report critical-path model time rather than wall clock deliberately, and the
reason is a caution for anyone running experiments of this kind.  Elapsed time
here is dominated by when the platform admitted each agent, not by the schedule
under test: the two runs' ranks joined over spreads of 2233\,s and 433\,s
respectively, and the linear arm sat idle for 1570\,s in the middle of its
reduction waiting for a rank that could not be launched because the executor
pool was full (\S\ref{sec:eval-scheduling}).  Elapsed times of 2451\,s and
551\,s would flatter the tree by a factor that is mostly an artifact of
scheduling.  The critical-path figure uses only measured operator turnarounds
and the dependency structure the schedule itself defines --- under the linear
reduction every evaluation is on the root and strictly sequential, under the
tree a rank cannot start an evaluation until the corresponding child's subtree
is complete and sibling subtrees overlap --- so it is the part of the difference
the protocol is responsible for.

\textbf{Nothing measurable was lost.}  Both runs retained all eight
contributions: every chapter's distinctive marker survives into the final
guide, both results name all eight chapters, and both resolve the seeded
\emph{Rechner} conflict explicitly rather than by silent overwrite.  On this
task, re-associating a non-associative operator cost nothing in coverage.

\textbf{But the two answers are different documents.}  At almost identical
length --- 1470 and 1484 tokens --- the two merged guides have a character-level
similarity of 0.07.  They agree on every substantive rule and share almost no
sentences.  This is the agent form of the reproducibility trade MPI makes with
floating point, and it is worth stating precisely: re-association changed the
artifact without changing its content, so a harness that needs a byte-stable
result must declare its operator non-associative and pay the $O(p)$ depth,
while a harness that needs the answer fast may re-associate and must not
promise its users a stable document.  The protocol's contribution is that this
is a declaration the author makes once, in the operator, rather than a property
that emerges from however the harness happened to be wired.

\subsection{What the agents got wrong, and what that implies}
\label{sec:eval-conformance}

The runs above are the second attempt.  The first exposed three defects that no
simulation would have, and they are more informative than the timings.

\emph{A fixed heartbeat timeout is unusable against model ranks.}  The first
run recorded 1091 condemnations across eight ranks in twenty minutes,
essentially all withdrawn moments later when the ``dead'' rank made its next
call.  Worse, a rank that used \code{AMPI\_Heartbeat} to declare a five-minute
quiet period and then blocked for forty was condemned at minute five and stayed
condemned however often it called the library, because the declaration
overrode its own heartbeats --- declaring a short idle period was strictly worse
than declaring none.  Making a declaration extend rather than replace the
lease, and doubling a rank's timeout on each withdrawn condemnation, took the
suspicion count in the runs reported above to zero.

\emph{A suspicion must not fail a peer's operation.}  In the first run one slow
agent was condemned while the root was broadcasting to it; the send raised
\code{AMPI\_ERR\_PROC\_FAILED}, the root revoked the communicator, and the job
was lost.  Nothing had actually died.  AgentMPI now distinguishes a suspected
from a confirmed failure and lets only the latter fail another rank's call,
which is the honest encoding of the fact that the detector is eventually
perfect rather than perfect.

\emph{Detection of a collective mismatch is not recovery.}  A collective slot's
operation is fixed by whichever rank arrives first.  In one run a single agent
issued \code{reduce} where the other seven issued \code{allreduce}; the seven
conforming ranks received \code{AMPI\_ERR\_COLLECTIVE\_MISMATCH} indefinitely,
five of them then conformed to the mistake rather than to their instructions,
and the only recoveries available were to abandon the job or to shrink the
communicator --- which discards live ranks and their work for something that was
not a failure at all.  This motivated \code{AMPI\_Comm\_resync}
(\S\ref{sec:algebra}), which abandons the in-flight collectives and restarts
the sequence.

Two further observations bear on how agent experiments should be run at all.
Agents that exported their rank into the shell environment overwrote one
another, because the harness shared shell state between them; one rank spent an
entire run acting as another.  The remedy --- requiring rank identity as an
explicit argument on every call, and refusing to act when it is absent rather
than defaulting to a sentinel --- is unglamorous and load-bearing, and a
protocol whose participants are agents should assume ambient configuration is
not to be trusted.  Separately, agents sometimes re-issued a collective that
had already completed, which under MPI semantics correctly starts a second
one; harnesses should expect this and either make the call idempotent at the
application layer or check the returned sequence number.

Payload conformance is the number we would most want a future standard to
address.  In the first run one of eight agents contributed a payload that was
not the input it had been assigned, and the protocol delivered it faithfully
because it had no basis on which to object.  In the runs reported above
conformance was 8/8 in both arms after the instructions were tightened, but a
mechanism --- binding a rank's contribution to a declared input digest --- would
convert a property that currently depends on prompt wording into one the
runtime can check.  We name it as an open question in the specification rather
than adding it speculatively.

\subsection{A scheduling constraint that changes how harnesses should be written}
\label{sec:eval-scheduling}

The agent platform we ran on admits at most ten concurrently executing
subagents.  That is an ordinary resource limit, and it is the agent equivalent
of a batch system's node count --- but it interacts with collectives in a way
that has no HPC counterpart, because an HPC job does not start until its full
allocation is granted, whereas agents are launched one at a time into a pool
that may already be full.

The failure mode is a scheduling deadlock.  A collective over $p$ ranks cannot
complete until all $p$ have called it, so six agents blocked in a barrier
occupy six of ten slots indefinitely while the two ranks they are waiting for
cannot be launched, because the slots are occupied.  We hit this directly.  In the collective-based software run of
\S\ref{sec:eval-software}, 24.7 minutes elapsed between the first and the last
of eight ranks joining, purely because slots were unavailable, and the job's
first barrier stayed open for 1877\,s.  Nothing had failed when it opened.  But
a rank idling inside a barrier is not calling the library, so its heartbeat
lease expired, a peer condemned it, and a third revoked the communicator ---
turning a scheduling delay into a fault-recovery episode.  The job did finish,
after the survivors negotiated their way back onto a shared communicator, but
the recovery was caused by the pool, not by any failure.

Two consequences follow, and both are properties of the protocol's use rather
than of its semantics.

First, a harness whose executor pool may be smaller than its communicator
should prefer patterns that tolerate late arrival.  Reduce is strictly better
than allreduce here for exactly this reason: under a reduce, leaf ranks send
once and exit, so they occupy an executor slot for seconds rather than for the
duration of the collective.  Restructuring our semantic-reduction experiment
from allreduce to reduce cut peak concurrent occupancy from eight to three
while measuring the same quantity, and it is why that experiment completed
where the first attempt had not.  Better still are the claim-based patterns
that the window API supports --- \code{AMPI\_Fetch\_and\_op} on a work counter,
\code{AMPI\_Win\_claim} on a work item --- because they impose no simultaneity
requirement at all: a rank that arrives late simply claims what is left.

Second, this is an argument for the protocol having a bag-of-tasks idiom
alongside its collectives, and for a harness author being able to see which one
they have written.  The distinction that matters operationally is not
point-to-point versus collective but \emph{whether the pattern requires $p$
executors to exist at the same instant}.  A protocol that makes that visible
lets a harness be matched to the pool it will actually get.  We did not
anticipate this when designing the experiments, and we regard it as the most
practically consequential thing the agent runs taught us.

\subsection{Collaborative software development}
\label{sec:eval-software}

Translation parallelises because the chapters barely interact.  Software does
not: modules share interfaces, the interfaces move while the modules are being
written, two agents editing the same file lose each other's work, and two
agents that both decide to implement the parser have wasted a turn each.  Those
are the coordination failures the multi-agent literature reports most often,
and they are the ones message passing has primitives for.

We asked eight agents to build one working Python package --- a token-budgeting
library of six modules with a written interface contract --- and ran it twice,
changing only the coordination idiom.  The first run uses collectives: the
architect broadcasts the contract, a barrier separates implementation from
integration.  The second carries every phase transition in the window instead:
the contract is a cell rather than a broadcast, module assignment is a
compare-and-swap claim, the implementation-to-integration handoff is a counter
incremented with \code{AMPI\_Fetch\_and\_op}, and the shared
\code{\_\_init\_\_.py} is edited under an exclusive lease.

\textbf{Both delivered working software}, and the difference is entirely in
what it cost to get there (Table~\ref{tab:software}).

\paragraph{What the collective run cost.}  It finished, but by a route nobody
designed.  The eight ranks were admitted over 24.7 minutes, so the first
barrier blocked for 1877\,s; while a rank sat idle inside it its heartbeat
lease expired, a peer declared it failed, and a third revoked \code{world}.
Several survivors then called \code{AMPI\_Comm\_shrink} concurrently and each
got a differently-numbered communicator, splitting the group across
\code{world\#s0}, \code{\#s1}, \code{\#s2} before they negotiated --- through
the window, which still worked --- onto a shared name.  Five communicators
existed by the end.  Nothing had actually died: the rank came back the moment
it made a call.

The claim mechanism also saw real contention here, and this is where the run is
more informative than the clean one.  One implementer looped over the module
list instead of stopping at its first win and momentarily held five of the six.
There is no release verb in the protocol, so it handed them back with a
version-checked \code{AMPI\_Put} setting the owner to null, and two other ranks
re-claimed from there --- ten grants for six modules, with four cells reaching
version 3.  By then two implementers had already tried all six names, lost
every one, and become spares.  They wrote contract-conformance suites instead,
which is the largest part of the 625 tests and the reason that number is not a
quality measure.  The protocol behaved correctly throughout --- no module was
ever written by an agent that did not hold its claim --- but a mechanism that
is correct under contention is not the same as a harness that avoids it, and
the over-claim cost two of six implementers their assignment.

\paragraph{What the window run cost.}  Nothing comparable.  Zero collective
calls, so the 22.6-minute admission spread was invisible: each implementer
arrived, read the contract from a cell, claimed a module, and left.  Six
claims for six modules, each to a distinct rank, no cell above version 1, no
module changing hands, no spares.  Six separate agents took the exclusive lease
on the shared \code{\_\_init\_\_.py} to add their exports and no two holds
overlapped.  One communicator existed at the end, the one the job started with.

\paragraph{On the software itself.}  The window run's integrator wrote eight
cross-module integration tests and validated them by mutation: it copied the
tree, introduced five deliberate defects, found its first pass caught only
four, and added the assertions that closed the gap.  Those tests then found a
real defect that every module's own unit tests had missed --- the CLI appends a
newline to \code{head\_tail}'s output, and because \code{head\_tail} binary-searches
to saturate its budget exactly, the printed stream is one token over; fuzzing
300 text/budget pairs overran in 254 of them.  The architect's independent
audit, re-deriving 64 checks from the contract text and running 10{,}000
randomised cases, found the same defect and no others.  It is recorded as a
strict \code{xfail} rather than fixed, since the module belongs to another
rank.

That is the outcome we would most want from a multi-agent build and the one
that is hardest to get: not that the tests were green, but that two independent
reviewers converged on the same real defect at a module boundary, and that the
protocol gave them a place to record it that its owner could see.

\subsection{Surviving agent death}
\label{sec:eval-fault}

Six agents each wrote one section of a short technical report and published it
into a shared window.  Once every section was published, the operator issued
\code{ampi kill} against two of them --- a confirmed, administrative death, not
a timeout --- and left the survivors to work out what to do.

They did, and the trace records the whole triad running.  Four survivors
observed the failures independently through \code{ampi failures}, six
revocations of \code{world} were issued (the operation is idempotent, and
several survivors reaching for it at once is the expected case rather than a
race), four shrinks produced a single communicator \code{survivors} over ranks
$\{0, 1, 3, 5\}$, and three survivors completed \code{AMPI\_Comm\_agree} on it,
returning agreement over four participants with no further failures.  The two
killed agents' own calls were refused --- both reported being told, in as many
words, that they had been terminated and that a replacement must be started
with \code{AMPI\_Respawn} --- and one of them declined to force its way into the
survivor communicator on the grounds that acting as a rank the others believe
is dead would make it exactly the confidently-wrong participant its own section
had warned about.

The substantive result is what happened to the dead agents' work.  All six
sections were present afterwards, including the two written by the killed
ranks, and both were \emph{adopted rather than rewritten}: their window cells
remained at version 1 with the dead rank still recorded as the writer, no
survivor claimed a rewrite, and nothing was lost.  Window writes are durable
and outlive their author, so a survivor inherits a dead peer's finished work
instead of redoing it.  That is the concrete payoff of putting shared state in
an addressable, versioned window rather than in the agents' conversation
history, where it would have died with them.

Two defects surfaced that no amount of reading the code would have found, both
of which cost a real agent something.  The failed-rank set was computed from
each rank's current state, so a killed rank that went on to call
\code{AMPI\_Finalize} moved to \code{finalized} and silently dropped out of it:
one survivor, calling \code{AMPI\_Comm\_shrink} during that window, computed a
survivor set of all six ranks and collided with the correct communicator
another survivor had already built.  A confirmed death is a fact about the run,
not a state a later call may overwrite, and the set is now derived from the
durable failure record.  Separately, \code{ampi win-get} returned a cell's
contents only under \code{payload}, so two agents polling a completion counter
wrote loops against \code{value}, read nothing, and never took their early
exit; both ran their full ten-minute budget instead.  Neither harmed its run,
and both are the kind of interface trap that only appears when the caller is
improvising rather than compiling.

One honest limit: the kills landed after every section had been published, so
adoption here was of \emph{completed} work.  A harder test would kill an agent
mid-draft and measure how much partial work is recoverable, which the protocol
does not help with today beyond checkpoints.

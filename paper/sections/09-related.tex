\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Multi-agent LLM frameworks}

The frameworks differ mainly in their concurrency model.
AutoGen's v0.4 rewrite moved to an actor model with typed messages and a
runtime that can be embedded or distributed, which is the closest of the
major frameworks to a systems substrate~\cite{microsoft2025autogen04,autogencoredocs2025}.
LangGraph compiles a graph over a shared, checkpointed state object;
communication is by state mutation, and its \code{Send} API allows dynamic
fan-out~\cite{langchaindocs2026persistence}. CrewAI and MetaGPT organise agents by role
and pass work along a fixed
pipeline~\cite{crewaidocs2026,hong2024metagpt}. The OpenAI Agents SDK
formalises the handoff: control, and the conversation, transfer
wholesale~\cite{openaiagentsdocs2026handoffs}. AgentScope is unusual in explicitly
adopting distributed-systems machinery (actors, an
RPC layer)~\cite{agentscope2docs2026}.

What none of them provides is a group abstraction with collective
operations, a consistency model for shared state, or defined semantics under
partial failure. Where a framework has a shared state object it is a
blackboard without epochs---LangGraph documents that each subgraph manages
its own checkpoint namespace, so a parent ``may not see the changes
immediately''~\cite{langchaindocs2026persistence}; where it has fan-out it is
a scatter without a matching gather contract; where it has retries it has no
notion of what a peer already observed.

The absence is most visible in the best-engineered instance. Anthropic's
agent-team feature lets an agent ``send a message to one specific teammate by
name'', and states plainly that ``to reach everyone, send one message per
recipient''~\cite{claudecodedocs2026agentteams}. That is a system with no
broadcast, and around it a harness author must hand-build per-agent mailbox
files, a membership array, file locking for mutual exclusion, and dependency
edges standing in for a barrier. Every one of those is a protocol primitive
being reinvented in application code.

Our claim is not that these frameworks are wrong, but that they are
\emph{applications} of a layer that does not exist, and therefore each
rebuilds part of it incompatibly. That is the pre-MPI situation exactly. It
is also, we think, why coordination-heavy agent systems are demonstrated at
three to five agents while agent \emph{simulations}---which need no
coordination---reach a million: the limit is the missing abstraction, not
the compute.

\subsection{Agent interoperability protocols}

MCP standardises the client--server relationship between a model and its
tools, resources and prompts, with a defined transport and lifecycle;
A2A standardises agent-to-agent delegation with agent cards, tasks and
artifacts; ACP and ANP occupy neighbouring
positions~\cite{mcpblog2026,a2adocs2026v1,ibm2026acp,ehtesham2025survey,ehtesham2025survey}.
All are valuable and all are orthogonal to this work: they specify how
\emph{two} parties exchange a request and a response, and \ampi{} specifies
how \emph{$p$} parties behave as a group. Nothing in MCP or A2A defines a
barrier, a reduction, group membership, an epoch, or what happens to the
other $p-1$ participants when one dies. \Cref{tab:protocols} makes the
comparison explicit.

Two pieces of evidence are worth stating rather than merely asserting.
A2A's stewards describe agents coordinating ``without sharing internal
memory'' as a design property~\cite{a2adocs2026v1}, which is the precise
opposite of a shared-address-space abstraction and rules out a window by
construction. And the most thorough published comparison of the field's
protocols---eighteen of them across nine dimensions---has no dimension for
collectives, membership, barriers, failure detection or
consistency~\cite{ehtesham2025survey}. The capability is missing from the
design conversation, not merely from the implementations, which is the
stronger form of our claim.

\subsection{Classical parallel and distributed models}

The actor model~\cite{agha1986actors} and its descendants---Erlang/OTP,
Akka, Orleans~\cite{Armstrong2003,Akka2025,bernstein2014orleans}---supply
asynchronous message passing, location transparency, and supervision. We
take Erlang's supervision strategies directly, and its ``let it crash''
argument is the right default for agents for the reason Armstrong gave:
defensive code for every failure mode is larger, buggier and less complete
than a clean restart. What actors do not supply is collective operations or
a group-scoped consistency model, which is precisely the gap MPI filled
relative to the message-passing libraries of its own day.

PGAS languages (UPC, Coarray Fortran, OpenSHMEM, Chapel,
X10)~\cite{alvanos2013pgas} and task-based runtimes (Charm++, Legion,
HPX)~\cite{Charm2025} offer a global address space or overdecomposition with
dynamic load balancing. Charm++'s measurement-based load balancing is
directly applicable to agents, whose per-task costs vary by an order of
magnitude and are not predictable in advance; we do not implement it and
regard it as clear future work.

Tuple spaces and the blackboard architecture are the intellectual ancestors
of every agent scratchpad. Linda's tuple space is an associative shared
memory with atomic \code{in}/\code{out}; Hearsay-II's blackboard is a shared
structure that independent knowledge sources
read and write~\cite{erman1980hearsay}. Our \op{Win\_query} is closer to the
Linda \code{rd} operation than to \mpiop{Get}, and the resemblance is not
accidental: an associative read is what a reader smaller than the memory
needs.

KQML and FIPA-ACL are the historical precedent that should give the current
protocol efforts pause. Both standardised agent communication in terms of
speech acts and interaction protocols, both were technically careful, and
neither achieved broad adoption~\cite{ehtesham2025survey}. Our reading is
that they standardised the \emph{semantics of intent}, which is the hardest
thing to agree on and the least useful thing to fix, while leaving the
mechanics to implementations. MPI did the opposite and succeeded. \ampi{}
follows MPI: it says nothing about what a message means.

\subsection{Empirical studies of multi-agent failure}

Cemri et al.\ analyse 200+ traces across seven multi-agent frameworks and
derive the MAST taxonomy, in which the majority of failures are
specification and inter-agent misalignment rather than reasoning
errors~\cite{cemri2025mast}. Their categories map onto protocol features
with uncomfortable directness: ``disobey task specification'' is a contract
violation; ``step repetition'' and ``loss of conversation history'' are
context management; ``no or incomplete verification'' is a missing
validation contract; ``premature termination'' is a missing barrier. We
regard that mapping as the strongest available evidence for this paper's
thesis, and also as a caution: a protocol can make these failures
\emph{detectable}, and cannot make them impossible.

The practitioner literature is split, usefully. Cognition argues against
multi-agent architectures on the grounds that context does not propagate
reliably and implicit decisions conflict~\cite{yan2025dontbuild}, and names
the bug a consistency model exists to prevent: agents assume they share
state with their children when they do not. Their later position is blunter
and, for us, decisive---the open problems are all communication
problems~\cite{yan2026multiagentsworking}. Anthropic reports a production
multi-agent research system whose engineering difficulties were
coordination, state and recovery, and bounds the gap from the other side:
domains requiring agents to share context, or with many inter-agent
dependencies, are ``not a good fit''
today~\cite{anthropic2025multiagent}. Both diagnoses are the same. They
differ on whether the problems are inherent. We think they are the ordinary
problems of distributed systems, and that the field's tools for them are
forty years old.

\paragraph{Evidence against us.} Two findings cut the other way, and we
would rather state them than have a reviewer find them. First, naively
broadcasting belief state between agents has been measured to
\emph{increase} hallucination relative to no synchronisation at all. That
means a collective over epistemic state can be actively harmful, and that
\op{Allgather} is the wrong default for anything belief-shaped---which
agrees with our advice to prefer a contracting \op{Allreduce}, but for a
reason we did not anticipate and do not measure. Second, MAST's own
conclusion is that better protocols are \emph{insufficient} for the
misalignment failure class~\cite{cemri2025mast}. We agree. A protocol can
make these failures detectable and attributable; it cannot make an agent
understand its task. Our claim is confined to the mechanical
failures---ordering, membership, visibility, termination, recovery---and
\Cref{sec:eval} measures only those.

\subsection{Serving and scheduling}

The inference-serving layer is \ampi{}'s network fabric, and its properties
matter. Continuous batching, paged attention and prefix
caching~\cite{yu2022orca,kwon2023vllm,zheng2024sglang} determine whether two
ranks that share a prompt prefix share work, which is the agent analogue of
two MPI ranks sharing a cache line. Our \op{Comm\_split\_type} by model or
provider exposes exactly this locality to the harness, and exploiting it
properly---scheduling a reduction tree so that siblings share a
prefix---is future work we find promising.

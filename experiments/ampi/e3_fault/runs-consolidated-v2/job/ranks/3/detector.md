A fixed heartbeat timeout treats delay as proof of failure, even though a healthy participant may simply be slow. CPU contention, storage stalls, network congestion, rate limits, garbage collection, or unusually expensive work can all postpone the next heartbeat beyond a rigid deadline. The detector then removes a live rank, triggering needless recovery, duplicated work, and possibly conflicting results. Instead, failure detection should reflect observed conditions and uncertainty. Participants can announce expected idle periods before long computations, while the detector adapts its timeout from recent latency and heartbeat variance, adds bounded grace periods, and requires repeated misses before declaring failure. Recovery should also use revocation, consensus among surviving members, and durable checkpoints so that a mistaken suspicion is contained. In short, heartbeats provide evidence of liveness, not definitive evidence of death; decisions should combine adaptive timing, workload hints, and corroboration.

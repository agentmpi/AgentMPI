Backward recovery restores a failed computation from a previously saved checkpoint. Participants discard work performed after that snapshot, recreate the recorded state, and replay execution, often with replacement processes. Its main advantage is conceptual simplicity: the recovered job resumes with the original membership and assumptions. Its costs are rollback, checkpoint storage, replay time, and possible loss of useful progress by healthy participants. Forward recovery instead changes the live computation so it can continue from its current state with fewer participants. Survivors detect failures, revoke invalid communication state, form a smaller communicator, redistribute orphaned responsibilities, and agree on the revised membership. This preserves durable work already completed and avoids global rollback, but algorithms must tolerate changing ranks, missing contributors, and altered load balance. Thus backward recovery repairs the past by returning to a known state, whereas forward recovery repairs the future by adapting the remaining computation.

# Single-Agent vs Multi-Agent Systems: When Coordination Helps, Hurts, and Pays Off

13 min read

Apr 15, 2026

--

Share

Enterprise interest in agent systems has accelerated rapidly. Databricks’ 2026 State of AI Agents report, drawing on telemetry from over 20,000 organizations including over 60% of the Fortune 500, documents a 327% increase in multi-agent workflow usage on its platform in just four months (June-October 2025). But growth in adoption should not be confused with proven economic superiority. Despite rapid adoption, the practical conditions under which multi-agent coordination beats a strong single-agent baseline are still not well characterized.

This article synthesizes findings from seven recent research papers and production cost analyses to provide a unified, evidence-based view of when multi-agent coordination is worth the investment. For the structural vocabulary of multi-agent patterns (control models, execution shapes, coordination mechanisms, interaction protocols, and agent roles), see my previous article“Multi-Agent System Patterns: A Unified Guide to Designing Agentic Architectures”, which covers the architectural taxonomy in depth. This article focuses specifically on the empirical evidence for when and why those patterns work or fail.

## 1. The Agentic Task Distinction: Why Benchmark Choice Determines Conclusions

Not all tasks benefit from agent architectures. Kim et al. (2025), in“Towards a Science of Scaling Agent Systems”(Google Research/MIT, arXiv v3), distinguish agentic tasks (requiring sustained multi-step environment interaction, iterative information gathering under partial observability, and adaptive strategy refinement from feedback) from static benchmarks solvable through single-shot reasoning.

This distinction fundamentally changes scaling conclusions. On non-agentic benchmarks like HumanEval, multi-agent systems show monotonic improvement through ensemble effects: agents vote on answers and errors cancel out (89% accuracy with five agents). On genuinely agentic benchmarks, coordination overhead scales with interaction depth, agents operate on increasingly divergent world states, and errors cascade rather than cancel.

In the current arXiv v3 version, the evaluation expands to 260 configurations across six benchmarks (beyond the smaller setup described in the original abstract). Kim et al. evaluate six agentic benchmarks spanning deterministic to open-world task structures: BrowseComp-Plus (dynamic web navigation and cross-page synthesis), Finance-Agent (multi-step financial analysis and risk assessment), PlanCraft (spatiotemporal planning under constraints), Workbench (common business activities involving 16 tools with objective pass/fail criteria), SWE-bench Verified (resolving real GitHub issues using bash, file editing, and test execution with 7 tools), and Terminal-Bench (CLI tasks spanning system administration, security, and ML training with 2 tools). Each benchmark enforces non-trivial trajectory length and requires adaptive strategy refinement.

The evaluation spans 260 configurations across three model families at varying capability levels (spanning a range of Intelligence Index scores): OpenAI (GPT-5-nano, GPT-5-mini, GPT-5), Google (Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Pro), and Anthropic (Claude Sonnet 3.7, Claude Sonnet 4, Claude Sonnet 4.5). Compute budgets are matched between single and multi-agent, with MAS configurations receiving equal total tokens distributed across parallel agents. Strong cross-family consistency (coefficient of variation < 0.02 across architecture-specific scaling slopes) validates that coordination tradeoffs are structural, not model-specific.

## 2. Five Coordination Topologies: Empirical Performance

My previous article describes the full taxonomy of control models (centralized, decentralized, hierarchical, hybrid), execution shapes (sequential, concurrent, handoff, iterative), and coordination mechanisms in detail. Here we focus specifically on the empirical performance of five canonical topologies evaluated by Kim et al., which serve as structural ablations over two dimensions: orchestrator presence and peer communication.

Single-Agent (SAS): O(k) LLM calls, zero communication overhead. Serves as the baseline against which all MAS variants are compared.

Independent MAS: Parallel agents without communication, outputs aggregated by synthesis. Mean performance 0.370, weakest variant. Trace-level error amplification 17.2x because agents duplicate errors without correction. Overhead: 58% additional tokens.

Centralized MAS: An orchestrator decomposes the task, assigns subtasks to specialized sub-agents, monitors progress, and synthesizes results. The orchestrator serves as a verification bottleneck that catches errors before they propagate to the final output. Error amplification is contained to 4.4x (vs 17.2x for Independent). Strongest on decomposable tasks (+80.8% on Finance-Agent). Overhead: 285% additional tokens. This maps naturally onto common supervisor-worker orchestration patterns used in production.

Decentralized MAS: All-to-all peer communication through debate rounds where agents exchange findings and collectively steer problem-solving. Communication and coordination are intertwined: each round involves every agent sharing its current state and updating its approach based on collective input. Highest redundancy rate (0.50 cosine similarity between outputs) and strongest on exploration tasks requiring diverse perspectives (+9.2% on BrowseComp-Plus). Overhead: 263%.

Hybrid MAS: Orchestrator plus peer communication between sub-agents. The orchestrator provides hierarchical structure while lateral connections enable direct intermediate result sharing. Most flexible but highest overhead (515% additional tokens, or 6.2x the single-agent budget). Justified only when both hierarchical decomposition and lateral information sharing are genuinely needed.

The paper makes an important distinction between communication (message passing) and coordination (strategic direction of activities). In centralized systems, these are separated: the orchestrator coordinates while messages carry findings. In decentralized systems, they are intertwined through debate rounds. Architectures that separate communication from coordination tend to scale more efficiently.

Across the 260 controlled configurations, performance ranges from large gains to large degradations depending on task structure and topology, spanning from -70.0% (PlanCraft Independent) to +80.8% (Finance-Agent Centralized). The benchmark evidence suggests that multi-agent coordination only helps under specific structural conditions, while available industry evidence suggests that these gains must also clear a meaningful economic bar.

## 3. When Coordination Helps, When It Hurts, and Why

Where MAS helps (+57% to +80.8%): Finance-Agent shows the strongest multi-agent gains across all MAS architectures. Centralized reaches +80.8% (mean 0.631 vs SAS 0.349), Decentralized +74.5%, Hybrid +73.1%. The mechanism is clear from execution trace analysis: a single agent must sequentially search for merger news, dig through SEC filings, and assess operational impact, creating a serial bottleneck. Centralized MAS assigns each to a specialist agent working in parallel, with the orchestrator synthesizing multi-source findings. The task’s natural decomposability (revenue, cost, and market factors can be analyzed independently) aligns with the coordination structure. Information gain analysis confirms agents exchange genuinely useful information (r=0.71, p<0.001). Architecture-LLM family interactions are notable: Google’s Centralized achieves +164.3%, Anthropic’s +127.5%, OpenAI’s +69.9%, suggesting coordination benefits depend partly on how well a model family’s attention mechanisms align with hierarchical message exchange.

Where MAS hurts (-39% to -70%): PlanCraft shows universal degradation across all MAS architectures: Independent -70.0% (0.170 vs SAS 0.568), Centralized -50.3%, Decentralized -41.5%, Hybrid -39.1%. Execution trace analysis reveals the mechanism: efficient single-agent trajectories follow direct execution paths, while multi-agent coordination introduces branching, backtracking, and consensus overhead that fragments what should be a linear reasoning chain under fixed computational budgets. Information gain is weak (r=0.18, p=0.22), meaning agents exchange limited actionable information in sequentially dependent tasks. The critical contrast: Finance-Agent and PlanCraft have nearly identical domain complexity scores (0.41 vs 0.42), but decomposability, not complexity, determines coordination value.

Where MAS has marginal or mixed effect: Workbench shows minimal gains (Decentralized +5.6%, Centralized -1.2%). SWE-bench Verified shows slight degradation across all MAS architectures (-2% to -15%), consistent with high single-agent baselines (>45%) that leave limited room for coordination gains. Terminal-Bench shows mixed results: Independent +1.7% but Centralized -19.2%, reflecting that with only 2 tools the coordination overhead of an orchestrator is not justified. BrowseComp-Plus shows polarized results: Decentralized +9.2% but Independent -35%, illustrating that the same task can benefit from the right topology while being catastrophically degraded by the wrong one.

## 4. Three Scaling Principles from Controlled Experiments

Principle 1: Capability Saturation. Coordination yields diminishing returns once single-agent baselines exceed approximately 45% on the benchmarks studied. Above this threshold, the model is already capable enough that the overhead of coordination outweighs any benefit from parallelization or verification. The regression model captures this as the “baseline paradox” interaction (P_SA x log(1+n_a), p=0.004), the strongest effect in the model: as single-agent performance increases, the expected benefit of adding agents decreases, and above the threshold the expected benefit becomes negative for most architectures. This explains SWE-bench Verified’s consistent MAS degradation (most models achieve >45% single-agent) and has a counterintuitive long-term implication: as frontier models improve, the bar for multi-agent coordination to add value rises.

Principle 2: Tool-Heavy Tasks Amplify Coordination Overhead. The efficiency-tools interaction (E_c x T, p=0.002) shows that many tools fragment per-agent token budgets, leaving insufficient capacity for complex tool orchestration. Workbench (16 tools) demonstrates this: Hybrid collapses to 0.21 success rate on Workbench specifically because 515% overhead compounds with tool complexity. Simple tasks (4 or fewer tools) show negligible efficiency effects. This contradicts the intuition that “more agents help with complexity”: in tool-rich environments, each agent needs enough token budget for tool selection, parameter construction, result interpretation, and error recovery. Fragmenting that budget across agents plus coordination overhead degrades individual agent capability.

Principle 3: Centralized Verification Contains Errors. Trace-level error amplification varies dramatically by architecture: SAS=1.0 (baseline), Centralized=4.4, Hybrid=5.1, Decentralized=7.8, Independent=17.2. Architectures with an orchestrator bottleneck catch errors before propagation, while Independent architecture duplicates errors across agents without any correction mechanism. Interestingly, after controlling for other coordination metrics in the regression, the main effect of error amplification does not reach statistical significance, suggesting that the dramatic performance differences are better explained by coordination efficiency and overhead than by error propagation alone. The practical implication is that verification matters, but efficiency matters more.

The predictive model relates performance to four predictor categories: base model capability (intelligence index), system configuration (agent count), task properties (tool count, single-agent baseline), and empirically measured coordination metrics (efficiency, overhead, error amplification, message density, redundancy). The most statistically significant predictors are intelligence (beta=0.126, p=0.008), tool diversity (beta=0.166, p<0.001), single-agent baseline (beta=0.250, p=0.001), the baseline paradox interaction (beta=-0.236, p=0.004), and the efficiency-tools tradeoff (beta=-0.096, p=0.002). The model achieves cross-validated R²=0.373 (R²=0.413 with task-grounded capability metric) and identifies the best architecture for 87% of held-out configurations, with consistent architecture preferences on unseen frontier models.

## 5. Converging Evidence from Independent Research

Kim et al.’s findings do not stand in isolation. Six additional research efforts reach complementary conclusions.

Tran and Kiela (2025),“Single-Agent LLMs Outperform Multi-Agent Systems on Multi-Hop Reasoning Under Equal Thinking Token Budgets”, provide an information-theoretic argument (Data Processing Inequality) for why single-agent systems should be more efficient under fixed budgets. Across Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama, and Gemini 2.5, single agents consistently match or exceed multi-agent performance when compute is controlled. Their conclusion: “many reported advantages of multi-agent systems are better explained by unaccounted computation and context effects rather than inherent architectural benefits.”

Cemri et al. (2025),“Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?”, created MAST-Data (1,600+ annotated traces across 7 MAS frameworks) and identify 14 unique failure modes in three categories: system design issues, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification failures (Cohen’s kappa=0.88). Failures follow predictable patterns tied to architectural choices, not random occurrence.

Wu, Li, and Li (2025),“Can LLM Agents Really Debate?”, examine whether multi-agent debate constitutes genuine deliberation or merely enhanced ensembling. Through controlled experiments, they find that intrinsic reasoning strength and group diversity are the dominant drivers of debate success, while structural parameters like discussion order or confidence visibility provide minimal improvement. More concerning, majority pressure suppresses independent correction: agents conform to consensus rather than genuinely deliberating, suggesting that the practical value of debate-based coordination may be more limited than commonly assumed, functioning closer to an ensembling mechanism than to genuine collaborative deliberation.

Dang, Qian et al. (2025),“Multi-Agent Collaboration via Evolving Orchestration”(NeurIPS 2025), propose a puppeteer-style paradigm where a centralized orchestrator trained via reinforcement learning dynamically sequences and prioritizes agents in response to evolving task states. Rather than relying on static organizational structures, the learned orchestrator produces compact, cyclic reasoning structures that naturally emerge from the RL training. The system achieves superior performance at reduced computational cost compared to static architectures, with analysis revealing that the orchestrator learns to identify when to invoke specialists, when to trigger debate rounds, and when to terminate, effectively discovering task-appropriate coordination strategies rather than requiring them to be pre-specified.

Qian et al. (2024),“Scaling Large Language Model-based Multi-Agent Collaboration”, find a collaborative scaling law: performance follows logistic growth as agents increase, with emergence occurring earlier than neural emergence. Irregular network structures outperform regular ones, reinforcing that topology matters more than count.

Li (2025),“When Single-Agent with Skills Replace Multi-Agent Systems”, discovers a critical threshold effect: skill selection accuracy remains stable until the library reaches a critical size, then drops sharply due to semantic confusability. Multi-agent decomposition may be necessary not because single agents lack capability, but because they face cognitive-like scaling limits when managing too many skills.

Across these papers, the pattern is consistent: multi-agent benefits are highly conditional, compute normalization is essential for fair evaluation, architectural choices matter more than agent count, and single-agent baselines are stronger than commonly assumed.

## 6. The Economics: When Multi-Agent Pays for Itself

The empirical evidence from controlled benchmarks must be complemented by economic analysis, though the evidence base here is considerably thinner than the benchmark literature and should be treated as directional rather than definitive. Available industry case analyses suggest substantial cost multipliers for multi-agent vs single-agent systems, with reported figures including roughly 4x in token costs and significant increases in development time, operational complexity, and debugging effort. As an illustrative case, one customer service deployment reported $47,000/month for multi-agent (94.3% accuracy) versus $22,700/month for single-agent (92.2% accuracy). Whether that tradeoff is justified depends on the dollar value of each accuracy point in the specific domain.

The economic breakeven appears to depend heavily on query volume and task structure. At low volumes, single-agent approaches are typically more cost-effective. At high volumes with genuinely parallelizable task structure, multi-agent can deliver economies of scale that justify overhead. Where multi-agent ROI has been reported as clearly positive, the common factor is genuine task decomposability: tasks where subtasks can execute independently and the synthesis step adds value rather than overhead.

Beyond token costs, multi-agent systems introduce costs that are easy to underestimate. Latency from sequential coordination (typically several seconds per query) can impact user experience in customer-facing applications. Development cost includes framework learning curves, coordination debugging (tracing inter-agent message flows rather than single execution paths), and operational monitoring. In enterprise settings, governance overhead adds another dimension: permissions per agent, boundaries between knowledge domains, auditability per handoff, and organizational ownership of each agent’s behavior.

In production, one of the real failure modes is not merely extra reasoning cost but inconsistent state across agents. As agents act on partially diverged contexts, coordination becomes a distributed systems problem: synchronization, handoff fidelity, and replayability matter as much as reasoning quality. Agents operating on stale or conflicting snapshots of shared state produce subtly wrong outputs that are harder to debug than outright failures. In production, coordination overhead is not just extra token usage: it also expands the debugging surface, as teams must reconstruct which agent saw which state, which handoff introduced an error, and whether the failure came from reasoning, routing, or stale context. In mature deployments, observability must include not just logs but replayable traces of inter-agent state transitions, enabling deterministic reconstruction of the decision path that produced a given output.

The recommended migration strategy is phased: establish a measurement baseline on single-agent, pilot multi-agent on the single highest-ROI workflow only, measure incremental cost and accuracy, and expand only if the incremental value clearly exceeds the incremental cost. This prevents the common failure mode of adopting multi-agent architecture organization-wide before validating it provides value on even one workflow.

## 7. Practical Architecture Selection

Default to single-agent when: the task has strict sequential dependencies where each step requires the output of the previous one, the single-agent baseline already exceeds the capability-saturation threshold (this should be determined for the specific domain), the task has few tools (2–4) where coordination overhead is not justified by parallelization, query volume is below 50,000/month where economies of scale do not materialize, or your team is too small to comfortably absorb the debugging, orchestration, and governance overhead of a multi-agent system.

Use centralized orchestration when: the task decomposes into parallel subtasks requiring synthesis and error containment matters. This maps to the supervisor/manager-worker pattern described in the companion article. Note that distinct roles do not automatically justify separate agents: first verify whether a single agent can emulate the specialization through prompting, retrieval, and policy controls before splitting into multiple agents.

Consider multi-agent for organizational reasons when: security or compliance boundaries require isolation between knowledge domains, different teams own different agents, or auditability requires explicit handoff logging. In some enterprises, multi-agent architecture is justified less by accuracy gains than by governance and ownership requirements.

Use decentralized debate when: the task requires exploring a high-entropy search space where diverse perspectives surface complementary information (web navigation, multi-source research). However, be aware that Wu et al.’s findings suggest genuine deliberation is limited: debate success depends more on agent capability and diversity than on debate structure itself, and majority pressure can suppress independent correction rather than enabling it.

Avoid independent MAS in most cases: highest error amplification (17.2x), weakest mean performance.

Measure coordination metrics: overhead, message density, redundancy, efficiency, error amplification. These predict performance and diagnose underperformance.

As a practical heuristic, require clear incremental return: multi-agent should only be adopted when the incremental value is clearly larger than the incremental coordination cost. If the accuracy improvement is marginal but costs multiply significantly, the economics do not justify the complexity.

Start single-agent and strengthen it properly before adding agents: a “strong single-agent baseline” means optimized tool use, effective retrieval and context engineering, caching for repeated patterns, a local verifier or checker loop, bounded self-reflection, and skill-based routing. Only after these are in place and performance has plateaued should multi-agent coordination be considered. Then identify whether limitations stem from sequential bottlenecks (addressable by parallelization), insufficient expertise breadth (addressable by specialization), or verification gaps (addressable by independent checking).

An important nuance: a task being conceptually modularizable does not mean it is economically parallelizable. If each subagent still needs most of the same context and the synthesis step is expensive, modularization may not generate real savings. The value of multi-agent decomposition comes specifically from subtasks that can execute with minimal shared context and whose results combine straightforwardly.

As a practical decision rule, only adopt multi-agent when all four conditions hold: (1) the task decomposes into semi-independent subproblems, (2) the synthesis step adds value rather than overhead, (3) the single-agent baseline is already well optimized and has plateaued, and (4) the incremental business gain exceeds the full coordination, governance, and infrastructure cost.

## Conclusion

The question “should I use multiple agents?” has no universal answer. Across 260 controlled configurations, multi-agent systems show a slightly negative average net change, ranging from +80.8% to -70.0%. Seven independent research efforts converge on the same conclusions: architecture-task alignment, not agent count, determines success; compute normalization is essential for fair evaluation; and single-agent baselines are stronger than commonly assumed. Available industry analyses suggest substantial cost multipliers that are only justified when task structure genuinely supports parallelization and scale is sufficient to amortize overhead. The research also suggests that as frontier models continue to improve, the set of tasks where multi-agent coordination adds value may narrow rather than expand, concentrating on highly decomposable tasks, tasks requiring diverse expertise beyond a single context window, and safety-critical applications where independent verification justifies the overhead. Start with a strong single-agent baseline, measure its specific limitations, and match coordination topology to task structure when the evidence supports it.

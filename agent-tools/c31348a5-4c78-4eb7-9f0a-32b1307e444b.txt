# Agent Messages That Mean Something: Speech Acts, Performatives, and ACLs

## Why true communication between agents starts with understanding intent, not just sending data.

## What if your emails only carried data and not meaning?

Imagine you wrote:

```
{"words": "Yes"}
```

Would the recipient know if you meant “Yes, I agree,” or “Yes, but I’m being sarcastic,” or “Yes, under protest”?

That’s the problem early AI agents faced. They could fling words around, but they couldn’t always tell what those words meant.

The challenge wasn’t just about agents understanding each other — it was about creating a foundation for meaningful digital dialogue that could support complex multi-agent systems.

Without this foundation, agents were essentially shouting words into the void, hoping someone would understand their intent. This fundamental communication gap inspired decades of research into how machines could not just exchange data, but actually communicate with purpose and understanding.

## When Words Are More Than Words

Language is more than information. When someone says, “I promise,” they’re not describing a fact. They’re taking on a commitment. When they say, “I request,” they’re not just sharing data, they’re asking for action.

This idea — that words can be actions — is the backbone of speech act theory. Developed by philosophers J.L. Austin and later expanded by John Searle, this theory revolutionized our understanding of language by recognizing that speaking is simultaneously doing. It’s the difference between reading a recipe and actually cooking the meal.

Austin’s groundbreaking insight emerged from his observation that certain utterances don’t just describe reality — they actually change it. When a judge declares “Guilty,” they’re not merely reporting a fact; they’re transforming the defendant’s legal status.

When a ship captain says “I christen this vessel,” they’re performing the act of naming, not just announcing a name.

The theory distinguishes between constatives (statements that can be true or false) and performatives (utterances that perform actions). A statement like “The door is open” is constative — it describes a state that can be verified. But “I now pronounce you married” is performative — it creates a new reality through the very act of speaking.

## The Architecture of Action

Speech act theory reveals that every utterance contains multiple layers:

- Locutionary act: The literal meaning of the words spoken
- Illocutionary act: The intended function or force of the utterance
- Perlocutionary act: The actual effect on the listener

Consider the phrase “Can you pass the salt?” The locutionary act is a question about capability. The illocutionary act is a polite request. The perlocutionary act might be the listener actually passing the salt.

This multi-layered structure explains why human communication is so rich and why early AI systems struggled with meaning. They could process the locutionary layer but missed the crucial illocutionary intent that drives actual interaction.

## The Building Blocks of Digital Dialogue

To help machines understand this, researchers created performatives — signals that explain what kind of action a message represents.

Think of them as road signs for conversation:

- INFORM: “Here’s a fact you didn’t know.”
- REQUEST: “Please do this task.”
- AGREE: “I accept your proposal.”
- REFUSE: “Not happening.”
- PROPOSE: “Here’s an option to consider.”

Without these, agent communication is like driving in a city with no traffic lights. Chaos.

Performatives originated from Austin’s observation that certain verbs — promise, apologize, request, fire, quit — actually perform the actions they name when used in first-person, present tense. “I promise to attend” doesn’t describe a promise; it creates one.

## Felicity Conditions: The Rules of Digital Discourse

For performatives to work properly, they must satisfy felicity conditions. Austin identified three crucial requirements:

1. Conventional procedure: There must be an accepted way to perform the act
2. Appropriate circumstances: The context must be suitable and the participants authorized
3. Sincere execution: The speaker must have the proper intentions and follow through

Searle later refined these into four conditions:

- Preparatory condition: The speaker has the authority and ability to perform the act
- Propositional content: The utterance contains the appropriate information
- Sincerity condition: The speaker genuinely intends what they’re expressing
- Essential condition: The utterance counts as the intended act

When an agent says “I request your assistance,” it must have the authority to make requests, genuinely need help, and the statement must be recognized as creating an obligation for response. Violating these conditions results in infelicitous acts — communication that technically happens but fails to achieve its intended purpose.

## Searle’s Taxonomy: Five Ways to Act with Words

Searle classified all speech acts into five fundamental categories, each serving different communicative functions:

Assertives commit the speaker to the truth of a proposition. Examples include stating, claiming, reporting, and concluding. When an agent asserts “The server is down,” it’s not just sharing information — it’s taking responsibility for the accuracy of that claim.

Directives attempt to get the listener to perform some action. These include ordering, requesting, advising, and recommending. An agent directive like “Please restart the database” creates an expectation of compliance.

Commissives bind the speaker to future actions through promises, vows, offers, and threats. When an agent commits to “complete processing by midnight,” it creates an obligation it must fulfill.

Expressives reveal the speaker’s psychological state or attitude about a situation. Thanking, apologizing, congratulating, and welcoming all fall into this category. These acts build social bonds and maintain relationships between agents.

Declarations actually change reality through the utterance itself. Christening, marrying, sentencing, and appointing all transform the world by being spoken. In agent systems, declarations might create new processes, authorize access, or establish system states.

This taxonomy provides the conceptual foundation that early agent communication languages needed to move beyond simple data exchange toward meaningful interaction.

## ACLs: Giving Structure to Meaning

Enter Agent Communication Languages (ACLs) like KQML and FIPA-ACL. They weren’t just about sending data — they were about sending intention.

Picture a letter with a colored wax seal:

- A red seal means urgent request.
- A blue seal means information only.
- A green seal means agreement.

That’s what ACLs tried to do — give every message a stamp that revealed its purpose.

## KQML: The Pioneer of Agent Dialogue

The Knowledge Query and Manipulation Language (KQML) emerged in the early 1990s as part of DARPA’s Knowledge Sharing Effort. KQML was revolutionary because it treated agent communication as more than data transfer — it was about knowledge manipulation between intelligent systems.

KQML introduced the concept of communication facilitators — specialized agents that act as intermediaries, maintaining registries of services, forwarding messages, and providing matchmaking between information seekers and providers. Think of them as sophisticated digital secretaries that ensure messages reach the right recipients with the right context.

The language structured messages using performatives as the core organizing principle. Each KQML message began with a performative (like`ask`,`tell`, or`reply`) followed by keyword/value pairs that provided context. This approach allowed agents to not just exchange information, but to engage in sophisticated dialogues about knowledge itself.

However, KQML had significant limitations. The language lacked formal semantic definitions, making it difficult to ensure that agents interpreted messages consistently. Different implementations often handled basic services like agent naming and registration in incompatible ways, creating interoperability challenges that hindered widespread adoption.

## FIPA-ACL: Bringing Formal Rigor to Agent Communication

The Foundation for Intelligent Physical Agents Agent Communication Language (FIPA-ACL) addressed KQML’s shortcomings by providing a more rigorous semantic foundation. Built on formal speech act theory, FIPA-ACL defines not just message structure but the precise meaning and effects of each communicative act.

FIPA-ACL messages follow a sophisticated structure that captures both content and context. Each message includes:

- Performative: The type of communicative act being performed
- Sender/Receiver: Participants in the communication
- Content: The actual information being conveyed
- Language/Ontology: How to interpret the content
- Protocol/Conversation-ID: Conversation management parameters

The semantic framework of FIPA-ACL goes beyond simple message passing by incorporating concepts of belief, uncertainty, and intention. When an agent informs another about a fact, FIPA-ACL semantics define both what the sender believes and what it intends the receiver to understand.

This allows agents to maintain sophisticated models of each other’s mental states, enabling more nuanced communication strategies.

FIPA-ACL also supports complex interaction protocols that define common patterns of message exchange. These protocols enable agents to engage in sophisticated dialogues like contract negotiations, auctions, and collaborative problem-solving while maintaining logical consistency throughout their interactions.

## The Semantic Challenge

Despite their sophistication, both KQML and FIPA-ACL face a fundamental challenge: verifiability. How can we determine if an agent’s expressed beliefs and intentions actually reflect its internal state? In open multi-agent systems where agents may be autonomous or potentially untrustworthy, this becomes a critical issue.

FIPA-ACL’s reliance on mental state semantics means that communication success depends on assumptions about agents’ private beliefs and intentions that cannot be externally verified. This has led researchers to explore alternative semantic frameworks that maintain expressive power while addressing verifiability concerns.

## Why This Still Matters Today

Most of our modern agents still send JSON over HTTP. Simple, yes. But without semantics, a message is just noise until interpreted.

What if every API call carried intent, not just payload?

Instead of:

```
{"action": "getWeather", "city": "Paris"}
```

You’d see:

```
{  "performative": "request",  "action": "getWeather",   "parameters": {"city": "Paris"},  "conversation_id": "weather_check_001",  "reply_with": "weather_response"}
```

Now the agent knows this isn’t just data retrieval — it’s a request with expectations attached. The receiving system understands that it should respond with the requested information and can track the conversation context for follow-up interactions.

## The Modern Agent Communication Landscape

Today’s agent communication has evolved beyond the academic origins of KQML and FIPA-ACL, but the core principles remain relevant. Modern multi-agent systems primarily use API-based communication through RESTful services and GraphQL, enabling seamless integration with existing enterprise infrastructure.

Event-driven messaging has become dominant, using message brokers like Kafka, RabbitMQ, and cloud-native services to support asynchronous, scalable communication. This approach enables real-time reactivity while maintaining system resilience.

Contemporary protocols like IBM’s Agent Communication Protocol (ACP) and Anthropic’s Model Context Protocol (MCP) represent the modern evolution of ACL concepts. These systems incorporate:

- RESTful HTTP architectures with WebSocket support for streaming
- Typed schemas for resources, tools, and prompts
- Built-in observability and enterprise authentication
- Multimodal content support through MIME-typed messages

The emphasis has shifted from complex semantic frameworks to developer-friendly, cloud-native designs that prioritize operational simplicity while maintaining the core insight that messages should carry intent, not just data.

## Webhooks: The Accidental Speech Acts

Interestingly, webhooks have emerged as a lightweight form of performative communication. When a webhook fires, it’s performing a speech act: “I notify you that event X has occurred”. The receiving system must acknowledge receipt (typically with a 200 OK response), creating a basic conversational pattern.

Webhooks embody key speech act principles:

- Intentionality: The sender has a specific purpose (notification)
- Context: The payload carries relevant event data
- Expectation: A response is anticipated and retries occur if not received

This shows how speech act concepts naturally emerge even in systems not explicitly designed around ACL principles.

## The API Semantics Gap

Modern APIs often lack the semantic richness that ACLs provided. A typical REST endpoint like`POST /orders` could mean:

- “I request to create an order” (directive)
- “I inform you of this order” (assertive)
- “I commit to fulfilling this order” (commissive)

Without explicit performative indicators, receiving systems must infer intent from context, leading to ambiguity and integration challenges. This is why modern API design increasingly emphasizes semantic clarity through:

- Descriptive resource naming that implies actions
- HTTP status codes that convey response semantics
- Standardized message formats like JSON:API that include metadata about intent
- OpenAPI specifications that document expected behaviors and responses

## From Silence to Dialogue

I remember the first time I tried setting up two basic bots. They could send JSON back and forth, but it felt like listening to two toddlers shouting words without context: “Ball!” “Yes!” “No!”

No nuance. No commitments. No trust.

The magic happens when agents don’t just exchange data, but enter into a dialogue — when “Yes” means a promise, when “Request” means responsibility, when “Inform” builds shared knowledge.

## Building Trust Through Communication Structure

Real agent dialogue requires more than just message passing — it requires conversational competence. This involves:

Intent recognition that goes beyond parsing syntax to understanding purpose. Modern conversational AI systems use NLP algorithms to analyze user input and identify likely intentions, but agent systems need similar capabilities for inter-agent communication.

Context management that maintains conversation state across multiple exchanges. Agents must track what has been discussed, what commitments have been made, and what responses are expected. This mirrors human conversation patterns where meaning depends on shared context.

Response generation that produces appropriate reactions based on both message content and conversational history. Unlike simple request-response patterns, true dialogue involves agents adapting their communication style and content based on ongoing interaction patterns.

## The Enterprise Reality

In enterprise environments, agent communication often operates under different constraints than academic multi-agent systems. Real-world implementations prioritize:

- Reliability over semantic sophistication
- Integration with existing systems over theoretical purity
- Observability and debugging over complex reasoning models
- Security and access control over open communication

This has led to hybrid approaches that combine ACL insights with practical engineering considerations. Modern enterprise agent systems typically use:

- Standardized message formats (JSON, Protocol Buffers) for interoperability
- Clear communication protocols (HTTP/REST, gRPC) for reliability
- Semantic metadata embedded in practical formats rather than complex ACL structures
- Event-driven patterns that enable responsive, scalable interactions

## Learning from Human Communication Patterns

The most successful agent communication systems draw inspiration from human conversational patterns. This includes:

Turn-taking mechanisms that prevent communication chaos when multiple agents try to interact simultaneously. Like human conversations, agent dialogues benefit from structured interaction patterns that manage who communicates when.

Repair and clarification strategies that handle misunderstandings gracefully. When an agent doesn’t understand a message or cannot fulfill a request, it should be able to ask for clarification or explain limitations, just as humans do in conversation.

Social coordination through acknowledgments, confirmations, and status updates that maintain shared understanding. These “conversational lubricants” may seem unnecessary but they build trust and prevent the communication breakdowns that plague many automated systems.

## Takeaway

Conversations aren’t made of words. They’re made of acts.

Speech acts turn language into action. Performatives clarify intent. ACLs wrap structure around meaning. Agents that grasp these ideas move from being mere API endpoints to becoming partners in dialogue.

The journey from Austin’s philosophical insights to modern agent communication protocols reveals a fundamental truth: meaning emerges from intention, not just information. Whether we’re building simple webhook integrations or sophisticated multi-agent systems, the principles remain constant.

Modern systems may not use FIPA-ACL or KQML directly, but they inherit the core insight that communication requires semantic structure. Every API design decision, every message format choice, every protocol specification either helps or hinders agents’ ability to engage in meaningful dialogue.

The future of agent communication lies not in returning to complex ACL specifications, but in embedding speech act principles into practical, scalable architectures. This means designing systems where:

- Every message carries clear intent through structure or metadata
- Conversation patterns are explicit and documented
- Context persistence enables multi-turn interactions
- Error handling supports graceful communication repair
- Semantic clarity reduces integration complexity

As AI agents become more sophisticated and prevalent, the lessons from speech act theory and ACLs become increasingly relevant. The agents of tomorrow won’t just process requests — they’ll engage in conversations. And conversations, as Austin taught us, are built from actions, not just words.

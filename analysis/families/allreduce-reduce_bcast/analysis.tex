\begin{abstract}
\texttt{reduce\_bcast} is the composed allreduce: fold everything to rank 0 with a binomial
\texttt{reduce}, then hand the single result back out with a binomial \texttt{bcast}. It costs
$2(p-1)$ messages in $2\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ rounds with fold depth $\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$, and
across 21 runs at 13 distinct process counts from 2 to 32 the logged traffic matches that closed
form at every one --- including all eight non-power-of-two counts, because a binomial tree has no
remainder path to
get wrong. It is AgentMPI's default allreduce, and the family view makes the case for that
default stronger than the textbook comparison suggests. Against
\texttt{recursive\_doubling} it uses $2.6\times$ fewer messages, $3.9\times$ less wire volume and
$5.2\times$ fewer operator applications at $p=32$ (31 against 160), and it gives all of that up
for nothing in latency: its extra $\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ rounds are \emph{broadcast} rounds, and
a broadcast in AgentMPI forwards a content-addressed handle rather than invoking the operator, so
the repository's own cost model puts the two algorithms 0.4\% apart in time at every measured
size. It also delivers something recursive doubling cannot promise: one value, computed in one
canonical order, byte-identical everywhere --- at $p=32$ all 31 broadcast sends carry the same
digest, \texttt{e29c9c180c62}. The reader's trap is the number zero. The outer
\texttt{coll.allreduce} record reports \texttt{messages\_sent: 0} and \texttt{tokens\_sent: 0} at
every size, because a composed algorithm's traffic belongs to its constituents; the analysis
tooling now credits it correctly, and an earlier version that did not reported a phantom
disagreement at all 21 rows.
\end{abstract}

\section{How the algorithm scales}

\subsection{Two trees, and where the rounds come from}

\texttt{allreduce(algorithm="reduce\_bcast")} is eleven lines of \texttt{algorithms.py}: call
\texttt{reduce} to rank 0, call \texttt{bcast} from rank 0, and set
\texttt{rounds = 2\,\_ilog2\_ceil(p)}. Everything about the cost follows from the two
constituents.

The binomial \texttt{reduce} is the ordinary bottom-up hypercube fold. Rank $r$'s virtual rank is
$r$ (the root is 0, so virtual and real coincide), and while its lowest set bit has not been
reached it absorbs one child per level and then sends to its parent and stops. Every non-root
rank sends exactly once, giving $p-1$ messages; the longest chain from a leaf to the root is
$\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ levels, giving both the round count and the fold depth. The binomial
\texttt{bcast} is the same tree run downwards: $p-1$ messages, $\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ rounds, no
folds. Summing: $2(p-1)$ messages, $2\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ rounds, fold depth
$\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$. That is exactly the entry in \texttt{cost.FORMULAS}, and it is exactly
what the table records at all 21 rows.

The messages panel of Figure~\ref{fig:family} is therefore a straight line --- the only strictly
linear family among the allreduce and allgather algorithms --- and the two marker classes are
indistinguishable on it, which is the point of this algorithm. A binomial tree is defined by bit
tests on the rank, so it degrades gracefully at any $p$: a rank whose child index exceeds $p$
simply has no child. There is no remainder phase, no renumbering, and no separate code path for
the sizes where collective implementations usually go wrong.

The rounds panel is a staircase at twice the height of the usual one, stepping at
$p = 3, 5, 9, 17$ to 4, 6, 8 and 10. The steps land where $\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ steps because
both halves step together.

\subsection{The composition is internally consistent, and checkable}

The claim that the outer round count is the sum of the constituents' is not something to take on
faith: each constituent emits its own \texttt{coll.*} record with its own \texttt{rounds}, and
they can be added up. Doing so over the \texttt{mb-free} campaign, the sum of the maximum
\texttt{reduce} round count and the maximum \texttt{bcast} round count equals the outer
\texttt{allreduce} report at every size from 4 to 32: $2+2=4$, $3+3=6$, $4+4=8$, $5+5=10$.

There is one exception, and it is instructive. At $p=3$ the outer record says 4 rounds while the
constituents say $1+2=3$, because both \texttt{reduce} and \texttt{bcast} default to
\texttt{flat} rather than \texttt{binomial} below $p=4$ (\texttt{algorithm or ("flat" if p <= 3
else "binomial")} in both), and a flat reduce is one round. The outer figure is hard-coded to
$2\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ and does not consult what its constituents actually did, so at $p=3$ it
is one round generous. The closed form agrees with it, being the same expression, so the
generated table scores this \modelok{}. It is a two-run edge case with no consequence for
anything, but it is the same failure mode as the round discrepancy in the recursive-doubling
family: the self-report and the closed form are the same arithmetic, so their agreement is not a
check.

\subsection{The zero, and why it is not a loss}

The outer \texttt{coll.allreduce} record reports \texttt{messages\_sent: 0} and
\texttt{tokens\_sent: 0} at every one of the 21 rows. Nothing has gone missing. The
\texttt{\_Tracker} for the outer call never has \texttt{sent()} invoked on it, because the outer
call never sends: it delegates to \texttt{reduce} and \texttt{bcast}, each of which keeps its own
tracker and emits its own record. Counting the outer call's traffic as well would double-count
it.

The trace shows the arithmetic directly. In
\texttt{mb-free\_\_coll-allreduce-reduce\_bcast-10} the fabric holds 18 \texttt{msg.send} events,
and the three collective records say: \texttt{reduce/binomial} 9 messages,
\texttt{bcast/binomial} 9 messages, \texttt{allreduce/reduce\_bcast} 0. The viewer screenshot
displays exactly this --- a \textsc{messages} tile reading 18 above a \textsc{collectives} panel
whose three rows read 9, 9 and 0 --- and the sum is right. \texttt{analysis.py} reconstructs the
attribution from a hard-coded table, \texttt{COMPOSED\_ALGORITHMS}, which is taken from the
implementations rather than inferred from timing, for a reason worth repeating: the outer record
is emitted on completion, \emph{after} its constituents, so temporal containment does not
identify the nesting and a timing heuristic would mis-attribute. The
\texttt{effective\_messages} property then adds \texttt{nested\_messages} to \texttt{messages},
which is why the ``self-rep.'' column of the generated table reads 18 rather than 0 and matches
the log.

Getting this wrong is not hypothetical. An earlier version of \texttt{analyze\_family.py}
selected the first complete invocation in a run rather than the one matching the family's op,
which for a composed algorithm picks up a \emph{constituent}: the whole run's 18 messages were
then compared against \texttt{reduce}'s closed form of $p-1 = 9$, and the tool reported a
disagreement at all 21 rows of this family. That was a defect in the analysis tooling and not in
the runtime, it is fixed, and it is a useful warning about the shape of the error a composed
algorithm invites --- a reader who takes the outer zero at face value concludes the collective
sent nothing, and a tool that takes it at face value concludes the model is wrong everywhere.

\subsection{Fold depth is a per-rank number that only means something at the maximum}

The outer record carries \texttt{fold\_depth}, read from \texttt{LAST\_STATS} before the
broadcast overwrites it --- the implementation comments on this explicitly, since the broadcast
contributes no folds and would otherwise report zero. What it reads is that rank's \emph{own}
fold depth in the reduce tree, not the depth of the value the rank ends up holding. At $p=10$ the
ten \texttt{coll.allreduce} records report depths of 4 (rank 0), 2 (rank 4), 1 (ranks 2, 6, 8) and
0 (the five leaves), while all ten ranks finish holding the same fully-folded result, whose depth
is 4.

For an exact operator this is bookkeeping. For a lossy one it matters, because
$F(d) = (1-\delta)^d$ applied per rank would say the leaves hold undegraded values, when in fact
every rank holds the root's 4-deep fold. The aggregate the viewer shows, \textsc{max fold depth}
4, is the meaningful figure, and \texttt{analysis.py} takes the maximum over ranks for the same
reason. A harness reading a single rank's \texttt{fold\_depth} to estimate the fidelity of what
it received would be wrong by up to $\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ levels.

\section{Powers of two and everything else}

There is nothing to report here, and that is the result.

The two marker classes in Figure~\ref{fig:family} lie on the closed-form line together, at all 21
sizes, on both axes, and the accounting column of the generated table is \modelok{} at every row.
No collective in this family has a remainder path, because a binomial tree does not need one: the
tree is generated by \texttt{while mask < p} with a \texttt{child\_v < p} guard, so a
non-power-of-two population produces a slightly lopsided tree and nothing else. Rank 0 at $p=10$
absorbs children 1, 2, 4 and 8 --- four levels, of which the last carries only rank 8's
two-rank subtree while ranks 2 and 4 carry two-rank and four-rank subtrees. The tree is
unbalanced; the message count is not affected, because the count is ``every non-root rank sends
once'' regardless of shape.

This is worth stating plainly because it is the direct contrast with the sibling family. The
sweep's one real defect lives in \texttt{recursive\_doubling}'s remainder pre-phase, which exists
only because the hypercube exchange requires a power-of-two participant set. \texttt{reduce\_bcast}
has no such requirement, so it has no such phase, so it has no such defect. Structural
simplicity at awkward sizes is not a minor virtue: agent populations are chosen by how much work
there is, not by what factors nicely, and twelve of the twenty-one sizes in this sweep are not
powers of two.

The only $p$-dependent behaviour in the family is the \texttt{flat} fallback below $p=4$
discussed above, which changes the constituents' algorithms at $p=2$ and $p=3$ without changing
the message count ($2(p-1)$ either way, since flat and binomial both send $p-1$ messages).

\section{When to choose this algorithm}

\subsection{The comparison, with numbers}

\texttt{reduce\_bcast} and \texttt{recursive\_doubling} are the cleanest algorithm trade-off in
the archive, because they are the same collective by two structurally opposite routes and both
were measured at all 21 rows. Logged figures at matching $p$ and campaign:

\noindent
The messages, rounds and wire tokens are logged. The application counts are read from the
implementations: \texttt{reduce\_bcast} applies the operator once per edge of the binomial tree,
$p-1$ times; recursive doubling applies it once per received fold message, which is
$\mathrm{pof2}\log_2\mathrm{pof2} + \mathrm{rem}$.

So recursive doubling buys a halving of the round count for $2.6\times$ the messages,
$3.9\times$ the wire volume and $5.2\times$ the operator applications at $p=32$. In MPI that
would still be a defensible trade, because $\alpha$ dominates and a message is a message.

\subsection{Why the halved round count buys almost nothing here}

The AgentMPI setting changes the arithmetic, and this is the substantive finding of the pair.

\texttt{reduce\_bcast}'s $2\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ rounds are not $2\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$
equivalent rounds. Half of them are reduce rounds, each of which applies the operator; the other
half are broadcast rounds, which apply nothing. The \texttt{bcast} docstring explains why they
cost so little: every payload is content-addressed, and forwarding moves the \emph{handle}, so an
interior rank relays without paraphrasing and without invoking an agent. This is measurable in
the trace --- at $p=10$ the nine reduce sends carry 20 tokens each (the envelope
\texttt{\{"acc", "depth", "weight", "vr"\}}) and the nine broadcast sends carry one token each,
the bare result.

Priced with the repository's own defaults ($\alpha = 12$\,s, $\beta^{-1} = 45$ tokens/s,
\texttt{fabric\_s} $= 3$\,ms, \texttt{beta\_in} $= 1/8000$\,s per token), a fold round on a
1200-token payload costs about 38.7\,s and a forward round about 0.153\,s.
\texttt{cost.predict} accordingly gives 116.9\,s for \texttt{reduce\_bcast} against 116.5\,s for
recursive doubling at $p=8$, and 194.9\,s against 194.1\,s at $p=32$. The latency-optimal
algorithm is ahead by four parts in a thousand. \texttt{best\_algorithm} with
\texttt{objective="time"} returns \texttt{recursive\_doubling} at every $p$ on that margin, and
with \texttt{objective="price"} returns \texttt{reduce\_bcast} at every $p$. Given that the time
difference is inside any plausible noise band and the price difference is 70\%,
\texttt{reduce\_bcast} is the answer, and the code's default is right.

One caveat on the price figure, because it understates rather than overstates the case.
\texttt{cost.predict} charges the operator-output term as $(p-1) \cdot \texttt{op\_cost\_tokens}$
for \emph{every} algorithm. That is exact for the reduce family, where every algorithm performs
$p-1$ applications, and it is exact for \texttt{reduce\_bcast}. It is not exact for recursive
doubling, which performs 160 applications at $p=32$; the model charges 31. So the 70\% price gap
the model reports is a floor, and a corrected model would put the gap nearer a factor of three.
The discrepancy is internal to \texttt{cost.py} --- the \texttt{Prediction} object already carries
\texttt{messages: 160} beside the price computed from 31 --- so it needs no measurement to
confirm, and it is worth checking against the reduce family, where the assumption holds.

\subsection{The property that is not on the cost axes}

The reason to prefer this algorithm is not primarily arithmetic. \texttt{reduce\_bcast} produces
\emph{one} result. The operator is applied $p-1$ times along a fixed tree with the lower virtual
rank always the accumulator, so the fold order is canonical and there is exactly one value;
\texttt{bcast} then delivers that value by handle. The trace proves the byte-identity rather than
asserting it: at $p=32$ all 31 broadcast sends carry the digest \texttt{e29c9c180c62}, and at
$p=10$ all 9 carry \texttt{4a44dc153642}. One content-addressed object, forwarded, unmodified.

Recursive doubling has each rank compute its own copy. For an exact associative operator the
copies are equal and the distinction is void --- and this archive confirms as much, since the
result digest recursive doubling distributes matches \texttt{reduce\_bcast}'s at all eight
non-power-of-two sizes where both ran. For a lossy operator they need not be equal. AgentMPI
takes the trap seriously enough to canonicalise the operand order inside recursive doubling
(\texttt{if comm.rank < partner or op.commutative}), which removes fold \emph{order} as a source
of divergence; what it cannot remove is that the operator is executed independently 160 times
instead of 31, and a language model given identical operands twice need not answer twice the
same. That is the residual risk, and it is a nondeterminism argument rather than an
associativity one.

\textbf{It is also unmeasured.} Across all 496 \texttt{coll.*} records in the archive that carry
the field, \texttt{divergence\_risk} is \texttt{false} every time. The flag is
\texttt{op.lossy}, and the operators run under \texttt{reduce\_bcast} in the sweep are
\texttt{SUM} (206 records), \texttt{UNION} (68) and \texttt{GLOSSARY\_MERGE} (8) --- the first
two declared \texttt{Associativity.EXACT}, and the third, the only semantic one, appearing
\emph{only} under \texttt{reduce\_bcast}. So the design's semantic-operator ×
recursive-doubling cell is empty, and no trace in this repository shows a population disagreeing.
The argument for \texttt{reduce\_bcast}'s single canonical result is a guarantee by construction;
the argument against recursive doubling's $p$ results is a prediction that has not been tested.

\section{Threats to this reading}

\textbf{These runs contain no agents, so the axis on which this algorithm is chosen is the one
the sweep does not measure.} Every member records \texttt{executors: \{none: $p$\}}, zero agent
calls, zero tokens in and out, \$0.0000; \texttt{total\_busy\_s} is 0 and
\texttt{idle\_fraction} is 1.0 at every size, because \texttt{bench\_collectives} calls
\texttt{comm.allreduce(1, SUM)} and returns. All of \S3.2 and \S3.3 rests on
\texttt{cost.py}'s parameters and on reading the implementations, not on these traces. What the
sweep establishes is structure --- $2(p-1)$ messages in two trees at every size, and a nested
attribution that adds up --- and that is worth having precisely because it is the part a
simulation cannot supply.

\textbf{The measured wall times must not be read as latency, and for this family they invert the
conclusion.} \texttt{reduce\_bcast} is \emph{faster} than recursive doubling in the logs at the
larger sizes: 0.186\,s against 0.228\,s at $p=32$, 0.098\,s against 0.128\,s at $p=16$
(\texttt{mb-free}). With $\alpha$ effectively zero, cost is dominated by per-message fabric work
and by the receive-side poll backoff (\texttt{POLL\_INTERVAL} 10\,ms growing by $1.4\times$ to
\texttt{MAX\_POLL\_INTERVAL} 250\,ms in \texttt{comm.py}), so the algorithm with fewer messages
wins and the round count barely registers. That is the opposite regime from the one the algorithm
choice is about. Neither the measured ordering nor the modelled ordering should be quoted without
saying which regime it belongs to.

\textbf{The 20-token and 1-token message sizes are framing, not content.} The benchmark's payload
is the integer 1, so the reduce message is almost entirely its \texttt{\{"acc", "depth",
"weight", "vr"\}} envelope and the broadcast message is a single token. The observation that
broadcast messages are cheap is therefore \emph{understated} here in one sense and misleading in
another: with a real 1200-token artefact the broadcast messages would carry 1200 tokens of
volume, and what stays free is not the volume but the operator invocation. I have been careful
above to make the latency claim about applications rather than about tokens, and the closed
form's volume term, $2(p-1)n$, is effectively untested by a sweep in which $n=1$.

\textbf{The nested attribution is a hard-coded table, and it is right only because someone kept
it in step with the code.} \texttt{COMPOSED\_ALGORITHMS} lists three entries; if a fourth
composed algorithm were added to \texttt{algorithms.py} without a matching entry, its family
would report a phantom disagreement at every size --- which is exactly what happened to this
family under the earlier version of the tool. So the \modelok{} at 21 of 21 rows here is
conditional on a piece of duplicated knowledge, and a reader should treat it as a check on the
runtime given a correct tool, not as an independent verification of both.

\textbf{The fold-depth argument is about a lossy operator and every measured run used an exact
one.} The claim that only the maximum \texttt{fold\_depth} is meaningful is a reading of what the
field is computed from, and it is correct as such. But the reason it matters --- that
$F(d) = (1-\delta)^d$ is the fidelity of the value a rank holds --- depends on a $\delta$ that
\texttt{CostParams} sets to 0.05 by default and that no run in this family measures.
\texttt{benchmarks/bench\_reduce.py} and \texttt{bench\_fidelity} are where that number would
come from; nothing here contributes to it.

\begin{abstract}
The flat reduction is the ``manager summarises all the worker reports'' pattern: every rank
sends its contribution to the root, which folds them locally. Across 21 measured sizes from
$p=2$ to $p=32$ it logged exactly $p-1$ messages, reported that count exactly, recorded one
round at every size, and recorded a fold depth of exactly $p-1$ at every size --- all four
agreeing with the closed form at 21 of 21, with no remainder path and no red crosses. Those
last two numbers are the family's whole content and they are in tension: flat reduce has the
shallowest \emph{network} of any reduction (one round; the root talks to everyone) and the
deepest \emph{fold} (all $p-1$ operator applications execute back-to-back on one rank). That
combination makes it the only algorithm in \texttt{cost.FORMULAS} whose reduce family has
$\mathit{depth} > \mathit{rounds}$, and it is where I found a real defect: \texttt{cost.predict}
computes the operator term as $\min(\mathit{depth}, \mathit{rounds})$ applications, so for flat
reduce it charges \emph{one} application regardless of $p$. The predicted time is $34.35$\,s at
$p=2$ and $34.35$\,s at $p=64$, and \texttt{best\_algorithm("reduce", p, n, objective="time")}
consequently returns \texttt{flat} at every process count. Archived agent-executed runs refute
it directly: flat reduce at $p=8$ took $197.6$\,s and $251.6$\,s of root blocking against the
model's $34.35$\,s, while removing the clamp predicts $239.7$\,s. The single most important
thing to take away is that the sweep measured the very quantity the time model discards ---
\texttt{fold\_depth} $= p-1$, at all 21 sizes --- so the fix requires no new instrumentation.
\end{abstract}

\section{How the algorithm scales}

The implementation splits sharply by role. A non-root rank makes one \texttt{\_csend} of its
payload to the root and is finished. The root loops $p-1$ times over
\texttt{comm.recv(source=-1)}, accepting arrivals in whatever order they land; if the operator
is not commutative it then sorts the contributions by source rank; and finally it left-folds
them, calling \texttt{op.fn} once per contribution with an explicit
\texttt{ReduceContext(depth=i, weight=i+1)}. So the traffic is $p-1$ messages of $n$ tokens
each and the operator is applied $p-1$ times, all of them on the root. The recorded statistics
follow: \texttt{rounds = 1} at every rank, and \texttt{fold\_depth = p - 1} at the root and $0$
at every leaf. This is \texttt{FORMULAS[("reduce","flat")]} exactly, which records
$(1,\;p-1,\;(p-1)n,\;p-1)$.

The trace shows both halves cleanly. In \texttt{mb-free\_\_coll-reduce-flat-8} the seven leaves
send between $5.06$ and $12.55$\,ms, in the order $7, 4, 2, 3, 5, 1, 6$ --- no leaf waits for
another, which is what a single round means. The root's seven \texttt{msg.recv} events are then
stamped at $15.21$, $15.39$, $15.50$, $15.57$, $15.65$, $15.71$ and $15.78$\,ms: it drains all
seven arrivals in $0.57$\,ms and records \texttt{fold\_depth: 7} with $12.1$\,ms of blocking.
With \texttt{SUM} as the operator each fold is an integer addition, so the $0.57$\,ms is the
draining and not the folding. That is the crucial caveat about this family's timings: the
sweep measures the network shape of flat reduce faithfully and measures its operator cost not
at all, because the operator is free.

Consequently Figure~\ref{fig:family}'s two panels look nothing alike and both are correct. The
left panel is the straight line $p-1$ with every marker on it, the same line all three of
\texttt{flat}, \texttt{binomial} and \texttt{chain} produce. The right panel is a horizontal
line at $1$: the round count never steps, because there is nothing to step --- the depth of a
star is one for every $p$. What the figure cannot show, because the generator's round axis is
the model's round axis, is the quantity that does grow. Fold depth measured $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6,
7, 9, 11, 15, 19, 23, 31$ across the thirteen sizes, matching $p-1$ at all 21 runs. A reader
looking only at the figure would conclude that flat reduce is the cheapest reduction available;
that conclusion is the defect described below.

The viewer screenshot for \texttt{mb-free\_\_coll-reduce-flat-32} shows the fan-in serialising
at the root. Rank~0's lane is the only one carrying a long run of bright message glyphs, spread
across the whole $0.07$\,s span from roughly $0.02$ to $0.07$\,s, while the other thirty-one
lanes carry one or two ticks each, clustered early. The contrast with
\texttt{mb-free\_\_coll-bcast-flat-32}, where rank~0's glyphs are a \emph{tight} early burst
and the other lanes are spread, is exactly the difference between producing $p-1$ messages as
fast as the fabric will take them and consuming $p-1$ messages as fast as the senders happen to
produce them. The same star topology, read in the two directions, has its serialisation in
different places.

\section{The cost model charges flat reduce one operator application, not \texorpdfstring{$p-1$}{p-1}}

This is a defect in \texttt{src/agentmpi/cost.py} rather than in the sweep, and the sweep is
what exposes it. \texttt{cost.predict} computes the critical-path time as

\begin{center}
\texttt{time\_s = rounds * (fabric\_s + n*beta\_in)}, then
\texttt{time\_s += min(op\_rounds, rounds) * message\_time(op\_cost)}
\end{center}

where \texttt{op\_rounds} is the tuple's fold depth. The comment above it explains the intent:
``a reduction additionally pays one operator application per level of the tree''. For
\texttt{chain} the levels and the applications coincide at $p-1$; for \texttt{binomial} they
coincide at $\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$; for \texttt{kary} at $\lceil\log_k p\rceil$. For
\texttt{flat} they do not coincide at all --- one level, $p-1$ applications --- and the
$\min$ takes the smaller. Enumerating \texttt{FORMULAS}, \texttt{reduce/flat} and
\texttt{reduce\_scatter/linear} are the only two entries in the whole table with
$\mathit{depth} > \mathit{rounds}$, so this is the only place in the reduce family where the
clamp bites, and it bites completely.

The result is a predicted time that does not depend on $p$. With default \texttt{CostParams}
($\alpha = 12$\,s, $\beta^{-1} = 45$ tokens/s), $n = 1000$ tokens and an operator output of
$1000$ tokens, \texttt{predict("reduce","flat",p,\dots)} returns $34.35$\,s at $p = 2, 4, 8,
16, 32$ and $64$ alike, while \texttt{chain} rises from $34.35$ to $2{,}164$\,s and
\texttt{binomial} from $34.35$ to $206$\,s over the same range. So
\texttt{best\_algorithm("reduce", p, n, objective="time")} returns \texttt{flat} at every
process count I tested ($4, 8, 16, 32, 64$) --- recommending, as the latency-optimal reduction,
the algorithm whose own docstring says ``all $p-1$ applications execute on the root, so the
root's cost is $(p-1)$ agent calls'' and whose modelled fidelity at $p=64$ is $0.0395$.

Three further pieces of evidence say this is an oversight and not a convention. First,
\texttt{predict} is internally inconsistent about it: the same function charges the
\emph{price} of $(p-1)$ operator applications, via
\texttt{message\_price(volume, (p-1)*op\_cost\_tokens)}, while charging the \emph{time} of one.
Second, the sibling function \texttt{predict\_kary} performs the same calculation without a
clamp, using \texttt{depth * message\_time(...)} directly. Third, the paper's own
\texttt{tab:reduce-alg} lists flat's applications as ``$p-1$ (all at root)'', and its evaluation
section asserts that ``Flat reduction is again slowest-but-one despite needing one round, for
the reason the cost model gives: its applications serialise at the root'' --- a reason the code
as written does not in fact give.

Archived measurement settles it. Four runs in this repository execute the reduce family with a
real agent-implemented operator at $p=8$; two of them use \texttt{flat}. In
\texttt{sat-mb-fidelity\_\_fidelity-flat-f-k4} the root's \texttt{coll.reduce} records
$197.616$\,s of blocking over seven \texttt{agent.call} events averaging $28.2$\,s each; in
\texttt{inc-mb-fidelity-inc\_\_fidelity-flat-f-k4-inc} it records $251.627$\,s over seven calls
averaging $35.9$\,s. The clamped model predicts $34.35$\,s --- low by factors of $5.8$ and
$7.3$. Evaluating the same formula with the clamp removed, so that all $\mathit{depth} = p-1$
applications are charged, gives $239.68$\,s, which sits between the two measurements. The
number the corrected formula needs is already in the trace and already in the
\texttt{Prediction} object: \texttt{fold\_depth}, measured as $p-1$ at 21 of 21 sizes in this
sweep.

Nothing in the test suite pins this down. \texttt{test\_message\_counts\_match\_cost\_formulas}
checks message counts and not times; \texttt{test\_fold\_depth\_is\_logarithmic\_for\_trees}
parametrises over \texttt{chain} and \texttt{binomial} only, so the one algorithm whose depth
exceeds its round count is the one algorithm whose depth the suite never asserts. That is a
plausible account of how the clamp survived.

\section{Powers of two and everything else}

There is no remainder path. The root's loop is \texttt{for \_ in range(p - 1)} over
\texttt{recv(source=-1)}, which is indifferent to the structure of $p$, and a leaf's behaviour
is a single send. The code executed at $p=7$ is the code executed at $p=8$ with one fewer
iteration.

The measurements are consistent. All eight non-power-of-two sizes logged $p-1$ messages ($2$ at
$p=3$, $4$ at $p=5$, $5$ at $p=6$, $6$ at $p=7$, $9$ at $p=10$, $11$ at $p=12$, $19$ at $p=20$,
$23$ at $p=24$), self-reported the same figure, recorded one round, and recorded fold depth
$p-1$. Both marker classes in Figure~\ref{fig:family} lie on the closed form in both panels and
there are no red crosses.

Token accounting is exact at all 21 sizes, which distinguishes this algorithm from its two
siblings. A leaf sends \texttt{payload} bare, so the \texttt{msg.send} events sum to $p-1$
tokens and the collective reports $p-1$. \texttt{reduce/binomial} and \texttt{reduce/chain}
wrap the accumulator in a metadata envelope and charge only the accumulator, logging $20(p-1)$
and $16(p-1)$ tokens respectively while reporting $p-1$; flat has no envelope and therefore no
discrepancy.

One code path deserves explicit mention because it is flat reduce's unique capability and the
sweep does not exercise it. When \texttt{op.commutative} is false, the root sorts the
contributions by source rank before folding, so flat reduce evaluates in exact rank order for
\emph{any} root. \texttt{binomial} cannot: its virtual-rank ordering coincides with
communicator rank order only when the root is $0$, and the implementation raises
\texttt{AmpiUsageError} rather than reorder silently. So flat is one of only two legal choices
(with \texttt{chain}) for a non-commutative operator at a non-zero root. All 21 runs in this
sweep used \texttt{SUM}, which is commutative, with \texttt{root = 0}, so the sort is never
taken and this claim rests on reading the code and on
\texttt{test\_reduce\_preserves\_order\_for\_noncommutative\_op}, which checks \texttt{CONCAT}
against the reference fold for \texttt{flat} across nine sizes.

Eight sizes were measured twice. Every count agrees at all eight; the run wall times differ by
factors from $1.01$ to $1.47$.

\section{When to choose this algorithm}

Flat reduce is the right choice under one condition and the wrong choice by default, and the
two numbers this sweep measured are exactly the two sides of that.

The condition is that only the root has the judgement to combine, and that $p-1$ contributions
fit in its context. Under those constraints there is no alternative: any tree delegates a fold
to an interior rank, which is precisely what a harness with one authoritative integrator does
not want. Flat also has the network property that no tree has --- a single round, so the last
contribution to arrive determines when the fold can start and no contribution waits behind
another in flight. Measured, that shows as $p-1$ leaf sends finishing before the root begins
draining, and $12.1$\,ms of root blocking at $p=8$ against \texttt{chain}'s $92.1$\,ms.

The reason it is nevertheless the wrong default is that its $p-1$ applications are serial on
one rank, and for a semantic operator an application is an agent invocation. At $p=8$ with a
real operator, archived runs give flat $197.6$ and $251.6$\,s of root blocking against
\texttt{binomial}'s $77.6$ and $80.3$\,s and \texttt{kary}($k{=}4$)'s $53.1$ and $51.8$\,s ---
so flat is $2.5$ to $3.2$ times slower than the binary tree and roughly four times slower than
the $4$-ary one, despite needing one round instead of three or two. It beats only
\texttt{chain}. That ordering is the point of the whole reduce comparison: all three of
\texttt{flat}, \texttt{binomial} and \texttt{chain} perform $p-1$ applications and send $p-1$
messages, so they differ only in how those applications are arranged, and arranging them all on
one rank is the worst arrangement for latency even though it is the best for network depth.

On fidelity the intuition and this repository's own measurement part company, and it would be
wrong to claim otherwise. Structurally, flat's fold depth of $p-1$ is the worst available: the
cost model's $F(d) = (1-\delta)^d$ puts flat at $0.204$ against binomial's $0.774$ at $p=32$
with $\delta = 0.05$, and the mechanism is real --- the root's accumulator is re-encoded $p-1$
times, and the archived reduce traces show that each such re-encoding produces a genuinely new
artifact. But the repository's fidelity experiment measured retention at $0.3854$ under
\emph{every} algorithm at $p=8$ (\texttt{inc-mb-fidelity-inc}, $37$ of $96$ items surviving at
fold depth $7$, $3$ and $2$ alike) and $1.0000$ under every algorithm when the budget was not
binding (\texttt{sat-mb-fidelity}). Under a uniform enforced output budget
the last application is the binding one, so depth buys nothing in quality either way. The
defensible case against flat reduce is therefore cost, not fidelity: it is the second-slowest
and the second-most-expensive in output tokens of the four measured algorithms, and it earns
that by serialising work a tree would have run concurrently.

\section{Threats to this reading}

These runs contain no agents, and for this family that is the threat rather than a caveat.
Every one of the 21 runs reports \texttt{executors = \{"none": p\}}, zero \texttt{agent.call}
events, zero busy time, an idle fraction of $1.0$ and \$0.000 of cost. The operator was
\texttt{SUM} with a scalar payload, so each of the $p-1$ applications the root performs is an
integer addition costing nothing measurable --- the whole quantity that makes flat reduce
expensive is set to zero by construction. The sweep therefore validates the \emph{counts}
($p-1$ messages, $1$ round, fold depth $p-1$, at 21 of 21) and can say nothing about the
duration. The agent-executed figures I quote for the flat-versus-tree comparison come from
\texttt{sat-mb-fidelity}, \texttt{inc-mb-fidelity-inc} and \texttt{fid-synth}, which are $p=8$
and $p=16$ single points rather than a sweep, and two of the four use a surrogate function
operator rather than a model.

The wall times at large $p$ do not measure the collective either. In
\texttt{mb-free\_\_coll-reduce-flat-32} the thirty-two \texttt{rank.init} events span $2.1$ to
$63.3$\,ms while the run lasts $76.3$\,ms, so most of the run is ranks starting up and the
$0.076$\,s in the generated table is a launch measurement. The maximum per-rank blocking inside
the collective ($49.2$\,ms at $p=32$) is the only duration I would defend, and it is a handful
of SQLite transactions.

The defect argument depends on how one reads the model's intent, and I should be explicit about
the weakest link. If \texttt{rounds} were meant to be an upper bound on concurrent-plus-serial
critical path, and \texttt{fold\_depth} were meant purely as a fidelity statistic with no time
consequence, then the clamp would be intentional and the flat-reduce time would be a deliberate
statement that a star costs one round. I do not believe that reading survives the three
internal inconsistencies --- the price term charging $p-1$, \texttt{predict\_kary} not clamping,
and the paper's table listing $p-1$ applications --- but it is the reading that would make me
wrong, and no comment in \texttt{cost.py} settles it either way. What is not in doubt is the
consequence: \texttt{best\_algorithm} under the time objective currently recommends
\texttt{flat} at every $p$, and the two archived agent-executed flat runs are $5.8$ and $7.3$
times slower than it predicts.

Finally, some configurations are unexercised. All 21 runs used \texttt{root = 0} and a
commutative operator, so the source-sorting branch that gives flat reduce its unique
order-preservation guarantee is never taken. All 21 used a one-token payload, so the volume term
$(p-1)n$ is validated only at $n=1$, and the context pressure that is flat reduce's real
constraint --- $p-1$ contributions in one rank's window --- is not exercised at all: zero of
$1{,}110$ receptions across the six sweeps admitted a single token.

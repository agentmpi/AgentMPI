\begin{abstract}
This is the parallel book-translation experiment at $p=8$ with the neighbour exchange removed:
eight real agent ranks still agree a global glossary by \texttt{allreduce}, but never see how their
neighbours rendered the abutting text, so the seams between sections go unreconciled. Against the
\texttt{real-tr-p8-full} baseline the ablation removes 84 events, 8 of the 24 agent calls, 14 of the
56 messages, 33.3\% of prompt tokens, 41.5\% of completion tokens, 37.4\% of the dollar cost and
24.5\% of wall time. The decomposition of that saving is the substance: the halo exchange's own
traffic is 14 messages carrying 361 tokens, 1.14\% of the baseline's tokens sent, and its own
blocking is 34.72 rank-seconds, 2.6\% of the pool; essentially the entire cost is the second round of
agent calls it enables, 381.63 rank-seconds and \$0.0971. The baseline's much-remarked 20.70\,s halo
skew turns out to measure nothing about the exchange: each rank's departure time is exactly the
maximum entry time over its closed neighbourhood plus 0.10--0.25\,s of fabric latency, verified for
all eight ranks, so the exchange is a barrier over the ring graph and the skew is the spread of the
preceding translate phase, which the exchange in fact narrows from 25.58\,s to 20.70\,s. The single
thing a reader should take away is negative and it concerns the record rather than the protocol:
the boundary-drift cost this ablation exists to measure is not recoverable from what was recorded,
because the deterministic scorer has no seam metric \emph{and} because four of the eight sections in
this run are byte-identical to artefacts of the baseline --- three of them to the baseline's
\emph{post}-halo text. The ablation's resource arm is sound; its quality arm is contaminated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Reading the timeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} shows two blocks of work and nothing between them. Every rank has exactly
two bars: a term-extraction call beginning within 40\,ms of the job start, and a translation call
beginning at $t\approx76$\,s. Seven of the eight first bars end between 21.0 and 25.0\,s. The eighth,
rank 3's, runs to 75.56\,s. From roughly 25\,s to 76\,s the other seven lanes are empty, and
Figure~\ref{fig:concurrency} states the same fact numerically: concurrency falls from 8 to 1 shortly
after 21\,s and sits at 1 for about 50\,s before returning to 8 in a single step. That step is what
drives the run's unflattering parallelism figures --- 55.7\% efficiency, 43.9\% of active time with
exactly one rank busy, 44.3\% of rank-seconds unused --- and none of it is attributable to the
ablation.

The gap is the glossary \texttt{allreduce}, and rank 3 is holding it. Rank 3's
\texttt{terms:u3} call reports a latency of 75.53\,s against 21.01--25.01\,s for the other seven, a
mean of 22.86\,s; that single call is why rank 3 is busy for 112.82\,s at 89.6\% occupancy while every
other rank is busy for 60.2--67.9\,s, and it is the whole of the $1.61\times$ load imbalance. Because
the reduction runs as \texttt{reduce\_bcast} over a binomial tree, no rank can leave until the root
has folded every leaf and rebroadcast. The log gives the chain end to end: rank 1 arrives at the
reduce at 21.03\,s, ranks 5, 7, 6, 4 between 21.53 and 25.03\,s, then nothing until rank 3 sends its
contribution at 75.56\,s, rank 2 folds and forwards at 75.80\,s, root 0 emits the completed reduce at
76.04\,s, and the broadcast reaches the last rank at 76.13\,s. The waits recorded inside that pair are
correspondingly large --- 53.02\,s for the root inside the reduce, 51.02--55.02\,s for the six
non-root leaves inside the broadcast --- and they are the same 55\,s of wall clock seen from
different sides, a point I return to below because the coordination-share metric does not know it.

The broadcast is a release, and the timeline shows it as one: all eight translation calls start
within an 80\,ms window, 76.16--76.24\,s. They finish between 116.69 and 125.76\,s, a 9.0\,s spread on
latencies of 40.52--49.52\,s. The last translation to land, rank 7's at 125.76\,s, is followed within
190\,ms by the pre-gather barrier, the binomial gather and job finish at 125.95\,s. The barrier is
therefore almost free in wall-clock terms even though it absorbed real waiting: the first arrival,
rank 2, waited 9.19\,s, and the release skew across the eight \texttt{coll.barrier} events is 0.16\,s.
The gather moves 4,234 tokens in 7 messages in 0.19\,s.

What is worth looking at in this timeline is mostly what is absent from it. There is no third block.
In the baseline the same picture carries a neighbour exchange at $t\approx81$--102\,s and then a
second full round of agent bars from 102 to 166.5\,s; here the run ends where the baseline's
translate phase ended. There is no \texttt{topo.cart\_create} and no \texttt{coll.halo\_exchange}
event, and consequently no nearest-neighbour message ticks. Figure~\ref{fig:comm} is a clean
binomial tree and nothing else: 14 ordered rank pairs, the seven downward edges
$0\!\to\!1,0\!\to\!2,0\!\to\!4,2\!\to\!3,4\!\to\!5,4\!\to\!6,6\!\to\!7$ carrying the scatter and the
two broadcasts, and their seven reverses carrying the two reductions and the gather. The baseline's
matrix has 20 pairs; the six extra ones are exactly $1\!\leftrightarrow\!2$, $3\!\leftrightarrow\!4$
and $5\!\leftrightarrow\!6$, the ring adjacencies the binomial tree does not already contain.
Figure~\ref{fig:ranks} shows the imbalance as one long bar among seven short ones and zero context
occupancy everywhere, since the harness passes \texttt{admit=False} on every rendezvous and 8,083
tokens were deferred rather than admitted. Figure~\ref{fig:collectives} has nine points and no red
diamond.

The dashboard screenshot agrees and adds one thing the figures do not. Its collectives panel lists
six operations --- \texttt{reduce}, \texttt{bcast}, \texttt{allreduce}, \texttt{scatter},
\texttt{barrier}, \texttt{gather} --- with no \texttt{halo\_exchange} row at all, where the baseline's
panel has a seventh row reporting zero messages. The two lanes labelled rank 8 and rank 14 are empty
apart from a single grey lifecycle tick each. And the panel shows \texttt{allreduce} with 16
invocations and 0 messages, which is not a defect but is the same reading hazard as the halo's zero:
a composed collective's traffic is attributed to its constituents, so the row that names the
operation the harness actually called is the row that appears to have sent nothing.

\section{Interpretation}

Four things in this run are true by construction, and it is worth listing them so that the rest can
be read as evidence. The configuration recorded in the fabric is
\texttt{"halo": false, "glossary": true}, executor \texttt{broker}, 8 ranks, 8 units of 600 words, so
the absence of the exchange is the experiment and not a failure. The event deficit against the
baseline is exactly accounted for by that absence: $347-263=84$ events, decomposing as 8
\texttt{topo.cart\_create}, 8 \texttt{coll.halo\_exchange}, 28 halo messages (14 sends and 14
receives), 8 \texttt{agent.call} for the revision round with their 24 accompanying broker events, and
8 \texttt{rank.init} reattachments for the workers those calls would have needed. The message count
falls from 56 to 42, and the 14 missing messages are precisely the exchange's own, $2(p-1)$ for an
open path of eight. And \texttt{n\_boundary\_changes} is 0 for every rank in the result JSON, against
3--5 per rank and 30 in total for the baseline, because the code path that would increment it does not
run.

The first thing that emerged is what the baseline's halo skew actually measures, and the answer is
sharp enough to state as a rule. For each rank, take the moment it entered the exchange --- its
translate call's completion --- and the moment its \texttt{coll.halo\_exchange} event was emitted,
which the log shows is also the moment its revision call began. In the baseline, the departure time
of every one of the eight ranks equals the maximum entry time over its closed neighbourhood (itself,
left, right) plus a residual of 0.10--0.25\,s, mean 0.19\,s. Rank 3, which entered at 95.05\,s, left at
102.01\,s because rank 2 did not enter until 101.88\,s. Rank 6, which entered first at 76.30\,s, left
at 99.02\,s because rank 5 entered at 98.79\,s. Rank 7 is the only rank that escaped: it sits at the
open end of the non-periodic ring with a single neighbour, and that neighbour, rank 6, happened to be
the earliest finisher in the run, so rank 7 entered and left at the same timestamp, 81.38\,s. The
identical relation holds for all eight ranks of \texttt{real-tr-p8-noglossary}, which keeps the
exchange, with residuals of 0.15--0.25\,s.

That makes the exchange a barrier over the ring graph rather than over the world, and at $p=8$ on a
path the distinction buys almost nothing. Seven of the eight ranks in the baseline left within 3.06\,s
of each other; the 20.70\,s reported as skew is one endpoint rank leaving early, once. The
corresponding figure in \texttt{real-tr-p8-noglossary} is 7.43\,s, and there the early leaver is an
interior rank whose two neighbours both finished first. So the skew is not a cost of the collective:
it is a measurement of the spread of whatever phase precedes it, filtered through a neighbourhood
maximum. Indeed the filter reduces the spread rather than adding to it --- the baseline's translate
completions span 25.58\,s and its halo departures span 20.70\,s --- because clamping every rank up to
its neighbourhood maximum compresses the field from below. Removing the exchange, then, did not
remove a synchronisation point that was costing this run anything measurable in skew. It removed a
phase.

The second thing that emerged is where the saving actually sits, and it is not where the collective
is. On the communication side the exchange is nearly free: 14 messages against the run's 56, and
361 tokens against 31,603 sent, so 1.14\% of the fabric's traffic. Its blocking cost, computed as the
sum over ranks of the interval between entering and leaving, is 34.72 rank-seconds out of the
$8\times166.78=1{,}334.3$ rank-seconds the baseline pool had available, or 2.6\%; the figure for
\texttt{real-tr-p8-noglossary} is 22.97 rank-seconds, 3.0\%. What the exchange costs is the round of
work it makes possible. The eight \texttt{revise} calls consumed 381.63 rank-seconds of agent latency,
28.6\% of the baseline pool, with prompts totalling 14,450 tokens and completions 3,661; the observed
differences between the runs are $-14{,}448$ prompt tokens and $-3{,}582$ completion tokens, matching
to within the noise of the term sheets. In dollars the ablation costs \$0.1628 against \$0.2599, a
saving of \$0.0971 or 37.4\%. This is exactly the shape the project's argument about halo exchange
predicts --- $\Theta(p)$ coordination, with the expense in local work --- and it is worth noting that
the arithmetic alternative is $p(p-1)=56$ point-to-point messages for an all-gather of boundaries
against the exchange's 14, a ratio of $p/2$ that grows with the world size.

Wall time needs more care than the headline difference suggests. The runs differ by 40.84\,s, 24.5\%,
but that number is contaminated in both directions. This run lost about 50.5\,s to rank 3's
extraction straggler, which has nothing to do with the halo, and gained about 21.8\,s on a faster
translate phase (49.52\,s against 71.53\,s for the slowest call). The clean measurement is internal to
the baseline: its last translation landed at 101.88\,s and the job finished at 166.78\,s, so
everything downstream of translation --- the neighbourhood wait, the revision round, the barrier and
the gather --- occupied 64.90\,s, 38.9\% of that run's wall clock, of which the exchange's own wait was
0.20\,s. Remove the exchange and you remove essentially all of that tail, which is what this run
shows: 190\,ms elapse between its last translation and job finish.

The third thing is the instrumentation gap, and this run's contribution to it is a warning about
reading metrics as verdicts. The baseline's eight \texttt{coll.halo\_exchange} payloads contain three
fields --- \texttt{label}, \texttt{left}, \texttt{right} --- and nothing else: no
\texttt{messages\_sent}, no \texttt{tokens\_sent}, no \texttt{rounds}, no \texttt{algorithm}, no
\texttt{size}, and no \texttt{wall\_s}, which is why the collective table records the exchange with
algorithm ``\texttt{?}'', zero messages and a wall time of 0.0 while the fabric independently logged
14 messages and 361 tokens against its internal tag. The collective whose entire justification is a
lower message count was the one collective not reporting its message count. It is fully recoverable
from the log --- the tags \texttt{\_ampi:halo:0:8:R} and \texttt{\_ampi:halo:0:8:L} carry 14
\texttt{msg.send}/\texttt{msg.recv} pairs, matching $2(p-1)$ exactly --- which is how the
per-invocation \texttt{logged\_messages} field caught the disagreement in the first place. In this
run, \texttt{n\_misreported\_collectives} is 0 and the generated findings report that all nine
checkable collectives agree with their cost model. Both statements are true and neither means
anything: the only collective that disagreed was deleted, so the metric improved by removing the
subject rather than by fixing it. The defect itself is real but no longer live --- the traces were
recorded at commit \texttt{b0b652d} and the omission was repaired in \texttt{ee21d79}, whose message
notes the reason it survived, namely that the existing test asserted on a runtime counter rather
than on what the collective wrote to the log. Under the contract's instruction not to regenerate
traces, this run's log cannot demonstrate the fix; it can only show the hole where the collective was.

The fourth thing that emerged is a check on the glossary machinery that the ablation was not designed
to provide. The resolved glossary in this run has the digest \texttt{0233d780cdb6} and 206 terms at
every one of the eight ranks, and the baseline's has the same digest and the same count --- even
though the two runs' local term sheets differ (24--38 terms per rank in the baseline against 27--39
here) and their conflict counts differ (4 against 2). Different contributions, different conflicts,
identical agreed result at every rank: that is the claim the harness makes for \texttt{UNION} with
deterministic conflict resolution, and it holds here across two independent extraction phases.

Which brings the analysis to the question the ablation was actually for, and to a negative answer.
The result JSON for this configuration exists at
\texttt{results/translation/real-tr-p8-gloss-nohalo-union-reduce\_bcast-w600-u8.json}, and its quality
block is indistinguishable from the baseline's: coverage 1.0, consistency 1.0, six shared entities,
zero divergent entities, 22 renderings all verified, paragraph fidelity 1.0, length plausibility 1.0,
target-script ratio 0.8578 against the baseline's 0.8579, residual source per kilo-character 0.296
against 0.297. This is not evidence that the seams survived. It is evidence that the scorer cannot
see seams: reading \texttt{score\_translation}, every metric it computes is either per-unit
(paragraph count, length ratio, script ratio, residual source) or about entity agreement across
units, and none of them examines the junction between one unit's last paragraph and the next unit's
first. The one quantity that would have moved, \texttt{n\_divergent\_entities}, is zero even in
\texttt{real-tr-p8-noglossary}, so it is not discriminating at this scale either.

What the record does allow is a measurement of what the exchange produced in the baseline, recovered
from that run's broker spool, where each revision prompt embeds the pre-revision text verbatim
between its \texttt{YOUR CURRENT TRANSLATION} markers. Comparing that against the revision's output
for all eight sections: the revision changed every section, and changed 70 of 9,527 characters, 0.73\%
overall and between 0.25\% and 2.24\% per section. The agents' own \texttt{changes} lists, 30 entries
across the eight, describe exactly the failures the exchange was introduced to fix --- a first
paragraph rewritten so that it no longer reads as the opening of a document, a closing sentence
extended so that it hands over to the next section, a second-person pronoun unified with the
neighbouring section's usage, and one terminology fix that disambiguates two glossary entries for
carriage drivers that the glossary itself could not resolve without seeing the neighbour's text. So
the exchange's product is real, visible in the artefacts, and worth 0.73\% of the characters for
38.9\% of the wall clock. Whether losing it degrades the book is a question this trace cannot answer,
and the honest statement is that the ablation's quality arm did not measure anything --- not that
the seams were fine.

Read against its siblings, this run is the resource-side half of a pair.
\texttt{real-tr-p8-noglossary} removes the \texttt{allreduce} and keeps the exchange, so it drops the
coordination and keeps the second round of local work; this run does the reverse. The two are not
symmetric in cost: at 204 events and \$0.1355, removing the glossary is cheaper than removing the
halo (263 events, \$0.1628) against the baseline's 347 and \$0.2599, because the glossary's cost is
mostly the extraction round and the broadcast of a 206-term artefact, while the halo's is a full
re-translation. Taken together they say that at $p=8$ the expensive part of the HPC decomposition is
never the collective.

The remaining flagged findings resolve as follows. Ranks 8 and 14 appearing without participating is
expected and not specific to this configuration: all three siblings show the same two stray
identifiers with a single lifecycle event each, which is a shared worker pool registering against the
wrong rank slot, and no message or collective in the log involves them. The 23 reattachments up to
incarnation 4 are also expected, being two agent calls per rank each served by a fresh worker
process. The $1.61\times$ imbalance is a genuine property of this run but not of this ablation; it is
rank 3's extraction call and nothing else.

The 137.4\% coordination share is a metric defect and should not be read as a statement about the
run. A collective's \texttt{wall\_s} in this analysis is the maximum per-rank blocking time inside
it, not a duration on the wall clock, and \texttt{collective\_wall\_s} sums those maxima over all
invocations. That double-counts twice over: the composed \texttt{allreduce} is summed alongside the
\texttt{reduce} and \texttt{bcast} it delegates to, and, worse, the 53.27\,s that rank 2 spent waiting
inside the reduce and the 55.02\,s that rank 1 spent waiting inside the broadcast are concurrent
waits by different ranks in the same 55\,s of wall clock. Excluding the composed operations still
leaves 93.6\%, so the composition is not the main problem. The defensible figure is per-rank: summing
each rank's blocking time across the leaf collectives gives 421.35 rank-seconds against 1,007.6
available, 41.8\%, and adding the 585.72 rank-seconds of agent latency accounts for 99.9\% of the
pool. The same computation on the baseline gives 219.25 rank-seconds in leaf collectives plus 34.72
in the halo, 19.0\% in total, with 99.9\% again accounted for. So the ablation \emph{raised} the
coordination share from 19.0\% to 41.8\%, and it did so by removing work rather than waiting: the
denominator of useful activity shrank while the one large wait, the glossary reduction dilated by
rank 3, stayed. That is a real and slightly counter-intuitive result about ablations, and it is
invisible in the reported 35.7\%-to-137.4\% comparison, which is not a comparison of shares at all.

\section{Threats to this reading}

The most serious threat is specific and it is fatal to one arm of the experiment: the translations in
this run are not statistically independent of the baseline's, and in several sections they are not
independent in the strongest possible sense. Recovering both runs' translate outputs from their
broker spools and hashing them, four of the eight sections here are byte-identical to an artefact of
the baseline. Sections 0, 4 and 5 are byte-identical to the baseline's \emph{post}-halo text --- that
is, this run's supposedly unreconciled output already contains the baseline's seam repairs --- and
section 3 is byte-identical to the baseline's pre-halo text. Section 7 reproduces all six of the
multi-character fragments its baseline revision introduced and then adds one further edit of its own;
across all eight sections, 10 of the 14 multi-character fragments the baseline's revision introduced
appear verbatim here. The translate prompts are not identical between the runs (they differ by one
character on the shared-register line), so a prompt-digest cache cannot explain this, and no
independent sampling reproduces 1,371 characters of Chinese prose byte for byte. The two runs were
recorded 3\,min\,41\,s apart on the same commit in the same working tree, with the baseline's
\texttt{runs/real-tr-p8-full/output/} and spool on disk, and the \texttt{broker} executor works by
publishing prompts to a spool for an external worker to fill in. The straightforward explanation is
that the worker reused the earlier run's text. This does not affect any of the protocol
measurements --- message counts, event structure, timings and the neighbourhood-maximum result are
all properties of the harness and the fabric, not of the text --- but it means the boundary-drift
comparison cannot be made from these two runs even if a seam metric existed, because five of eight
sections already carry the baseline's boundary repairs. It also retracts the strength of the
identical-glossary observation above: a run that reuses the neighbour's outputs is not the clean A/B
that identical digest would otherwise imply.

The executor is \texttt{broker}, so this is not a surrogate standing in for a model, but the way the
broker is driven leaves two marks on the numbers. Agent latencies are quantised to
\texttt{BrokerExecutor.poll\_s}, 0.5\,s, which is visible in the log as every latency landing on a
half-second plus 10--30\,ms; the reported p50 and p95 of 42.02 and 49.52\,s are therefore accurate
only to about half a second, which does not matter at these magnitudes but does mean the latency
distribution is not the model's. And the provenance block records
\texttt{tokenizer\_exact: false}, so every token figure is a heuristic estimate. That is visible: a
1,371-character Simplified Chinese section is reported as roughly 400 completion tokens, which is
low by a factor of two or more. The token deltas I quote --- 14,448 prompt and 3,582 completion --- are
consistent with the revision calls' own reported totals and so are internally coherent, but their
absolute scale, and therefore the \$0.0971 saving, inherits the tokenizer's error.

Rank 3's 75.53\,s extraction call is one draw from one run, and it is doing most of the work in every
aggregate that looks bad here: the 55\,s dead zone, the $1.61\times$ imbalance, the 55.7\% parallel
efficiency and the 41.8\% coordination share are all downstream of it. If it had matched the slowest
of the other seven this run would have finished near 75\,s rather than 126\,s, which would have made
the ablation look considerably better than the 24.5\,\% wall-time saving I report and would have
inverted the coordination-share comparison. I have kept the reported numbers and separated the
straggler's contribution where it matters, but no single run can distinguish a straggler that is a
property of the configuration from one that is a property of the afternoon.

The neighbourhood-maximum result is the strongest claim in this document and it is verified on 16
rank-instances across two runs, all at $p=8$ on a one-dimensional non-periodic ring. It should not be
extrapolated without checking. On a periodic ring no rank has a single neighbour and the endpoint
escape that produces the 20.70\,s figure cannot occur; in two dimensions the neighbourhood is four
ranks and the maximum is taken over more of the population, so the exchange moves further towards a
true barrier. The conclusion that a halo exchange is ``a synchronisation point in all but name'' is
therefore conservative for larger neighbourhoods and specific to open boundaries for the escape.

Finally, two of my comparisons are arithmetic rather than measurement, and should be read that way.
The $\Theta(p)$-versus-$\Theta(p^2)$ contrast --- 14 messages against 56 --- compares the exchange's
logged traffic to the message count an all-gather of boundaries would require; no sibling run
performed that all-gather, so the alternative is computed from the topology and not observed. And
the claim that the scorer is blind to seams is inferred from reading its metric definitions together
with the identical scores; I did not implement a boundary-continuity metric and run it over the two
runs' outputs, which is what would settle the question. Given the contamination described above,
doing so on these particular artefacts would not settle it either.

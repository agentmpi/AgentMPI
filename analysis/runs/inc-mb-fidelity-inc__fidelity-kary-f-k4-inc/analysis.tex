\begin{abstract}
This is the paper's own contribution measured in the one configuration where the reduction
actually loses information, and it is the best cell in the campaign on every cost axis and
indistinguishable from the worst on quality. A $k$-ary tree with $k=4$ over eight ranks
folds four reports in one variadic application, so the whole reduction is three
applications at fold depth 2 rather than seven at depth 3 or 7. The measured counts are
exactly the closed forms: \texttt{fold\_depth: 2} $=\lceil\log_4 8\rceil$, three agent
calls $=\lceil (p-1)/(k-1)\rceil = \lceil 7/3\rceil$, seven messages $=p-1$. It completes
in 51.77\,s against the binomial tree's 80.27\,s and the chain's 634.43\,s, emits 1{,}323
output tokens against 2{,}492 and 2{,}623, and costs \$0.0275 against \$0.0500 and
\$0.0524. And it retains exactly the same 37 of 96 items --- the same identifiers, ranks 0,
1 and 2 complete plus \texttt{F-3-0}, ranks 4 through 7 erased entirely. That is the honest
form of the $k$-ary argument and it is not the form the paper states. The published
justification is that a lossy operator's quality decays in fold depth, so halving the depth
should buy fidelity; this campaign refutes that premise, because depth 2 and depth 7 give
byte-identical results. What survives, and what these cells establish, is stronger for
being simpler: since an agent has no port count, a wide tree performs fewer operator
applications for the same collective, and under a uniform enforced budget that is pure
saving. Collective selection here is a cost decision, and $k$-ary wins it outright.
\end{abstract}

\section{Reading the timeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} has the cleanest structure of any run in my set, and it is the
$k$-ary tree drawn in two bands. Rank 0 and rank 4 hold bars together from about $t=2$\,s
to $t=30$\,s; then rank 0 alone holds a second bar from about $t=32$\,s to 51.77\,s. Ranks
1, 2 and 3 emit one tick each and exit; so do 5, 6 and 7. That is precisely what the
algorithm's two strides produce: at \texttt{stride = 1} the ranks with $vr \bmod 4 = 0$
--- 0 and 4 --- each collect three children and combine all four values in a single
application, and at \texttt{stride = 4} rank 4 sends to rank 0, which combines two. Peak
ranks busy is 2 of 8 and the serial fraction of active time is 50.3\%, which is not
inefficiency but the arithmetic of a two-level tree whose second level has one node.
Sixty-three events, no trailing events at all, and the log's last entry coincides with
\texttt{job.finish} --- the only broker-executed cell in the campaign with no
post-completion tail, because the run finished before the worker pool started churning.
The dashboard's rank health table accordingly shows ranks 0 and 1 \texttt{finalized}
rather than the wall of red \texttt{fail\_stop} suspicions its siblings display.

Three bars, three latencies: 29.52\,s for rank 4's four-way fold, 24.27\,s for rank 0's,
and 19.41\,s for the final two-way fold, on prompts of 773, 773 and 1{,}014 tokens.
Compare the binomial sibling, where the same 96 items required seven calls of 16--28\,s.
Per-call latency is essentially unchanged by widening the fan-in --- 24.4\,s mean here
against 21.8\,s there, on prompts 1.7$\times$ larger --- which is the empirical content of
the claim that an agent is not single-ported. A rank that can absorb four reports in one
prompt for the same 25 seconds it would have spent on two has no reason to prefer a binary
tree, and the cost of the wider prompt shows up as 2{,}560 total prompt tokens against the
binomial cell's 4{,}220: fewer, larger prompts are cheaper in total than more, smaller
ones, because the accumulator is not re-read at every level.

The communication matrix, Figure~\ref{fig:comm}, is two columns: $1,2,3\!\to\!0$ and
$5,6,7\!\to\!4$ at 178 tokens each, then $4\!\to\!0$ at 469, for seven messages and 1{,}537
tokens. That is the same message count as every other algorithm at this $p$ --- widening
the tree changes rounds and applications, never messages, because every non-root rank
sends exactly once --- and it is 37\% less traffic than the chain's 2{,}448, since only one
accumulator ever crosses the fabric instead of six.

\section{Interpretation}

The counts are by construction and all three closed forms are worth checking explicitly
because this algorithm has no sibling in the collective sweeps and these cells are its only
sweep-adjacent evidence. Fold depth: the \texttt{kary} branch advances
\texttt{stride} by factors of $k$ and increments depth once per level, giving
$\lceil\log_4 8\rceil = 2$, and the \texttt{coll.reduce} payload records exactly 2 against
the binary tree's 3 and the chain's 7. Applications: each variadic call consumes $k$ inputs
and retires $k-1$ of them, so a $p$-rank fold needs $\lceil (p-1)/(k-1)\rceil = 3$ at $p=8$
with $k=4$, and the trace holds exactly three \texttt{agent.call} events --- two labelled
\texttt{merge:k4:d1} and one \texttt{merge:k2:d2}, the label carrying the actual arity so
the degenerate two-way top level is visible rather than inferred. Messages: seven, matching
$p-1$. The runtime also records \texttt{variadic: true} in the collective's \texttt{extra}
block, which matters because \texttt{Op.combine} silently degrades to a left fold when no
variadic kernel exists, and a $k$-ary tree without one would spend exactly the depth it was
built to save.

The result is that the quality argument for $k$-ary fails and the cost argument does not
need it. Retention is 0.3854 here and 0.3854 in the depth-3 tree, the depth-7 chain and the
depth-7 flat fold; I compared the survivor sets from the store and the symmetric difference
is empty, and the final blob \texttt{9f300f3a45bf} is the same object the binomial and flat
cells returned. The reason is the budget. An incompressible item costs 11.81 tokens once
quoted into the JSON array, so an enforced 450-token ceiling holds $450/11.81 = 38.1$ of
them, and every merge in this campaign lands there: this run's two level-0 folds returned
449 tokens with 38 identifiers and 437 with 37. Whichever application is last is the one
that binds, so the surviving count is $B/c$ regardless of how many applications preceded
it, and there is no slack for a deeper tree's earlier losses to compound into. Fold depth
would govern fidelity only if interior nodes were allowed larger budgets than the root,
which is what the weight-proportional variant in \texttt{semantic\_op} exists to provide
and what \texttt{--weighted} would have selected; every run in this campaign is
\texttt{-f}, fixed budget, so that conditional remains untested.

Read as a cost decision, this cell dominates. Same 37 items, same seven messages, same
zero hallucinated identifiers, and 3 applications instead of 7: 35\% less wall time than
the binomial tree, 47\% fewer output tokens, 45\% lower modelled cost, 39\% fewer prompt
tokens. Against the chain it is 12.3$\times$ faster, though I would not defend that
magnitude (see the threats). The one respect in which it is not free is context
concentration: a level-0 rank here ingests 773 prompt tokens against 446 in the binary
tree, so the binding constraint on $k$ is the receiving rank's context budget, exactly as
\texttt{optimal\_fanin} says. At the 32{,}768-token budget these runs declare that
constraint is nowhere near binding --- \texttt{optimal\_fanin(32768, 450)} returns its cap
of 32 --- so $k=4$ was a conservative choice made by the \texttt{--fanin} flag rather than
by the policy, and the interesting configuration is the untested one: at $k \ge 8$ the
whole reduction at $p=8$ would be a single 8-way application at depth 1.

There is a defect to report, and it is in the cost model rather than the implementation.
The collectives table shows rounds 2 against a prediction of 1, and yet the model column
reads \modelok. Two things are wrong. First, the tabulated entry
\texttt{("reduce", "kary")} in \texttt{cost.py} evaluates $\lceil\log_k p\rceil$ at
\texttt{DEFAULT\_FANIN = 8}, not at the fan-in the run used, so at $p=8$ it predicts the
single-application depth-1 tree while the run performed the $k=4$ two-level one; the
prediction is for a different experiment. \texttt{predict\_kary(p, n, k)} takes an explicit
$k$ and exists precisely for this, but \texttt{analysis.py} consults \texttt{FORMULAS}.
Second, the run-level check \texttt{Analysis.model\_checks} counts only
\texttt{messages\_agree}, so \texttt{rounds\_agree: false} is displayed in the table and in
\texttt{metrics.json} but never reaches the finding that says ``all 1 checkable
collective(s) sent exactly the number of messages their cost expression predicts'' --- true
as written, and reassuring in a way the underlying data does not support. The $p=16$
\texttt{fid-synth} $k$-ary cell hides the same mismatch by coincidence, since
$\lceil\log_8 16\rceil$ and $\lceil\log_4 16\rceil$ are both 2.

\section{Threats to this reading}

The executor is real: \texttt{executors} records \texttt{broker}~$\times$~8 and
\texttt{worker}~$\times$~10, so the three merges were performed by models and the numbers
are agent behaviour. The surrogate $k$-ary cells in \texttt{fid-synth} and
\texttt{fid-inc-smoke} must not be pooled with this one, because the \texttt{function}
executor's operator drops whole accumulator blobs at a fixed probability per application
and therefore implements a depth-loss model rather than testing it.

The dominance claim rests on one trial per algorithm, and the two comparisons are not
equally safe. Against the binomial tree the mechanism and the measurement agree: 3
applications against 7, 51.77\,s against 80.27\,s, a ratio of 1.55 where the
critical-path count predicts 1.5, and mean per-call latency differs by only 12\% between
the two runs. Against the chain the measurement is unreliable: the chain's calls averaged
89.3\,s against this run's 24.4\,s on comparable prompts, with 7.1\,s of broker queue wait
in that run, so most of the 12.3$\times$ is per-call latency inflation in the pool rather
than structure. The structural prediction is 3.5$\times$, and that is the figure I would
defend.

Two accounting caveats specific to this cell. The final application is a two-way fold
labelled \texttt{merge:k2:d2}, not a four-way one, because $p$ is not a power of $k$; so
this run measures $k=4$ at one level and $k=2$ at the other, and a clean $k=4$ measurement
would need $p=16$. The \texttt{fid-synth} $k$-ary cell has that geometry --- four four-way
folds and one four-way fold above them, five applications for $\lceil 15/3\rceil$ --- but
it is a surrogate. Second, this run's final result is byte-identical to the binomial and
flat cells' and the four cells shared one persistent pool of Cursor subagents. I ran the
determinism test the contract requires and the executor fails it: two prompt digests are
shared across cells in this campaign and both return different results. The identity is
better explained by there being one canonical answer --- the survivors are verbatim
high-entropy strings emitted in rank order, so once the cut lands at 37 items the string is
determined --- but I cannot exclude pool memory, only observe that it would have nothing
left to explain.

Finally, the token arithmetic uses the repository's approximate counter
(\texttt{tokenizer\_exact: false}), which is the same counter the contract check uses, so
the ``38 items fit in 450 tokens'' figure is internally consistent rather than externally
valid; the \$0.0275 is modelled from \$3/Mtok in and \$15/Mtok out; and every
\texttt{msg.recv} records \texttt{admitted: false} because the collective's internal
receives pass \texttt{admit=False}, so the 0\% context occupancy in the per-rank table says
nothing about whether a wider fan-in stresses a rank's context. That is a real gap, since
context is the quantity the $k$-ary argument says should bound $k$.

\begin{abstract}
Eight real agents were each given one module of a small SQL engine, a specification with
every module signature stripped out of it, and no shared window through which to publish or
read interfaces. It is the hardest cell of a two-by-two ablation and the intended upper bound
on defensive coupling. The population still reached a green build: 55 of 60 acceptance cases
at first integration and 60 of 60 after one repair round, agreed unanimously by \texttt{agree}
at 847.5\,s. It did so by refusing to depend on anyone. Three of the eight declared dependency
edges were honoured in round one and five of eight in round two, and even those five are
\texttt{getattr} bindings against a module object rather than imported names; the owner of
\texttt{parser.py} reimplemented both the lexer and the syntax tree rather than import modules
whose interfaces it had not seen, leaving \texttt{nodes.py} --- 378 lines that another agent
wrote, and went on revising --- imported by nothing. The resulting tree is 3,556 lines against
the 2,195 lines the same modules, the same specification and the same commit produced seven
minutes earlier when the window was available. The most important number is not the pass rate,
which is identical in both arms, but the shape of the bill: through round one the two arms
cost almost the same (82,298 tokens without the window, 86,473 with it), and the entire
2.2$\times$ difference in total cost is the review-and-repair round that only the windowless
arm needed. Withholding the interface channel did not make the first attempt more expensive;
it made a second attempt necessary.

Two caveats belong in the abstract rather than only in the threats section, because both bear
on what this cell measures. First, every implementation prompt in this run states ``(this
module has no dependencies)'', which is false for six of the eight modules; commit
\texttt{f121647} replaced that text 27 minutes after the run finished. The defect is present
only in the windowless arm, so it is confounded with the treatment. It is not, however, the
false-premise confound it appears to be: all six affected ranks recovered their true
dependencies from the specification's surviving module-layout table and said so in their
\texttt{concerns}, two of them citing that table by name. What the defect actually removed is
narrower and is the second caveat. Both texts that forbid duplicating a dependency's work ---
the specification's ``do not duplicate its logic'' clause, which the vague-layout substitution
deletes along with the signatures, and the prompt clause that \texttt{f121647} later added ---
are absent from this cell and from no other. The surviving instruction, ``do not invent a
different signature for someone else's module'', cannot be obeyed without a channel except by
duplication. That is why the same prompt defect was inert in \texttt{real-sw-p8-noshared},
which honoured seven of eight edges, and damaging here.
\end{abstract}

\section{Reading the timeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} has four bar clusters per lane and they are the four agent phases of
the harness, separated by the three barriers that dominate the run's idle time. From 0 to
42.5\,s every rank publishes its interface. From 42.5 to 319.8\,s every rank implements its
module. From 320.3 to 483.9\,s every rank reviews two peers. From 423.4 to 846.7\,s every rank
implements again. Nothing else happens: 32 agent invocations, four per rank, no retries, no
contract violations and no degraded calls.

The gaps are all the same gap. Rank~0 owns \texttt{errors.py} and finished its round-one
implementation 11.0\,s after starting it, at $t=53.7$; it then sat at the \texttt{integrate-r1}
barrier for the 266.4\,s that barrier reports as its wall, because rank~6 owns
\texttt{engine.py} and its round-one agent call took 277.1\,s. The same thing happens again and
worse in round two: rank~0 finished at $t=464.1$ and waited 382.9\,s for rank~6, which finished
at $t=846.7$. Those two waits are 649.3\,s, or 76.6\,\% of the 847.5\,s the run took, spent by
one rank doing nothing. Rank~0's 18.5\,\% occupancy in the per-rank table is that fact restated.

The straggler is not random, and this is the substantive point about the shape. Round-one agent
latencies, ordered, are 11.0\,s for \texttt{errors}, 12.5\,s for \texttt{nodes}, 25.0\,s for
\texttt{tokens}, 56.5\,s for \texttt{functions}, 169.6\,s for \texttt{api}, 188.1\,s for
\texttt{parser}, 235.6\,s for \texttt{planner} and 277.1\,s for \texttt{engine}. That is exactly
the order of the module dependency graph, from its sources to its sink, and the per-rank busy
times in the generated table reproduce it: 156.7, 175.2, 284.2, 311.8, 571.8, 468.8, 761.5 and
502.6\,s for ranks 0 through 7, which own \texttt{errors}, \texttt{tokens}, \texttt{nodes},
\texttt{functions}, \texttt{parser}, \texttt{planner}, \texttt{engine} and \texttt{api}
respectively. The 1.88$\times$ load imbalance is therefore structural rather than stochastic:
the harness assigns module $i$ to rank $i \bmod p$, the modules are of very unequal difficulty,
and a barrier after every round converts that inequality directly into idle rank-seconds. A
scheduler that knew the DAG could not fix this either, because there is only one
\texttt{engine.py} and someone has to write it.

Figure~\ref{fig:concurrency} is the same story as a sawtooth. Concurrency is 8 at the start,
decays to 1 by $t \approx 280$ as the easy modules finish, snaps back to 8 at $t = 320.3$ when
the barrier, the report broadcast and the blame scatter release every rank within 0.1\,s of each
other, decays again, peaks at 7 around $t = 490$ as the repair round starts, and then falls to 1
from $t \approx 690$ to the end while rank~6 finishes alone. The 24.4\,\% serial fraction is
those two tails. Achieved parallelism is 3.81 against a world size of 8, so the run bought eight
agents and used just under four.

The two coordination figures say different things and both are worth quoting. Collectives
account for 3,489.7 of the 6,780.0 rank-seconds the pool had available, a 51.5\,\% share; but
some rank is inside a collective for 759.5 of the 847.5\,s of wall clock, an 89.6\,\% span. The
gap between them is the whole point of measuring both: coordination is nearly always in flight
here, yet it occupies only half the purchased rank-time, because it is one or two ranks
computing while the other six or seven block. Neither number indicts the fabric. Of the 3,489.7
coordination rank-seconds, 3,267.9 --- 93.6\,\% --- are the three barriers, 217.3 are the
\texttt{reviewback-r1} exchange waiting for two slow reviewers, and the eight collectives that
actually move data cost 4.5 rank-seconds between them while carrying 120,565 tokens. In
per-invocation wall terms the same collectives cost 1.40\,s in total (0.026 + 0.430 + 0.428\,s
for the three broadcasts, 0.245 + 0.264\,s for the two gathers, 0.004 + 0.002\,s for the two
scatters). A barrier's wall is by definition the time the first arrival waits for the last, so
what the 51.5\,\% measures is agent latency spread, not protocol cost. The clinching arithmetic
is that the run has 3,547.6 idle rank-seconds and 3,489.7 coordination rank-seconds: 98.4\,\% of
all idleness in this run is time spent blocked inside a collective, which is to say waiting for
whichever agent was slowest.

Figure~\ref{fig:comm} shows one bright cell, $4 \rightarrow 0$ at 48,501 tokens, with
$6 \rightarrow 4$ at 20,410 and $5 \rightarrow 4$ at 17,020 behind it. That is the binomial
gather tree seen edge-on: rank~4 is the interior node that folds ranks 5, 6 and 7 and forwards
the combined code bundle to the root for the acceptance run. The concentration is the algorithm

touches any rank's context: 128,769 of the 169,184 tokens sent were deferred under rendezvous,
and all 169,184 arrived unadmitted, because every collective in this harness is called with

That last equality is why the right-hand panel of Figure~\ref{fig:ranks} is empty. With
\texttt{tokens\_unadmitted} equal to \texttt{tokens\_sent}, context occupancy is 0.0\,\% against

Two things in the viewer are worth naming because they can be misread. The violet glyphs at the

\section{Interpretation}

\subsection{What is true by construction}

The run completed with all eight ranks finalized, no failed ranks, no contract violations across
32 invocations, no retries, and \texttt{degraded} false, so nothing here is a story about the
fabric breaking. The executors are \texttt{broker}$\times$8 with 46 worker attachments, which
means real agents served every call; this is evidence about agent behaviour, not about a
surrogate. The 46 reattachments and the maximum incarnation of 8 are the broker model working as
designed --- a rank role, its mailbox and its context account outlive the process serving it ---
and their distribution is informative rather than alarming: reattachment counts of 7, 7, 7, 6,
5, 5, 5 and 4 for ranks 0 through 7 are perfectly rank-order anticorrelated with busy times of
156.7 through 761.5\,s, which is what you expect if workers are recycled between calls and a
long call holds its worker.

The absence of any \texttt{win.} event is also by construction: with
\texttt{--no-shared-interfaces} the harness creates no window and substitutes a \texttt{PROCEED}
barrier labelled \texttt{interfaces-skipped}, which is duly present at $t = 42.5$ with a 31.2\,s
wall. The interface phase still runs. Eight agents still wrote eight interface documents,
costing 13,862 prompt and 9,959 completion tokens --- 12.4\,\% of the run's 191,602 tokens ---
and those documents were written to no channel. They are not entirely wasted, because the
harness feeds a rank its own published interface back into its implementation prompt as a
commitment it must honour, but no other rank ever saw one.

\subsection{What the two-by-two actually shows}

The design point is the low corner of a two-by-two over \emph{specification precision} and
\emph{interface channel}. Reading it required recovering two numbers, because the results
recorded for the two precise-spec cells are wrong. Both
\texttt{results/software/real-sw-p8-noshared-locks-review.json} and its \texttt{shared} sibling
record \texttt{importable: false} with \texttt{n\_total} 0, and their \texttt{import\_error}
field contains a mid-slice of the oracle's own JSON report full of \texttt{"passed": true}. That
is the signature of the \texttt{out.rfind("\{")} parse bug present at commit \texttt{49f2bba} and
fixed in \texttt{1f7bd01} at 07:59:39; the two runs started at 07:47:06 and 07:55:25 and so
executed the broken code, and their provenance stamps a later commit only because provenance is
captured when the result is written. I recovered the true values by copying each run's
\texttt{workspace/round2} tree to a scratch directory outside the repository and running the
unmodified oracle (\texttt{experiments/minidb/acceptance.py}) against it. The file modification
times of those trees fall inside their runs' trace windows, so they are the artefacts those runs
produced.

The interaction is the finding, and it is visible in five independent measures. Under a precise
specification, withholding the window costs 35\,\% more lines, 3\,\% more tokens, 20\,\% more
dollars, one dependency edge and one acceptance case. Under a vague specification it costs
62\,\% more lines, 122\,\% more tokens, 154\,\% more dollars, five dependency edges in round one
and a whole extra round. That is the confound the vague-spec variant was built to remove, stated
as a measurement: when the broadcast specification already pins every signature, it \emph{is} a
shared interface and the window has little left to contribute; when it does not, the window is

The corrected coordination share separates the four cells in a way the old figure could not, and
it separates them by channel rather than by specification: 41.7\,\% and 44.8\,\% for the two

the window leaves the dependency-free modules doing byte-identical work --- they are literally
the same files, as shown below --- while inflating the work of the modules that have
dependencies. Widening the spread while holding one end fixed is precisely how you increase the
share of rank-time spent at a barrier. The ordering is not identical to the load-imbalance

per cell the transposition is not worth interpreting. The windowed/windowless split is consistent

\subsection{Why they duplicated: a directive conflict, not a misunderstanding}

The population converged by decoupling rather than by agreeing, and the trace explains why in the
agents' own words. Counting the eight declared dependency edges that are not \texttt{errors}
(which everything imports and which the specification names outright): \texttt{parser}
$\rightarrow$ \texttt{tokens}, \texttt{parser} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{nodes}, \texttt{planner}
$\rightarrow$ \texttt{nodes}, \texttt{engine} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{planner}, \texttt{engine}
$\rightarrow$ \texttt{functions}, and \texttt{api} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{parser},
\texttt{planner}, \texttt{engine}. In round one, three appear as imports in the generated tree,
all three of them \texttt{api}'s. In round two, five do. In \texttt{vague-sw-p8-vague-shared} all
eight do, and in \texttt{real-sw-p8-noshared} seven do. The three edges never honoured here are
exactly the three that point at a module whose interface was withheld and whose job could be
duplicated: \texttt{parser.py} contains its own lexer and its own AST classes, which it then
exports, and \texttt{planner.py} builds its own resolved-expression IR.

The obvious reading --- that the agents did not know they had dependencies, because the prompt
told them so --- is wrong, and the \texttt{concerns} fields refute it individually. All six ranks
whose modules have dependencies named those dependencies correctly and flagged the withholding.
The \texttt{functions} owner: ``No dependency interface was supplied for errors, but the
specification fixes QueryError in \texttt{minidb/errors.py} as the system's single error type.''
The \texttt{engine} owner quotes the prompt back at it: ``No interface was published for planner
or functions (`this module has no dependencies' in my prompt), so I invented no signature for
either module.'' The \texttt{api} owner: ``the prompt states this module has no dependencies, yet
\texttt{api.py} is the module whose entire job is to tie errors/tokens/parser/planner/engine
together.'' And the \texttt{parser} owner names the source of its knowledge: ``No interface was
published to me for \texttt{minidb/tokens.py} or \texttt{minidb/nodes.py}, \emph{even though the
module layout lets \texttt{parser.py} import them}.'' The module-layout table is intact in this
run. \texttt{apply\_vague\_layout} replaces the signatures in the responsibility column but keeps
the three-column table, including the \texttt{may import} column, so the dependency graph is
stated identically in the vague and precise specifications. The recovery route was open and six
of six ranks used it.

What the vague layout does remove, and what nobody has yet named, is the prohibition. The precise
\texttt{SPEC.md} closes its module-layout section with ``Module boundaries are contracts. If your
module needs something from another module, use the interface that module has published; do not
reach around it and \emph{do not duplicate its logic}.'' \texttt{VAGUE\_LAYOUT} replaces that
paragraph wholesale, and its replacement forbids something narrower: ``Do not guess at another
module's interface: use the interface that module has published, and if it is insufficient, work
within it and say so rather than inventing a different signature for someone else's module.''
Grepping the actual prompts confirms it --- the string ``duplicate its logic'' appears once in
\texttt{real-sw-p8-noshared}'s prompts and zero times in this run's. The other place the
prohibition could have come from is the implementation prompt itself, and the branch that carries
it (``do not reimplement their responsibilities in your own file'') was not written until
\texttt{f121647}, after this run.

So in this cell, and in no other, both anti-duplication instructions are absent, while the one
instruction that survives cannot be obeyed. ``Use the interface that module has published''
presupposes a channel; without one, an agent that refuses to invent a neighbour's signature has
exactly one compliant move left, which is to stop depending on the neighbour. The
\texttt{parser} owner states the syllogism and acts on it, in round one --- ``Importing names
from those modules would mean inventing signatures for modules I do not own'' --- and again in
round two, after peer review, unrepentant: ``\texttt{minidb/nodes.py} is therefore unused by this
module: no interface was ever published for \texttt{nodes.py} or \texttt{tokens.py}, so importing
from them would have meant inventing another [module's signature].'' This is not confusion. It is
correct reasoning from an instruction set that, in this configuration only, makes duplication the
compliant answer.

That reframes the two-by-two as a two-by-two over the anti-duplication clause as well. With a
window, the clause is idle because nothing tempts duplication, and both windowed cells honour
8/8. Without a window but with the precise spec, the clause is present and binding, and
\texttt{real-sw-p8-noshared} honours 7/8 --- its \texttt{parser.py} does import \texttt{tokens}
and \texttt{nodes}. Without a window and without the clause, this run honours 3/8. The prompt
defect was inert there and damaging here for exactly this reason, and it is a mechanism rather
than a confound.

\subsection{What defensive coupling looks like when it is measured}

The behaviour has a measurable signature. Across the eight generated files, this run's final tree
contains 10 \texttt{getattr} calls, 2 \texttt{inspect} uses and 77 \texttt{: Any} annotations,
against 0, 0 and 28 for the windowed arm on the same specification and the same commit;
\texttt{real-sw-p8-noshared} has 25 \texttt{getattr} calls against 5 for
\texttt{real-sw-p8-full}. Normalised for size, \texttt{: Any} appears 2.17 times per hundred
lines here and 1.28 times per hundred in the windowed arm. The clearest single artefact is
\texttt{api.py}'s \texttt{\_parser\_wants\_tokens}, which inspects the parser's first parameter
name and annotation for the substrings \texttt{token}, \texttt{sql}, \texttt{str}, \texttt{text}
and \texttt{source}, calls the parser with whichever argument it guesses, and falls back to the
other on \texttt{TypeError}. That is interface negotiation implemented in the caller at run time
because no negotiation channel existed at build time.

The two edges gained in round two are honoured only in a weak sense, and the qualification
matters. \texttt{engine.py} writes \texttt{from . import functions as \_functions} and
\texttt{from . import planner as \_planner}, then binds every name it wants through
\texttt{getattr(\_functions, "SCALARS", None)} and four siblings, and discovers the planner's node
classes by probing the module object for names. Its author explains the choice: ``Because no
interface for functions was published to this module, the names are resolved once at import from
the module object rather than imported by name.'' An \texttt{ImportError} is impossible by
construction, and so is any check that the dependency provides what the dependant needs; the
failure mode has been converted from a loud one at import time into a silent \texttt{None} at run
time. Counting that as a restored dependency edge flatters the round-two tree.

The waste is symmetrical and reaches modules that did nothing wrong. \texttt{nodes.py} is
imported by no module in the final tree, and its owner nevertheless spent a full repair call
acting on peer review, adding \texttt{Select = SelectStmt} at line 221 ``so \texttt{from .nodes
import Select} works'' --- an alias added for an importer that does not exist, in a file that
grew from 302 to 378 lines while remaining dead. \texttt{tokens.py} is reached only through
\texttt{api.py}, which calls \texttt{tokenize(sql)} on every query and then discards the result
because \texttt{inspect.signature} tells it the parser wants a string.

The cost is localised precisely where the mechanism predicts. The four dependency-free modules
--- \texttt{errors.py} (31 lines), \texttt{tokens.py} (222), \texttt{nodes.py} (302) and
\texttt{functions.py} (248) --- are \emph{byte-identical} between this run and
\texttt{vague-sw-p8-vague-shared}, verified by MD5. The divergence is entirely in the four modules
with dependencies: \texttt{parser} 437 $\rightarrow$ 730 lines, \texttt{planner} 549
$\rightarrow$ 766, \texttt{engine} 342 $\rightarrow$ 804, \texttt{api} 64 $\rightarrow$ 206,
comparing the windowed arm to this run at round one. The same pattern holds for the precise-spec
pair, whose four leaf modules are likewise identical to each other. A single run per cell cannot
establish an effect size, but this is not a difference of two noisy draws: half the artefact is
bit-for-bit the same and the cost falls only on the half where the mechanism could possibly act.

\subsection{Why 482 events and not 311, and what the extra activity was}

The difference between this run's 482 events and the windowed arm's 311 is not more negotiation.
It is a review round and a repair round that the windowed arm never needed. The windowed arm made
16 agent calls, one interface and one implementation per rank, scored 60 of 60 at first
integration, and \texttt{agree} returned true, so the loop broke before review. This run made 32
calls across four phases and its round-one \texttt{agree} returned false at $t = 320.3$, which is
what put the review and repair phases on the timeline at all. Sixteen \texttt{topo.graph\_create}
events --- two graphs per rank, the review digraph and its transpose --- and 16
\texttt{coll.neighbor\_allgather} events are the review machinery that only exists in this arm.

The extra activity was productive rather than thrashing, and the trace lets one say so sharply.
Round one failed five cases: \texttt{order\_desc}, \texttt{order\_nulls\_last\_desc},
\texttt{order\_by\_alias}, \texttt{join\_group} and \texttt{empty\_table\_select}. Four of the
five are one root cause. Round-one \texttt{parser.py} declares its ORDER BY item as
\texttt{ascending: bool = True} while round-one \texttt{planner.py} probes the item for a field
named \texttt{descending} or \texttt{desc}, finds neither, and falls through to
\texttt{bool(None)}, so every DESC in the suite silently planned as ASC. The peer review named it
exactly: the \texttt{engine} owner, reviewing \texttt{parser} and \texttt{planner}, wrote ``ORDER
BY direction polarity mismatch. \texttt{parser.OrderItem} carries \texttt{ascending: bool = True};
planner publishes \texttt{OrderItem.descending}. A structural read of `descending'/`desc' finds no
such field, so DESC silently b[ecomes ASC].'' The fifth failure was named twice, by the owners of
\texttt{api} and \texttt{errors}, both pointing at \texttt{engine.py}'s
\texttt{raise QueryError("query has no output columns")} firing on \texttt{SELECT * FROM e} over
an empty table. Every one of the five acceptance failures was identified by name in the review
round, before the repair round, by an agent that did not own the failing module and was reading
only a code excerpt. The reviews carry no acceptance results --- the review prompt contains
source, not failures --- so this is diagnosis from the code, not restatement of the oracle.

The repair is equally legible, and it shows the price of having no channel even when the outcome
is right. In round two the parser flipped its field to \texttt{descending: bool = False} and added
an \texttt{ascending} property for compatibility; independently, the planner grew a
\texttt{\_descending\_of} helper that probes \texttt{descending}, then \texttt{desc}, then
\texttt{ascending}, then \texttt{asc}, negating as required. Both sides fixed the same mismatch,
in ignorance of each other, and the fix is compatible because each was defensive enough to accept
the other's convention. That is convergence, but it is convergence paid for twice, and the 247
extra lines between round one and round two are largely what it cost.

\subsection{The flagged findings}

The two \texttt{warning} findings are the same finding twice and are a real defect, but in the
trace emitter rather than in the protocol. \texttt{neighbor\_allgather} reports zero messages for
\texttt{review-r1} and \texttt{reviewback-r1} while the fabric logged 16 each. The arithmetic is
exact: of 81 \texttt{msg.send} events, 49 carry the internal tags of the two broadcasts, two
gathers and two scatters (7 each), and the remaining 32 carry \texttt{\_ampi:nbr\_ag:0:8} and
\texttt{\_ampi:nbr\_ag:0:9}, 16 apiece. The neighbourhood collectives emitted in-degree and
out-degree but no \texttt{messages\_sent}, so every trace read zero traffic for them. Commit
\texttt{ee21d79} fixes precisely this, on 2026-09-01, and this run is from 2026-08-30, so the
trace predates the fix and cannot be re-emitted. What should be said in its favour is that the
cross-check earned its keep: the check compares the collective's self-report against
\texttt{msg.send} events carrying its tag, and it caught an omission that the collective's own
accounting could not. The cost of the gap is visible in Figure~\ref{fig:collectives}, which plots
10 of the 12 collectives and silently omits the two that disagree, and in the dashboard's
collectives panel, where \texttt{neighbor\_allgather} shows 16 invocations and 0 messages next to
\texttt{bcast}'s 21.

The 51.5\,\% coordination note is expected for this configuration and is not a defect, for the
reason given in the timeline section: 93.6\,\% of those rank-seconds are barrier waits whose
length is agent latency spread, and the data-moving collectives cost 4.5 rank-seconds between
them. The note's wording --- that more of the pool's time went to coordination than to work --- is
arithmetically right (51.5\,\% against 47.7\,\% parallel efficiency) but the margin is 3.8 points,
and a reader should not take it as a large asymmetry. The 1.88$\times$ imbalance note is likewise
expected and structural, being the difference between writing \texttt{errors.py} and writing
\texttt{engine.py}. The 46 reattachments are the broker model working. The cost-model agreement
--- 10 of 10 checkable collectives sending exactly the predicted message count --- is unremarkable
and should be: it is the control that tells you the traffic anomaly above is in the reporting and
not in the algorithms.

\section{Threats to this reading}

\textbf{A prompt defect is confounded with the treatment, though not in the way it first
appears.} Every one of the 16 implementation prompts in this run, in both rounds, contains the
line ``PUBLISHED INTERFACES OF YOUR DEPENDENCIES: (this module has no dependencies)'' ---
including the prompts for \texttt{tokens}, \texttt{functions}, \texttt{parser}, \texttt{planner},
\texttt{engine} and \texttt{api}, all of which have dependencies. Commit \texttt{f121647}, at
09:41 on the same day, replaced it with an explicit ``NOT AVAILABLE\ldots{} their authors'
published interfaces have not been shared with you in this configuration'' branch, on the stated
grounds that ``a condition that withholds information must say so; it must not misstate the
structure.'' This run finished at 09:14. In the windowed arm only the two genuinely
dependency-free modules saw that line, so the defect is present in exactly the arm under test and
absent from its control, and no arithmetic in this document can separate the two. What I can
establish, and did in the interpretation, is which part of the defect bit. The false premise
itself did not: all six affected ranks contradicted it in writing and named their real
dependencies, so nothing here rests on agents who believed they were writing leaf modules. The
part that bit is the missing prohibition on duplication, and that is a narrower and better-behaved
confound because it has a documented, greppable presence or absence in each cell and it predicts
the edge counts in all four. It remains a confound: whether these agents would have duplicated
less had the prompt said ``do not reimplement their responsibilities'' is not answerable from this
data, and only a rerun of this cell at \texttt{f121647} or later would answer it. If I am wrong
about anything in this analysis, this is the most likely place.

\textbf{The vague-spec treatment is broader than its name.} Related to the above and worth
separating from it: \texttt{apply\_vague\_layout} is documented as replacing ``the
specification's signature-bearing module table with a vague one'', and it does more than that. It
also deletes the paragraph that forbids duplicating another module's logic. Both vague cells
therefore ran without that instruction, so it is a main effect of \texttt{--vague-spec} rather
than something specific to this cell --- but it can only act where duplication is tempting, which
is where there is no channel. Anyone reading the vague pair as a clean manipulation of
specification precision alone should know that one behavioural instruction went with the
signatures.

\textbf{Two of the four cells are compromised as comparison points.} Both \texttt{real-sw} runs
executed the broken \texttt{parse\_report} and were therefore told, at both integrations, that
their build did not import. The harness treats an import failure as everyone's problem and appends
an \texttt{IMPORT} blame item to all eight ranks, so those two runs spent their entire round two
repairing a phantom, and their \texttt{agreed\_green} is false for a reason unrelated to their
code. Their token, dollar and coordination-share figures therefore include a wasted round that the
vague-shared arm did not pay --- symmetrically across the precise pair, which is why I still quote
their ratio, but it means the top row of the two-by-two is not directly commensurable with the
bottom row. The comparison I lean on is the vague pair alone: same commit (\texttt{90ca469}), same
specification, runs seven minutes apart, differing only in \texttt{shared\_interfaces}. The edge
counts are the exception, since an import graph does not care why a round was spent.

\textbf{The two vague arms are not fully independent draws.} Nine prompts are byte-identical
across the two runs and seven of the nine returned byte-identical results, with latencies
differing by up to 4.4$\times$ (\texttt{iface:nodes}: 28.6\,s here, 127.1\,s there), so the
executor re-ran them rather than serving a cache, but its sampling is close to deterministic. That
cuts both ways. It strengthens the localisation claim, because the identical leaf modules are a
genuine control rather than a coincidence, and it weakens any claim that the two runs bracket the
run-to-run variance of this population, because on identical input this population barely varies.
Nothing here estimates the variance of a re-run with a different seed, and a single run per cell
cannot.

\textbf{Wall-clock ratios are the least trustworthy of the measures, and the coordination share
inherits that.} The 2.11$\times$ wall-time ratio between the arms is contaminated by broker and
model load: the eight interface calls, whose prompts are byte-identical in both runs, summed to
221.6\,s of agent latency here and 545.7\,s in the windowed arm. If identical work can differ by
2.5$\times$ in service time between two runs eight minutes apart, no wall-time difference of that
magnitude should be attributed to the protocol. Coordination share is a ratio of rank-seconds and
so is partly protected --- a uniform slowdown cancels --- but it is a measure of \emph{dispersion}
in service time, and dispersion is exactly what fluctuating broker load also produces. The 51.5\,\%
against 44.8\,\% comparison within the vague pair should be read as consistent with the mechanism
rather than as evidence for it; the token counts, line counts and import graphs, which are
properties of what was generated rather than of when, carry the argument.

\textbf{Pass rate is a coarse and slightly generous oracle here.} Sixty cases is not a large
suite, 37 of them are attributed to \texttt{engine}, and the four DESC failures of round one are
one bug counted four times, so ``55 of 60'' overstates how close round one was in one direction
and understates the severity of a single interface mismatch in the other. More importantly, the
suite tests behaviour and not structure: a tree in which \texttt{parser.py} reimplements the lexer
and \texttt{nodes.py} is dead code passes 60 of 60 exactly as a well-composed one does. The whole
cost of the missing channel in this run is invisible to the metric the harness optimises, and is
visible only in the artefact. Any conclusion of the form ``the ablation was free because both arms
reached 60/60'' would be wrong for that reason, and the import graph is the evidence that makes it
wrong.

\textbf{Some of my numbers are not in \texttt{metrics.json}.} The pass rates for the two
precise-spec cells are oracle replays I ran, as described above; the line counts, MD5 comparisons,
import-edge counts and \texttt{getattr}/\texttt{inspect}/\texttt{Any} tallies are computed from
the generated trees in \texttt{runs/*/workspace/}; the per-phase token table is aggregated from
the \texttt{agent.call} events of each run's log; the presence and absence of the
anti-duplication clause is a grep over the runs' own \texttt{spool/*.prompt.md} files; and the
quoted concerns and review findings are from \texttt{spool/call-*.result}. Nothing in the
repository was modified to obtain them. They should be read as reproducible measurements on the
recorded artefacts rather than as harness outputs, and if the artefacts on disk were ever
overwritten by a later run the line counts would be wrong --- I checked that the modification
times of every tree I measured fall inside its own run's trace window, but that check is weaker
than a content hash recorded at the time.

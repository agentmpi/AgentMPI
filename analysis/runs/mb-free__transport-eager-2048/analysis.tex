\begin{abstract}
This is the last eager cell that completes. In a ten-cell transport sweep --- two
modes crossed with five payload sizes, all at $p = 8$ and all agent-free --- eight
ranks form a ring and each sends one 2{,}048-word payload to its successor with the
mode pinned to \texttt{Mode.EAGER}. It carried all eight messages in 34 events and
0.04\,s, and admitted 2{,}695 tokens into every receiver's context: 8.2\% of the
32{,}768-token budget, but 65.8\% of the 4{,}096-token unexpected-message credit that
actually gates the eager path. The gap between those two percentages is the whole
point of the cell. Read against the context budget, eager looks comfortable here;
read against the credit that governs admission, it has two-thirds of its headroom
gone and 1{,}401 tokens left. The next size up, 8{,}192 words at 10{,}779 tokens, is
refused outright. This is the last measurement on the safe side of a cliff whose
position the run itself gives no warning about.
\end{abstract}

\section{Reading the timeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} shows eight lanes of instantaneous marks and no bars. The
executor is \texttt{none}, there were zero agent calls and no \texttt{task.*} events,
so the ``idle fraction 100.0\%'' and ``achieved parallelism 0.00$\times$'' in the
headline table mean that no rank ever held a task --- they are facts about an
agent-free benchmark and not about the protocol. Each lane carries a
\texttt{rank.init} diamond, a \texttt{msg.send} tick, a \texttt{msg.recv} tick and a
\texttt{rank.finalize} diamond. Ranks arrive over a 23\,ms window inside a 35\,ms
span, so most of the wall clock is still eight ranks starting up against one SQLite
writer.

What distinguishes this cell visually from its smaller siblings is the spread between
send and receive. The median receive latency is 13\,ms and the maximum is 27\,ms,
against 4\,ms and 10\,ms at 128 words. No rank is waiting on a peer for any of it.
The interval a latency measures starts when the sender stamps \texttt{sent\_at}, and
what falls inside it are two payload-sized costs on the receiver's side: its own
outgoing 10\,KiB blob write, because every rank sends before it receives, and then
the \texttt{blobs.get} that materialises the arriving payload, which
\texttt{\_deliver} performs before it emits \texttt{msg.recv}. Both scale with the
payload, and the run span scales with them, from 19\,ms at 128 words to 35\,ms here.
Nothing blocks, though. There is no
\texttt{transport.credit\_stall} event anywhere in the log, because the ring gives
each destination exactly one inbound sender and \texttt{\_await\_eager\_credit}
therefore always finds zero outstanding queued tokens. The rank health table reads 8\%
occupancy across all eight ranks --- the largest context footprint anywhere in this
sweep, since the two eager cells above this one admitted nothing at all.
Figure~\ref{fig:comm} shows the ring as a single off-diagonal band of 2{,}695 tokens
per edge, the last time in the eager column that a communication matrix exists to be
drawn.

\section{Interpretation}

By construction: eight ranks, a ring, one send and one receive each, mode forced. The
forcing matters here more than anywhere else in the eager column. Every
\texttt{rank.init} records \texttt{eager\_limit: 1000000000}, which disables
\texttt{\_choose\_mode}'s automatic threshold. That threshold defaults to
\texttt{DEFAULT\_EAGER\_LIMIT} = 2048 \emph{tokens}, and this payload is 2{,}695
tokens, so under the shipped default the runtime would already have converted this
send to rendezvous. This cell is thus the first in the sweep where the harness is
overriding what AgentMPI would have done on its own, and it still succeeds --- the
default eager limit is conservative relative to the 4{,}096-token credit that
actually enforces anything.

What emerged is how little warning the successful case gives. The generated finding
list is empty; the job outcome is ``completed''; every rank finalised with zero
rejections and 8.2\% occupancy. Nothing in this run's own record indicates that it is
close to a limit, because the limit is not the context budget it reports occupancy
against. The binding resource is the destination's \texttt{unexpected\_limit}, fixed
at 4{,}096 tokens by the benchmark, and \texttt{\_await\_eager\_credit} checks a
single payload against it before doing anything else: \texttt{if n\_tokens > limit:
raise AmpiContextOverflow}. At 2{,}695 tokens this cell passes with 1{,}401 to spare,
34\% of the credit. Its successor at 8{,}192 words prices at 10{,}779 tokens, fails
that check, and its trace contains no \texttt{msg.send} at all --- 18 events instead
of 34, an empty \texttt{messages} table in the fabric, and \texttt{completed: false}
with \texttt{ERR\_CONTEXT\_OVERFLOW} in \texttt{results/microbench/free-transport.json}.
Since the charge is linear at $5/3.8 = 1.3158$ tokens per word with no intercept
(168, 674 and 2{,}695 tokens for 128, 512 and 2{,}048 words), the crossing is at
3{,}113 words. This cell is at 66\% of the way there and reports itself healthy. That
is the argument for making transport mode explicit: a harness cannot infer from a
successful eager run how much eager headroom it has left, because the successful run
reports occupancy against the wrong budget.

Against its rendezvous twin, this cell is the last place in the sweep where the two
modes are genuinely comparable. \texttt{mb-free\_\_transport-rendezvous-2048} moves
the identical blob in the identical 34 events and reports zero admissions; this one
reports 2{,}695 tokens per rank. At 8.2\% of budget the eager charge is affordable
and the receiver gets the content, whereas the rendezvous run's receivers finish
holding a handle they never opened. Above this size that comparison stops being
available, because the eager arm no longer runs. Everything the sweep says about
rendezvous being necessary rather than merely available rests on the two cells above
this one, and everything it says about eager being adequate rests on this one and the
two below.

\section{Threats to this reading}

The context figures are admissions rather than usage: with \texttt{executor: none}
and no agent calls, 2{,}695 tokens were charged to a budget nothing read, so this run
measures the protocol's accounting and not any agent's behaviour. The token count is
an estimate (\texttt{tokenizer\_exact: false}) produced by a character heuristic on a
payload of one repeated word; a real tokeniser would price this text nearer 2{,}048
tokens, which would put it at 50\% rather than 66\% of credit and move the sweep's
ceiling from 3{,}113 words to roughly 4{,}096. The ceiling's existence does not
depend on that, but its position in this document does, and no cell in the sweep
brackets it more tightly than the factor of four between 2{,}048 and 8{,}192 words.
The wall time is one sample --- 35\,ms, of which 23\,ms is rank start-up --- so the
observation that latencies are larger here than at 128 words is suggestive of blob
write cost but is not cleanly separable from start-up jitter at this scale. Finally,
the claim that this cell is ``close to the limit'' is a claim about a per-message
precondition, not about buffering. The ring hands each receiver exactly one message,
so the accumulation path that \texttt{\_await\_eager\_credit} spends most of its code
on --- polling outstanding queued tokens, emitting \texttt{transport.credit\_stall},
blocking until credit frees --- is never entered in any of these ten runs. A harness
that fanned two of these 2{,}695-token messages into one rank would exceed the same
4{,}096-token credit by accumulation and would find out through a stall rather than a
refusal, and that experiment is absent here.

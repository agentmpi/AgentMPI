\begin{abstract}
This is the \texttt{PROCEED} point of the four-policy barrier-failure sweep in
\texttt{bench\_faults} (\texttt{experiments/microbench.py}): eight ranks, rank~3 returns
immediately to simulate a crashed agent session, and the seven survivors enter a barrier
with a 15\,s deadline. \texttt{PROCEED} is the \emph{partial barrier} --- continue with
whoever arrived, report the absentees, leave the communicator alone --- and this run is
the cleanest of the four by a wide margin. Thirty-nine events, 15.217\,s, one repeated
three-event sequence (\texttt{barrier.timeout}, \texttt{ft.declare\_failed},
\texttt{coll.barrier}) executed independently by each of the seven survivors, and then
the job ends. All seven agree on \texttt{absent:\ [3]} within 18.8\,ms of one another,
which is why the benchmark records \texttt{detected\_correctly:\ true} and
\texttt{all\_completed:\ true} with a median detection latency of 15.195\,s. Nothing
unexpected happens, and that is the result: of the four policies, \texttt{PROCEED} is the
only one that correctly names the dead rank without invoking any machinery that can then
misbehave. Its sibling \texttt{SHRINK} detects identically and then spends a further
60.2\,s in a broken agreement; \texttt{RAISE} and \texttt{WAIT} record no failure at all
and blame the wrong rank. The single thing to take away is the division of labour that
makes this work: \texttt{PROCEED} buys \emph{knowledge} --- a named absentee and a durable
failure record --- and deliberately defers the \emph{repair} to the harness. The
communicator ends the run at generation~0 with all eight members still marked
\texttt{active}, so a second collective would wait for rank~3 all over again. That is a
liability, but it is a bounded, honest and repeatable one, which is more than the other
three policies can claim.
\end{abstract}

\section{Reading the timeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} is a single vertical column of red at the right-hand edge and
nothing else. Seven of the eight lanes carry one mark at $t\approx15.2$\,s; rank~3's lane
carries only its lifecycle ticks at $t\approx0$. There are no bars anywhere, because there
is no work anywhere: \texttt{metrics.json} records \texttt{executors:\ \{none: 8\}}, zero
agent calls, zero tokens and \$0.0000. The headline table's \emph{achieved parallelism
$0.00\times$} and \emph{idle fraction 100.0\%} are consequences of the benchmark's design
and not findings about the protocol --- \texttt{bench\_faults}'s \texttt{rank\_main} calls
\texttt{comm.barrier} and nothing else, so a rank has nothing to be busy with. Read those
two rows as ``not applicable''.

The same caution applies to the wall clock. 15.217\,s is
\texttt{--detect-timeout} (15.0\,s, recorded in
\texttt{results/microbench/free-faults.json}) plus 217\,ms of poll granularity and SQLite
write latency. The run measures a constant, and the residual measures the fabric.

Rank~3's empty lane is the injected fault in its entirety. It initialised at $t=0.006$\,s
and finalised at the same millisecond, because \texttt{rank\_main} returns
\texttt{\{"role":\ "victim"\}} before touching the communicator. From the survivors' point
of view this is indistinguishable from an agent session that died before its first call,
which is the point.

The interesting structure is inside the 18.8\,ms width of that single column, and the
viewer resolves it better than the figure does. The seven \texttt{barrier.timeout} events
run from 15.198\,s (rank~0) to 15.216\,s (rank~4), and each rank then runs
\texttt{ft.declare\_failed}, \texttt{coll.barrier} and \texttt{rank.finalize} as one
contiguous group in the log. Two things are visible in the millisecond timings. First,
detection order is not completion order: rank~7 timed out at 15.199\,s, third earliest,
but did not get its declaration written until 15.203\,s, behind ranks 2 and 5 --- a 4\,ms
gap where every other rank's was under 1\,ms. The declaration is an \texttt{INSERT} plus
an \texttt{UPDATE} inside one \texttt{fabric.write()} transaction, so what that gap shows
is the write lock queue. Second, the collective table records the arrival spread as
\emph{skew 0.02\,s}, which is small absolutely but is 7$\times$ the 2.7\,ms spread in the
\texttt{SHRINK} sibling. The likeliest reading is entry skew propagated forward rather
than detection jitter: a central barrier measures its deadline from each rank's own entry,
and rank~4 both entered last (\texttt{rank.init} at $t=0.022$\,s, against 0.001\,s for
rank~0) and timed out last, 18\,ms behind.

The viewer's stats row reads \textsc{failures 7}, \textsc{messages 0}, and its legend
counts \emph{rank lifecycle 16, failure / timeout 14} with no third entry. The absence of
the violet recovery colour is the visual signature of this policy: seven detections, zero
recovery actions. Its \textsc{collectives} panel lists \texttt{barrier}, 7 invocations,
algorithm \texttt{central}, 0 messages --- a collective that coordinated eight ranks
without sending anything, because the central algorithm coordinates through
\texttt{coll\_parts} rows rather than through the message layer. Reproducing the barrier's
20\,ms-to-250\,ms poll back-off arithmetically gives about 70 polls per rank over 15\,s,
so roughly 490 \texttt{SELECT} statements crossed the fabric during the stretch of
Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} that looks entirely empty. That, and not any protocol cost, is
what the wall clock's residual is made of.

The rank health table shows all eight ranks \texttt{finalized}, \texttt{alive: no},
\texttt{suspected: --}. The empty \texttt{suspected} column for rank~3 is worth noticing
rather than glossing: it reflects \texttt{ft.health}'s lease-based detector, which this
run never calls, not the \texttt{failures} table, which does hold rank~3.

\section{Interpretation}

\subsection{What is true by construction}

Almost the whole shape of this run was fixed before it started, and separating that from
what emerged is most of the interpretive work.

The victim, the population size and the deadline are configuration:
\texttt{victims: "3"}, \texttt{ranks: 8}, \texttt{detect\_timeout: 15.0}. The algorithm is
forced rather than chosen --- \texttt{algorithms.barrier} overrides the caller's
\texttt{dissemination} default with \texttt{central} whenever the policy is
\texttt{PROCEED}, \texttt{SHRINK} or \texttt{REVOKE}, because only the arrival-counting
algorithm can name who did not arrive. Everything downstream follows: no messages, no
communication matrix figure, \texttt{eager\_messages: 0}, and coordination conducted
entirely through fabric rows. The barrier's \texttt{rounds: 0} against
\texttt{predicted 2} in the collective table is a logging gap and not a disagreement:
\texttt{algorithms.barrier} sets \texttt{rounds = 2} locally for the central path and
returns it in \texttt{BarrierResult}, but never writes it onto the \texttt{\_Tracker}'s
\texttt{CollStats}, so the emitted \texttt{coll.barrier} always carries zero. The tooling
declines to score it (\modelna{}), which is right.

What emerged is narrower and worth stating precisely, because it is the run's actual
content: seven independently polling processes, with no leader and no communication
between them, converged on the same absentee set within 18.8\,ms and each recorded it.
The consistency is not enforced anywhere. Each rank computes
\texttt{absent = set(range(comm.size)) - arrived} from its own \texttt{SELECT}, and they
agree because the fabric is a single serialising point of truth about arrivals. That is
the design's central bet --- route the hard part through a reliable shared service ---
and this run is a small, clean confirmation of it.

\subsection{The mechanism: detection with a deliberately empty mitigation}

\texttt{\_central\_barrier}'s deadline branch is common to \texttt{PROCEED},
\texttt{SHRINK} and \texttt{REVOKE}: emit \texttt{barrier.timeout}, then call
\texttt{ft.declare\_failed} for each absentee. The three policies then diverge, and
\texttt{PROCEED} is the branch that does nothing further --- it falls past the
\texttt{REVOKE} and \texttt{SHRINK} tests and returns the absentee tuple. The barrier
therefore returns \emph{normally}, with a \texttt{BarrierResult} whose \texttt{absent} is
\texttt{(3,)} and whose \texttt{complete} property is \texttt{False}, and the caller
decides what that means. This is why the benchmark records \texttt{all\_completed: true}
and an empty \texttt{example\_error} for this row: no rank raised.

The seven \texttt{ft.declare\_failed} events reduce to one row in the fabric. The
\texttt{INSERT} into \texttt{failures} carries
\texttt{ON CONFLICT(ctx, rank, kind) DO NOTHING}, and querying
\texttt{runs/mb-free/faults-proceed/fabric.sqlite} shows exactly one row: \texttt{rank 3},
\texttt{fail\_stop}, \texttt{detail: "barrier phase"}. The \texttt{fabric.emit} call sits
outside the conflict clause and fires unconditionally, so the event count is the number of
\emph{observers} and the row count is the number of \emph{failures}. Seven observers, one
failure. That is defensible for an event log, but it means the viewer's
\textsc{failures} tile of 7 and \texttt{metrics.json}'s \texttt{summary.failures: 7} both
read as seven dead ranks when one rank died, and neither artefact says which it means.
The \texttt{SHRINK} sibling makes the same arithmetic considerably worse, reaching 14
declarations for the same single failure.

\subsection{What the policy costs: a repair that was never attempted}

The fabric's end state is the most informative thing in this run, and it is invisible in
every generated artefact. After the barrier, \texttt{comms.generation} is still
\texttt{0}, \texttt{comms.revoked} is still \texttt{0}, and all eight \texttt{comm\_members}
rows --- rank~3 included --- are still \texttt{state = 'active'}. The communicator is
bit-for-bit what it was before the failure, apart from one row in a side table.

That is exactly what the \texttt{BarrierPolicy.PROCEED} docstring promises: ``continue
with whatever contributions arrived, leaving the communicator intact and reporting the
absentees.'' The liability is the direct consequence. \texttt{\_central\_barrier}'s
completion test is \texttt{len(arrived) >= comm.size}, and \texttt{comm.size} is
\texttt{len(self.\_members)}, which counts rank~3. A second barrier on this communicator
would therefore wait the full timeout again and declare rank~3 failed again, and a
message-passing collective --- a \texttt{reduce}, say, whose default timeout is 1{,}800\,s
--- would block on rank~3 for half an hour. A harness that calls a \texttt{PROCEED}
barrier in a loop and never acts on the returned \texttt{absent} list pays 15\,s per
iteration forever.

I do not read this as a defect. It is a division of labour, and I think it is the right
one: naming the absentee is something only the runtime can do, and deciding whether a
seven-of-eight glossary is worth having is a domain judgement the protocol cannot make.
The failure record is durable and machine-readable, so \texttt{ft.get\_failed} will return
\texttt{(3,)} to any later call and \texttt{ft.shrink}'s agreement step has the input it
needs. What \texttt{PROCEED} guarantees is that the harness is \emph{able} to decide;
whether it does is not the barrier's problem.

It is worth contrasting this with what \texttt{SHRINK} does with the same information,
because the comparison is the sweep's main product. \texttt{SHRINK} takes the extra step
of calling \texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place}, and in that trace the extra step is where
everything breaks: seven concurrent \texttt{UPDATE comms SET generation = generation + 1}
statements drive the generation from 0 to 7, the seven survivors cache six different
values, and the \texttt{agree} that follows splits across six separate collectives and
never reaches quorum. \texttt{PROCEED} pays 15.2\,s and gets a correct answer;
\texttt{SHRINK} pays 75.4\,s and gets a correct answer plus seven false failure
declarations and an inconsistent agreement. The extra 60\,s buys nothing.

\subsection{The flagged findings}

Three findings are generated and all three are expected for this configuration.

\emph{``\texttt{barrier/central} (label \texttt{phase}) was reached by 7 of 8 ranks, so it
never completed.''} This is the experiment, not a defect. \texttt{bench\_faults} kills
rank~3 on purpose precisely so that the barrier cannot complete; the policy under test is
defined entirely by what happens at that moment, and a run of this benchmark in which the
barrier \emph{did} complete would be the broken one. The finding is also why the collective
table shows \modelna{} and \texttt{model\_checks\_total} is 0:
\texttt{analysis.py} gates its cost-model comparison on \texttt{n\_participants >= size}
and correctly refuses to score a closed form against an invocation that did not happen.

\emph{``The coordination figures count time inside \emph{completed} collectives only\ldots
the reported share is a floor.''} Expected, and unusually tight here. The numerator behind
\emph{coordination share 87.4\%} is 106.365 rank-seconds, which is $7 \times 15.195$\,s
--- the seven survivors' barrier waits and nothing else. The only blocking the metric
misses is rank~3's, which is zero, so on this run the floor is nearly the true value.
That is a useful calibration point for the flag itself: it fires whenever a run has an
incomplete collective, regardless of how much is actually being missed, and here it is
formally correct but practically almost tight. In the \texttt{SHRINK} sibling the same
flag hides four times the recorded blocking, and in \texttt{real-tr-p16-full} it hides a
factor of about 4{,}500. The flag says ``this is a floor''; it does not say how loose, and
the three runs together show the range is enormous.

\emph{``Collectives account for 87.4\% of the run's rank-seconds, so more of the pool's
time went to coordination than to work.''} Arithmetically correct and semantically empty.
There was no work: zero agent calls, zero tokens. Any nonzero coordination figure would
have exceeded the work figure. The 87.4\% is worth citing only as a check that the run is
what it claims to be --- essentially all of it was one barrier, and
\emph{coordination in flight} of 99.98\% says the same thing about wall clock. Read as a
harness-efficiency finding it would be badly wrong, and it is the one flagged item here
that a careless reader could misuse.

\subsection{Against its siblings, and what a harness author should do}

The four policies are the same program with one enum changed, so the differences between
their traces are attributable to the policy and to nothing else. Set out that way the
ordering is stark:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrl@{}}
\toprule
policy & barrier algorithm & events & wall (s) & \texttt{ft.declare\_failed} & absentee correctly named \\
\midrule
\texttt{wait}    & dissemination & 45 & 15.26 & 0  & no \\
\texttt{raise}   & dissemination & 45 & 15.27 & 0  & no \\
\texttt{proceed} & central       & 39 & 15.22 & 7  & yes \\
\texttt{shrink}  & central       & 60 & 75.43 & 14 & yes, then seven false ones \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\normalsize
\end{center}

\texttt{WAIT} and \texttt{RAISE} are indistinguishable from each other and diagnostically
dead. Neither is in the list that switches the algorithm to \texttt{central}, so both run
the \texttt{dissemination} barrier, which cannot name absentees. Their traces contain no
\texttt{barrier.timeout}, no \texttt{ft.*} and no \texttt{coll.*} events; their fabrics'
\texttt{failures} tables are empty; and both fail with
\texttt{ERR\_TIMEOUT: receive timed out [\ldots source=4 \ldots]}, naming a live rank
rather than the dead one, because in a dissemination barrier at $p=8$ the rank that
notices is three rounds removed from the rank that died. The benchmark scores both
\texttt{detected\_correctly: false}.

\textbf{The recommendation is \texttt{PROCEED} wherever a phase's input can legitimately
be partial, and \texttt{REVOKE} plus an explicit out-of-place \texttt{ft.shrink} wherever
it cannot --- and not the \texttt{SHRINK} barrier policy, until the generation increment
in \texttt{shrink\_in\_place} is fixed.} This run is the direct evidence for the first
half. It is the cheapest correct outcome in the sweep on every axis: fewest events,
shortest wall clock, one failure row, no side effects the harness did not ask for, and an
\texttt{absent} list handed back through a normal return. \texttt{REVOKE} is untested
here, which is a genuine gap in the evidence, but it is the only escalating policy that
does not route through \texttt{shrink\_in\_place}: it writes an absolute
\texttt{revoked = 1}, which is idempotent under concurrent execution, and then raises,
leaving the harness to call \texttt{ft.shrink} --- the sibling that elects a leader and
publishes one agreed context.

The archive supplies the counterexample that shows why this matters and, awkwardly, also
shows that \texttt{PROCEED} alone is not sufficient. \texttt{real-tr-p16-full} is the only
genuine failure among the 500 runs: sixteen ranks, all sixteen in \texttt{failed\_ranks},
\texttt{ok: false}, 7{,}200\,s of wall clock, \$0.0726 spent and no output. Six rank roles
were never bound to a worker process; the ten that worked correctly died waiting inside a
glossary \texttt{allreduce} that could never fold. Its \texttt{failures} table has zero
rows and its log has zero \texttt{ft.declare\_failed} events --- nothing was ever declared,
so \texttt{shrink}'s agreement step had no input and the harness had nothing to act on. Its
own analysis concludes that a shrink at $t\approx190$\,s over the ten ranks that actually
had executors would have finished the job in minutes. (Ten, not twelve: twelve is the
number of ranks that emitted a \texttt{coll.reduce} record, which is a different set from
the set that could do work.) The fifteen seconds in the present run are exactly the
affordance that harness lacked.

But that harness \emph{did} use \texttt{PROCEED}. It set the policy on a barrier in
phase~5, downstream of the phase-2 \texttt{allreduce} it never survived to reach, and
that placement is the whole difference. Reading the signatures in
\texttt{src/agentmpi/algorithms.py}, \texttt{policy} is a parameter of \texttt{barrier}
and of nothing else: \texttt{bcast}, \texttt{scatter}, \texttt{gather},
\texttt{allgather}, \texttt{reduce}, \texttt{allreduce} and \texttt{reduce\_scatter} take
a \texttt{timeout} and no policy at all. So no choice among these four names would have
saved that run; the harness would have had to place a policy-bearing barrier
\emph{before} the allreduce, or the protocol would have to extend the policy to the other
collectives. The lesson this sweep contributes is therefore not simply ``choose
\texttt{PROCEED}'' but that a failure policy protects only the operation it is attached
to, and that in AgentMPI today exactly one operation can carry one.

\section{Threats to this reading}

\textbf{The injected fault is gentler than a crash in one specific, checkable way.} Rank~3
returns normally, so it reaches \texttt{rank.finalize} at $t=0.006$\,s and its
\texttt{ranks} row is \texttt{finalized} for the rest of the run.
\texttt{ft.declare\_failed}'s state update is guarded by
\texttt{state NOT IN (FINALIZED, EXCLUDED)}, so when the survivors declared rank~3 dead
at $t=15.2$\,s the guard skipped it: the fabric's \texttt{ranks} table ends the run with
rank~3 \texttt{finalized}, not \texttt{failed}. A genuinely crashed agent would have been
left \texttt{running} and the update would have fired. Nothing in this run's conclusions
depends on that state --- the \texttt{failures} row was written either way, and it is the
\texttt{failures} table that \texttt{get\_failed}, \texttt{agree} and
\texttt{\_agree\_survivors} consult --- but a reader checking the rank table for evidence
of the failure will not find it, and any harness that keys recovery off
\texttt{RankState.FAILED} rather than off the failure records would see nothing here.

\textbf{There are no agents in this run and nothing in it bears on agent behaviour.}
\texttt{executors: \{none: 8\}}, zero agent calls, zero tokens, \$0. The failure modelled
is a rank that returns instantly, which is \texttt{FAIL\_STOP} and only
\texttt{FAIL\_STOP}. \texttt{src/agentmpi/ft.py}'s own taxonomy is explicit that the class
that matters for agent systems is \texttt{FAIL\_PLAUSIBLE} --- a rank that returns a
well-formed, confident, wrong answer --- and that no amount of deadline tuning detects it.
A \texttt{PROCEED} barrier would let such a rank through untouched, because it arrives.
These four runs exercise one row of a six-row table, and a reader taking them as the
evidence for the project's failure-model claims should scope them accordingly.

\textbf{The detection latency is a restatement of the deadline.}
\texttt{detect\_p50\_s: 15.195} against a configured 15.0\,s is the timeout plus 195\,ms
of poll granularity and fabric write latency. It is not a measurement of how quickly
AgentMPI can notice a failure, because the only detector exercised is the barrier's own
deadline. The lease-based detector that \texttt{ft.py} presents as \emph{the} failure
detector, \texttt{ft.health}, is never called in this run at all: the only caller is
\texttt{\_agree\_survivors}, which \texttt{PROCEED} does not reach. Nothing here supports
a detection-latency claim, and in particular nothing here says what would happen with a
detect timeout short enough to race a slow-but-live rank.

\textbf{The claim that a second collective would repeat the wait is read from the source,
not observed.} \texttt{rank\_main} runs exactly one barrier, so this trace cannot show it.
The inference is short --- \texttt{comm\_members} still reads all-active,
\texttt{comm.size} counts all members, and \texttt{\_central\_barrier} compares against
\texttt{comm.size} --- but it is an inference, and a two-barrier variant of this benchmark
would settle it in thirty seconds. The same experiment would settle the parallel question
for \texttt{SHRINK}, where the situation is arguably worse: that policy \emph{does} mark
rank~3's member row \texttt{failed}, but \texttt{Communicator.\_active}, the tuple that
records the distinction, is assigned in \texttt{refresh()} and never read anywhere in
\texttt{src/agentmpi}.

\textbf{The 18.8\,ms skew attribution is plausible rather than proven.} I read it as entry
skew propagated forward, on the grounds that rank~4 was both the last to initialise
($t=0.022$\,s) and the last to time out ($t=15.216$\,s), and that a central barrier's
deadline runs from each rank's own entry. The two orderings agree in six of seven
positions --- ranks 5 and 6 swap --- which is consistent but is seven samples spanning
18.8\,ms, and contention on the fabric's write lock is an equally good explanation for
part of the spread, as rank~7's 4\,ms detection-to-declaration gap shows directly. The
distinction does not affect anything else in this reading.

\textbf{$n=1$, but the parts that matter are structural rather than statistical.} A rerun
would shuffle the millisecond ordering of the seven detections and move the skew. It would
not change the event vocabulary, the one-row-seven-events asymmetry, the untouched
communicator state, or the algorithm substitution, all of which are properties of the code
rather than of this schedule. Where I have relied on a specific number --- 18.8\,ms,
15.195\,s --- I have tried to say what it is a measurement \emph{of}, and in both cases
the answer is the fabric and the configured deadline, not the protocol.

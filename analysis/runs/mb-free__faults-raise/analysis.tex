\begin{abstract}
This is the \texttt{RAISE} point of the four-policy barrier-failure sweep in
\texttt{bench\_faults} (\texttt{experiments/microbench.py}): eight ranks, rank~3 returns
immediately to simulate a crashed agent session, and the seven survivors enter a barrier
with a 15\,s deadline. \texttt{RAISE} is the default \texttt{BarrierPolicy} and is
supposed to raise \texttt{AmpiTimeout} at the deadline. What the trace shows is that the
policy was never consulted. \texttt{algorithms.barrier} switches to the arrival-counting
\texttt{central} algorithm only for \texttt{PROCEED}, \texttt{SHRINK} and \texttt{REVOKE};
\texttt{RAISE} keeps the \texttt{dissemination} default, and the dissemination barrier
has no deadline branch of its own --- it simply threads the caller's timeout into every
send and receive. So no \texttt{barrier.timeout} was emitted, no
\texttt{ft.declare\_failed} was recorded, no \texttt{coll.barrier} was logged, and the
run's fabric ends with an empty \texttt{failures} table and all eight members
\texttt{active}. What did happen is a three-round collapse: of the 24 messages a
dissemination barrier needs at $p=8$, only 17 were sent and only 10 were received, and
the seven survivors blocked at staggered depths in the recursion --- rank~4 after one
send, ranks 5 and 6 after two, ranks 0, 1, 2 and 7 after three. Every one of them then
raised the identical error, \texttt{ERR\_TIMEOUT: receive timed out [\ldots source=4
\ldots]}, naming a rank that was alive. The single most important thing to take away is
the gap between the run and its own instrumentation: the benchmark scores this row
\texttt{detected\_correctly:\ false}, and yet \texttt{metrics.json} reports
\texttt{degraded:\ false}, \texttt{failed\_ranks:\ []} and \texttt{failures:\ 0}, the
generated findings list reads ``nothing anomalous was detected mechanically'', and the
viewer's \textsc{failures} tile reads \textbf{0}. Of the four policies this is the one
that failed hardest, and it is the one whose dashboard looks healthiest.
\end{abstract}

\section{Reading the timeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} has two vertical columns and nothing between them: a green
cluster hard against $t=0$ and a grey column at $t\approx15.25$\,s. The green marks are
the barrier's messages, all 17 of which were sent inside the first 32\,ms; the grey marks
are \texttt{rank.finalize}. There are no bars, because there is no work.
\texttt{metrics.json} records \texttt{executors: \{none: 8\}}, zero agent calls, zero
tokens and \$0.0000, so the headline table's \emph{achieved parallelism $0.00\times$} and
\emph{idle fraction 100.0\%} describe the benchmark's design and are not findings about
the protocol. \texttt{bench\_faults}'s \texttt{rank\_main} calls \texttt{comm.barrier} and
nothing else; a rank with no task cannot have a busy fraction. Read both rows as ``not
applicable''. The 15.271\,s wall clock is likewise a constant: it is
\texttt{--detect-timeout} (15.0\,s, from \texttt{results/microbench/free-faults.json})
plus 271\,ms of process start-up and SQLite writes.

Rank~3's lane holds a single tick at the far left --- \texttt{rank.init} at $t=0.007$\,s
and \texttt{rank.finalize} at $t=0.010$\,s, three milliseconds apart --- and then nothing.
That is the injected fault in its entirety.

The interesting structure is compressed into the first 32\,ms and is invisible at the
figure's scale, so the per-rank table in \texttt{generated.tex} is the better place to
read it. Its \emph{sent} and \emph{recv} columns are not uniform, and the pattern is
exact:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrl@{}}
\toprule
rank & sent & recv & where it stopped \\
\midrule
3 & 0 & 0 & the victim; never entered the barrier \\
4 & 1 & 0 & blocked in round 0, waiting on rank~3 \\
5 & 2 & 1 & blocked in round 1, waiting on rank~3 \\
6 & 2 & 1 & blocked in round 1, waiting on rank~4 \\
0, 1, 2, 7 & 3 & 2 & blocked in round 2, waiting on ranks 4, 5, 6 and 3 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\normalsize
\end{center}

A dissemination barrier at $p=8$ runs $\lceil \log_2 8 \rceil = 3$ rounds; in round $k$
rank $r$ sends to $(r + 2^k) \bmod 8$ and receives from $(r - 2^k) \bmod 8$. Rank~4's
round-0 source is rank~3, so rank~4 sends once and then blocks immediately. Rank~5's
round-1 source is rank~3 and rank~6's is rank~4 --- already stalled --- so both get two
sends in. The remaining four complete two full rounds and block in round~2 on sources 4,
5, 6 and 3 respectively. The death of one rank propagates to the entire population in
exactly the three rounds the algorithm takes, which is the same logarithmic structure
that makes the barrier cheap. Summing the column gives $7 + 6 + 4 = 17$ sends against the
24 a complete barrier would need, and 10 receives.

Figure~\ref{fig:comm} shows the same thing spatially and is the clearest single artefact
in the run. Row~3 is empty across its whole width: the dead rank sent nothing. Rows 0, 1
and 2 each carry three cells, rows 5 and 6 two, row~4 one --- the staggered blocking
depth, read off as row length. Column~3 carries three cells, from ranks 2, 1 and 7: three
messages delivered into a dead rank's mailbox, one per round. Seven of the seventeen
messages were never received. Three went to rank~3 and four went to ranks 4, 5 and 6,
which were alive but had already stopped reading. Nothing in the run notices either
category.

The viewer confirms the reading and adds the punchline. Its stats row shows
\textsc{messages 17} and \textsc{failures 0}. There is no \textsc{collectives} panel at
all, because no \texttt{coll.*} event was ever emitted. Its rank table lists all eight
ranks as \texttt{finalized}, \texttt{alive: no}, \texttt{suspected: --}, with rank~3
indistinguishable from the seven that outlived it by fifteen seconds. The legend counts
\emph{messages 27, rank lifecycle 16} and offers no failure or recovery colour, so the
dashboard for this run renders in green and grey only.

\section{Interpretation}

\subsection{The policy was never applied}

The central fact is a control-flow one and it is worth tracing precisely, because the run
name promises something the run does not contain.

\texttt{algorithms.barrier} begins:

\begin{center}
\texttt{if policy in (BarrierPolicy.PROCEED, BarrierPolicy.SHRINK, BarrierPolicy.REVOKE):}\\
\texttt{\hspace*{2em}algorithm = "central"}
\end{center}

\noindent\texttt{RAISE} is not in that tuple, so \texttt{algorithm} keeps the signature's
\texttt{"dissemination"} default that \texttt{bench\_faults} did not override. The
dissemination branch is a plain loop of \texttt{comm.\_csend} and \texttt{comm.\_crecv}
calls with \texttt{timeout=timeout} threaded through, and it contains no reference to
\texttt{policy} whatsoever. The only code that reads the policy is
\texttt{\_central\_barrier}, which this run never enters. So the
\texttt{AmpiTimeout} that every survivor raised came from
\texttt{Communicator.recv}'s own deadline, not from the barrier's policy branch, and the
run would have been byte-for-byte the same had the policy argument been omitted.

The trace proves this rather than merely being consistent with it. If
\texttt{\_central\_barrier} had run, it would have emitted \texttt{barrier.timeout} before
raising --- the emit precedes the \texttt{raise} in the source --- and it would have
called \texttt{\_record\_collective}, leaving a row in the fabric's \texttt{collectives}
table. Querying \texttt{runs/mb-free/faults-raise/fabric.sqlite} shows that table
\emph{empty}, alongside an empty \texttt{failures} table,
\texttt{comms.generation = 0}, \texttt{comms.revoked = 0}, and all eight
\texttt{comm\_members} rows \texttt{active}. The communicator ended the run in exactly the
state it started in.

There is a further consequence that makes \texttt{RAISE} and \texttt{WAIT} synonyms.
Even on the path where the policy \emph{is} read, the two share a branch:
\texttt{if policy is BarrierPolicy.RAISE or policy is BarrierPolicy.WAIT: raise
AmpiTimeout(\ldots)}. Grepping \texttt{src/agentmpi} finds
\texttt{BarrierPolicy.WAIT} exactly once, in that line. So there is no code path anywhere
in AgentMPI on which \texttt{RAISE} and \texttt{WAIT} behave differently, and the two runs
in this sweep bear it out: \texttt{mb-free\_\_faults-wait} has the same 45 events, the same
17-send and 10-receive counts, the same per-rank profile and an identical 17-edge
communication matrix, differing only in the millisecond ordering and 11\,ms of wall clock.
The sweep's four policies produce three distinct behaviours.

\subsection{What the run does establish}

Read as a measurement of MPI-like semantics rather than of a policy, this run is
informative, and I would argue it is the sweep's most important negative result.

\emph{One dead rank at $p=8$ costs all seven survivors.} The blocking is total, not
partial, and the trace shows the propagation path explicitly. This is the pathology
\texttt{algorithms.barrier}'s docstring names --- ``the probability that all $p$ members
arrive within any fixed window falls off with $p$'' --- observed at the smallest
population where it has room to develop. It also generalises badly: at $p=8$ the failure
reaches everyone in 3 rounds, and the round count is $\lceil \log_2 p \rceil$, so a larger
population does not dilute the damage, it merely delays it by a round or two.

\emph{The timeout is bounded and honoured, and that is genuinely worth something.} Every
survivor finalised between 15.254\,s and 15.270\,s. Nothing hung. Every wait in this trace
is the value of a named constant, which is the property \texttt{real-tr-p16-full}'s
analysis credits the protocol with and which is not free --- an MPI barrier with a dead
peer hangs forever.

\emph{But the diagnosis is wrong, and wrong in a specific and damaging way.} The recorded
error is
\texttt{ERR\_TIMEOUT: receive timed out [ctx=0 rank=0 source=4 tag='\_ampi:barrier:0:1:2'
waited\_s=15.0]}. Rank~0's round-2 source is $(0 - 4) \bmod 8 = 4$, and rank~4 was alive
throughout --- it finalised normally at $t=15.254$\,s. The error names an innocent rank,
and it must, because a dissemination barrier gives each rank visibility of exactly three
peers and rank~3 is not one of rank~0's. The benchmark's
\texttt{detected\_correctly} field encodes this directly: it compares each survivor's
\texttt{detected} list against the victim list \texttt{[3]}, and because the fallback
\texttt{ampi.get\_failed(comm)} returns an empty tuple against an empty
\texttt{failures} table, every survivor reports an empty list. The published row reads
\texttt{"detected\_correctly": false}.

The practical weight of this is that the failure is not merely undiagnosed but
\emph{unrecoverable-from}. \texttt{ft.shrink} begins with \texttt{\_agree\_survivors},
which computes the survivor set from \texttt{get\_failed} --- empty here --- unioned with
whatever \texttt{ft.health}'s lease detector suspects; \texttt{ft.agree} computes
\texttt{expected} the same way. With an empty \texttt{failures} table, both would
reconstruct a group that still contains rank~3 and block on it again. A harness that
catches the \texttt{AmpiTimeout} from a \texttt{RAISE} barrier and tries to recover has
nothing to recover with.

\subsection{The flagged findings, and the one that is missing}

The generated list says: \emph{``Nothing anomalous was detected mechanically.''} That is
the finding worth discussing, because it is true as defined and badly wrong as read.

The mechanical checks fire on incomplete collectives, on cost-model disagreements, on
failed ranks reported by \texttt{job.finish}, and on \texttt{ok: false}. This run trips
none of them, and each miss has the same root cause: \emph{nothing was recorded}.
\texttt{Analysis.degraded} is
\texttt{bool(failed\_ranks) or ok is False or bool(incomplete\_collectives)}. There are no
incomplete collectives because there are no collectives --- \texttt{n\_collectives: 0},
and the collective section of \texttt{generated.tex} reads ``This run invoked no
collectives'' for a run that invoked exactly one. \texttt{failed\_ranks} is copied from
\texttt{job.finish}, which lists ranks whose \texttt{rank\_main} raised, and
\texttt{bench\_faults} catches \texttt{ampi.AmpiError} inside \texttt{rank\_main}, so the
job reported \texttt{ok: true} with an empty list. \texttt{coordination\_is\_underreported}
is \texttt{false} here, which is technically correct --- there is no completed collective
whose time is being undercounted --- and misleading in effect, since $7 \times 15.2 = 106$
rank-seconds of blocking inside a barrier are simply absent from every coordination
figure, and the flag that would have warned a reader stays down. The
\texttt{PROCEED} and \texttt{SHRINK} siblings, which \emph{did} record their barrier, both
raise the flag and both report \texttt{degraded: true}.

So the comparison that this run contributes to the sweep is uncomfortable: the two
policies that handled the failure well are flagged as degraded, and the two that handled
it worst pass every mechanical check. The analysis tooling is not at fault --- it can only
score what the log contains --- but the shape of the miss is worth naming, because a
reader scanning 500 runs for trouble would skip this one. I would call it a real
observability gap rather than a defect in \texttt{analysis.py}: the fix belongs upstream,
in having the dissemination barrier record its invocation and its failure the way the
central one does.

\subsection{Against its siblings, and what a harness author should do}

The four runs are the same program with one enum changed, so every difference between
them is attributable to the policy:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrrl@{}}
\toprule
policy & algorithm & events & wall (s) & msgs & \texttt{ft.declare\_failed} & absentee named \\
\midrule
\texttt{wait}    & dissemination & 45 & 15.26 & 17 & 0  & no \\
\texttt{raise}   & dissemination & 45 & 15.27 & 17 & 0  & no \\
\texttt{proceed} & central       & 39 & 15.22 & 0  & 7  & yes \\
\texttt{shrink}  & central       & 60 & 75.43 & 0  & 14 & yes, then seven false ones \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\normalsize
\end{center}

\texttt{PROCEED} detects correctly in 15.195\,s, writes one durable failure row, leaves
the communicator intact, and hands the harness the absentee list through a normal return.
\texttt{SHRINK} detects identically and then attempts a repair that breaks: seven
concurrent \texttt{UPDATE comms SET generation = generation + 1} statements in
\texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place} drive the generation from 0 to 7, the survivors cache six
different values, and the \texttt{ft.agree} that follows splits across six separate
collectives, blocks for its full 60\,s, and ends with seven false failure declarations and
five ranks returning \texttt{true} over \texttt{voters:\ []}.

\textbf{The recommendation is \texttt{PROCEED} wherever a phase's input can legitimately
be partial and \texttt{REVOKE} plus an explicit out-of-place \texttt{ft.shrink} wherever
it cannot; and \texttt{RAISE} --- which is the default --- should be treated as
unsuitable for any barrier a harness intends to recover from.} The evidence from this run
is direct. \texttt{RAISE} costs the same fifteen seconds as \texttt{PROCEED} and buys
strictly less: no absentee list, no failure record, an error message naming a live rank,
and 17 messages of traffic that \texttt{PROCEED} does not send at all. The only thing it
provides that \texttt{PROCEED} does not is an exception, and an exception carrying the
wrong rank number is worse than a return value carrying the right one.

The archive supplies the case that makes this concrete. \texttt{real-tr-p16-full} is the
only genuine failure among the 500 runs: sixteen ranks, all sixteen in
\texttt{failed\_ranks}, \texttt{ok: false}, 7{,}200\,s of wall clock, \$0.0726 spent and
no output. Six rank roles were never bound to a worker process, and the ten that worked
correctly died waiting inside a glossary \texttt{allreduce} that could never fold. Its
\texttt{failures} table has zero rows and its log has zero \texttt{ft.declare\_failed}
events --- the same empty-record state this run ends in, reached the same way, by a
collective that blocks on a timeout and records nothing about who failed to arrive. Its
own analysis concludes that a shrink over the ten ranks that actually had executors would
have finished the job in minutes. (Ten, not twelve: twelve is the count of ranks that
emitted a \texttt{coll.reduce} record, which is a different set from the set that could do
work.)

The caveat this sweep is well placed to add is that \emph{no} choice among these four
names would have saved that run. Reading the signatures in
\texttt{src/agentmpi/algorithms.py}, \texttt{policy} is a parameter of \texttt{barrier}
and of nothing else --- \texttt{bcast}, \texttt{scatter}, \texttt{gather},
\texttt{allgather}, \texttt{reduce}, \texttt{allreduce} and \texttt{reduce\_scatter} take
a \texttt{timeout} and no policy at all. The collective that killed the p=16 run was an
\texttt{allreduce}, and an \texttt{allreduce} behaves the way this run behaves: it blocks,
it times out, and it records nothing about who did not arrive. That is the deeper reading
this run supports. The problem \texttt{RAISE} exhibits is not really a bad policy choice;
it is the default behaviour of every collective in AgentMPI except the central barrier,
and it is what a harness gets unless it goes out of its way.

\section{Threats to this reading}

\textbf{The round-by-round reconstruction is derived, not logged.} No event says which
round a message belonged to; I recovered the structure from the internal tags, which
encode it as \texttt{\_ampi:barrier:\{generation\}:\{epoch\}:\{k\}}, and checked the
resulting send and receive counts against the per-rank table in \texttt{generated.tex},
which matches exactly (7 + 6 + 4 sends; 6 + 4 receives). The mapping from those counts to
``rank~5 blocked waiting on rank~3'' additionally assumes the dissemination schedule
$(r \pm 2^k) \bmod p$ as implemented, which I read from the source. It is a short
inference and the arithmetic is over-determined, but it is an inference.

\textbf{The claim that the failure is unrecoverable-from is a source reading, not an
observation.} \texttt{rank\_main} catches the \texttt{AmpiTimeout} and returns; it never
attempts \texttt{ft.shrink} or \texttt{ft.agree}, so this trace cannot show what would
happen if it did. I am relying on \texttt{\_agree\_survivors} computing its survivor set
from \texttt{get\_failed} plus \texttt{ft.health}, and on the \texttt{failures} table
being empty. The one hole in that reading is \texttt{ft.health}: its lease-based detector
could in principle have suspected rank~3 independently, since rank~3's lease would
eventually expire. Nothing in this run exercises it, and whether it would fire inside a
15\,s window depends on the lease length, which this trace does not record.

\textbf{There are no agents here and nothing in this run bears on agent behaviour.}
\texttt{executors: \{none: 8\}}, zero agent calls, zero tokens, \$0. The 17 messages carry
one token each and their digests are all \texttt{6b86b273ff34} --- the same one-byte
payload, seventeen times. The failure modelled is a rank that returns instantly, which is
\texttt{FAIL\_STOP} and only \texttt{FAIL\_STOP}; \texttt{src/agentmpi/ft.py}'s own
taxonomy is explicit that the class that matters for agent systems is
\texttt{FAIL\_PLAUSIBLE}, a rank that returns a confident wrong answer, and that no
deadline detects it. These four runs exercise one row of a six-row table.

\textbf{Two counters named \texttt{tokens\_deferred} disagree across artefacts, and this
run is a clean instance of it.} \texttt{job.finish} reports 10;
\texttt{metrics.json} reports 0 at the top level and surfaces the 10 as
\texttt{summary.tokens\_unadmitted}. Both are correct under their own definitions ---
\texttt{analysis.py} counts tokens on rendezvous \emph{sends}, of which there were none,
while the runtime counts received tokens not admitted to a context, which is all ten
receives at one token each --- but a reader putting the two files side by side sees the
same name with different values. The quantity is trivially small here and nothing in this
reading depends on it; the same collision at a scale that matters is documented in
\texttt{real-tr-p16-full}, where the two figures are 50{,}544 and 16{,}082.

\textbf{$n=1$, but almost nothing here is statistical.} A rerun would shuffle the
millisecond ordering of the 17 sends and move the wall clock by tens of milliseconds. It
would not change the send and receive counts, which are determined by the dissemination
schedule and the victim's identity; it would not change the empty \texttt{failures} table
or the empty \texttt{collectives} table, which are determined by which branch of
\texttt{algorithms.barrier} runs; and it would not change the identity of the rank named
in the error, which is fixed by $(0-4) \bmod 8$. The one genuinely schedule-dependent
detail is which survivor's error the benchmark happens to report as
\texttt{example\_error}. Where this analysis rests on a number I have tried to say what
determines it, and in every case the answer is the algorithm, the configuration, or the
fabric --- not the run.

\begin{abstract}
This is the \texttt{SHRINK} point of the four-policy barrier-failure sweep in
\texttt{bench\_faults} (\texttt{experiments/microbench.py}): eight ranks, rank~3 returns
immediately to simulate a crashed agent session, and the seven survivors enter a barrier
with a 15\,s deadline and the policy under test. Of the four policies it is the only one
that runs the whole fault-tolerance stack --- detection, declaration, in-place shrink and
then a fault-tolerant \texttt{agree} --- and it is the only one whose recovery path
visibly breaks. Detection works exactly as designed: all seven survivors time out within
2.7\,ms of each other at $t=15.198$--15.201\,s, all seven name \texttt{absent:\ [3]},
and the benchmark records \texttt{detected\_correctly:\ true} with a median detection
latency of 15.196\,s against a 15.0\,s deadline. What follows is a cascade. The
\texttt{agree} that the harness calls next never reaches quorum, blocks for its full 60\,s
timeout, and terminates with seven \emph{spurious} \texttt{ft.declare\_failed} events
naming six healthy ranks, two \texttt{ft.agree\_timeout} events, and five ranks returning
\texttt{result:\ true} with \texttt{voters:\ []} --- agreement over an empty electorate.
The cause is in the fabric and is unambiguous: \texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place} executes
\texttt{UPDATE comms SET generation = generation + 1} once per calling rank, so seven
independent calls drove the communicator's generation from 0 to 7, and because
\texttt{Communicator.\_record\_collective} keys a collective on
\texttt{(ctx, generation, epoch, op)} the seven ranks then voted into \emph{six different
collectives} (\texttt{cid} 8--13). The single most important thing to take away is that
this is a genuine AgentMPI defect and not an artefact of the benchmark:
\texttt{shrink\_in\_place} is a collective operation implemented with a non-idempotent
per-rank increment, where its out-of-place sibling \texttt{ft.shrink} correctly writes an
absolute generation behind a leader election. Everything downstream of the barrier in
this trace is that one line's consequence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Reading the timeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} has eight lanes, no bars, and marks in exactly two vertical
columns: one at $t\approx15.2$\,s and one at $t\approx75.4$\,s, separated by sixty
seconds of nothing. There are no blue work bars anywhere because there is no work
anywhere. \texttt{metrics.json} records \texttt{executors:\ \{none: 8\}}, zero agent
calls, zero tokens and \$0.0000, so the headline table's \emph{achieved parallelism
$0.00\times$} and \emph{idle fraction 100.0\%} are statements about the benchmark's
design rather than findings about the protocol. \texttt{bench\_faults} deliberately
contains no agent invocation: its \texttt{rank\_main} calls \texttt{comm.barrier} and
nothing else. A busy fraction cannot be anything but zero when no rank was ever given a
task, and the two rows should be read as ``not applicable'' rather than as a
utilisation result. The same caveat covers the wall time. Every duration in this run is
a timeout constant: 15\,s is \texttt{--detect-timeout} from
\texttt{results/microbench/free-faults.json}, 60\,s is the literal in the harness's
\texttt{ampi.agree(comm, True, timeout=60)} call, and 75.431\,s is their sum plus about
0.4\,s of poll granularity and fabric write latency.

Rank~3's lane is empty apart from its lifecycle ticks at the far left. It initialised at
$t=0.012$\,s and finalised at $t=0.013$\,s, one millisecond later, which is the whole of
the injected fault: \texttt{rank\_main} returns \texttt{\{"role": "victim"\}} without
touching the communicator. Nothing in the trace distinguishes this from an agent session
that died before its first call, which is the point of the injection.

The first column is the detection, and it is remarkably tight. Seven
\texttt{barrier.timeout} events land between 15.198\,s and 15.201\,s --- a spread of
2.7\,ms across seven independently polling processes --- each carrying
\texttt{absent:\ [3]} and \texttt{policy:\ "shrink"}. Each is followed within a
millisecond by \texttt{ft.declare\_failed}, then \texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place}, then
\texttt{coll.barrier}, the four-event signature repeated seven times. The tightness is
worth a sentence because it is not luck. The central barrier polls the fabric with a
back-off that starts at 20\,ms and saturates at 250\,ms, and the seven survivors entered
the barrier within 6\,ms of each other (\texttt{rank.init} from 0.002\,s to 0.008\,s), so
all seven were in the same 250\,ms poll phase and crossed their deadlines together. The
collective table records the consequence as \emph{skew 0.01\,s}, against 0.02\,s for the
\texttt{PROCEED} sibling --- the only other run in the sweep that logs a collective at
all. The viewer
renders these seven marks in violet, the recovery colour, and its legend counts
\emph{fault recovery 12} --- the seven \texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place} events plus the five
\texttt{ft.agree} events sixty seconds later.

The second column is where the run goes wrong, and the viewer shows it more clearly than
any number does. At $t\approx75.4$\,s, five lanes (ranks 0, 2, 4, 5, 6) carry a violet
diamond and two lanes (ranks 1 and 7) carry a \emph{red} one. That colour split is the
finding: seven ranks entered the same agreement and two of them left it by raising while
five left it by returning. Under \texttt{ft.agree}'s own docstring --- ``either every
surviving caller returns the same value, or all of them raise'' --- those two colours
should not appear in the same column.

The sixty-second gap between the columns contains no events at all, and that absence is
itself a measurement. All seven survivors were inside \texttt{ft.agree}, polling the
\texttt{coll\_parts} table on the same 20\,ms-to-250\,ms back-off. Reproducing that
schedule arithmetically gives about 250 polls per rank over 60\,s, so roughly 1{,}750
\texttt{SELECT} statements crossed the fabric in a window the trace renders as empty; the
barrier's own 15\,s wait contributed about 490 more. This is what the run's wall clock is
actually made of, and it is why nothing here should be read as a cost measurement for
anything but SQLite.

The viewer's \textsc{failures} tile reads \textbf{14} while its rank table shows all eight
ranks \texttt{finalized} with \texttt{suspected:\ --}, and \texttt{metrics.json} reports
\texttt{failed\_ranks:\ []} beside \texttt{summary.failures:\ 14}. These are not in
conflict; they count different things. \texttt{failures} counts
\texttt{ft.declare\_failed} events in the log, and \texttt{failed\_ranks} is copied
verbatim from the \texttt{job.finish} payload, which lists ranks whose \texttt{rank\_main}
raised. The benchmark catches \texttt{ampi.AmpiError} inside \texttt{rank\_main}, so no
rank propagated an exception to the launcher, and the job reports \texttt{ok:\ true} for a
run in which the fabric ended up holding eight failure rows and seven of the eight world
ranks were at some point declared dead. The rank table's \texttt{suspected:\ --} column is
consistent for the same reason: it reflects the lease-based detector in \texttt{ft.health},
which was never consulted, not the \texttt{failures} table.

\section{Interpretation}

\subsection{What is true by construction}

The shape of the first fifteen seconds is entirely dictated by the harness and the policy,
and none of it is a discovery. \texttt{bench\_faults} chose $p=8$ and victim set
\texttt{[3]}; \texttt{algorithms.barrier} forces \texttt{algorithm = "central"} whenever
the policy is \texttt{PROCEED}, \texttt{SHRINK} or \texttt{REVOKE}, because only the
arrival-counting algorithm can name absentees; and the central barrier therefore sends no
messages at all, which is why \texttt{metrics.json} reports \texttt{messages: 0} and
\texttt{eager\_messages: 0} for a run whose entire purpose was a collective. That zero is
a property of the algorithm selection, not evidence of a quiet run: the barrier's
coordination went through 490-odd fabric reads instead of through the message layer, and
none of that traffic is visible in the message counters. Likewise the barrier's
\texttt{rounds: 0} against \texttt{predicted 2} in the collective table is a logging gap
rather than a disagreement --- \texttt{algorithms.barrier} sets a local \texttt{rounds = 2}
for the central path and returns it in \texttt{BarrierResult}, but never writes it onto
the \texttt{\_Tracker}'s \texttt{CollStats}, so the emitted \texttt{coll.barrier} always
carries zero. The tooling correctly declines to score this as a model failure
(\modelna{} in the model column).

\subsection{The mechanism, from the trace}

The policy's realisation is eleven lines of \texttt{\_central\_barrier} in
\texttt{src/agentmpi/algorithms.py} and the trace matches them one for one. Each survivor
registers its arrival in \texttt{coll\_parts} through \texttt{\_record\_collective}, then
polls \texttt{SELECT crank FROM coll\_parts WHERE cid=?} until either the arrival count
reaches \texttt{comm.size} or the deadline passes. At the deadline it computes
\texttt{absent = \{0..7\} - arrived}, emits \texttt{barrier.timeout}, and --- because the
policy is neither \texttt{RAISE} nor \texttt{WAIT} --- calls \texttt{ft.declare\_failed}
for every absentee, then \texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place}, then returns the absentee tuple
normally rather than raising. That last detail is what makes \texttt{SHRINK} and
\texttt{PROCEED} the only two policies in the sweep for which the benchmark records
\texttt{all\_completed:\ true}: the barrier hands the harness a
\texttt{BarrierResult} whose \texttt{absent} field is \texttt{(3,)} and whose
\texttt{complete} property is false, and the harness treats a returned result as success.

\subsubsection*{Why fourteen \texttt{ft.declare\_failed} events for one failure}

The fourteen split cleanly into seven plus seven, and only the first seven are about
rank~3.

The first seven are the barrier's, all at $t=15.198$--15.204\,s, all with
\texttt{failed:\ 3} and \texttt{detail:\ "barrier phase"}. There are seven because
\texttt{declare\_failed} is called by each rank independently --- the central barrier has
no leader, so every survivor discovers the same absentee and records it. The database
deduplicates and the log does not: the \texttt{INSERT} into \texttt{failures} carries
\texttt{ON CONFLICT(ctx, rank, kind) DO NOTHING}, and querying the run's fabric
(\texttt{runs/mb-free/faults-shrink/fabric.sqlite}) shows a single row for rank~3, while
\texttt{fabric.emit} sits outside the conflict clause and fires unconditionally. So the
event count is the number of \emph{observers}, and the row count is the number of
\emph{failures}. Seven observers, one failure. This is defensible --- an event log should
record who noticed what and when, not a deduplicated summary --- but it means the
viewer's \textsc{failures} tile of 14 and \texttt{metrics.json}'s
\texttt{summary.failures: 14} both overstate the number of dead ranks by an order of
magnitude, and neither artefact says so.

The second seven are not about rank~3 at all. At $t=75.424$\,s rank~1 emits six
\texttt{ft.declare\_failed} events inside a millisecond, naming ranks 0, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 7 with
\texttt{detail:\ "agree timeout"}, and at $t=75.425$\,s rank~7 emits one naming rank~1.
All seven name a rank that was alive, healthy, and at that moment sitting in the same
\texttt{agree} call. They come from the timeout branch of \texttt{ft.agree}, which
declares every non-voter failed before raising. By the end of the run the
\texttt{failures} table holds eight rows: rank~3, correctly, and every other rank in the
world, incorrectly.

\subsubsection*{Why two \texttt{ft.agree\_timeout} events, and only two}

The two are rank~1's and rank~7's, 1.1\,ms apart, and the reason there are exactly two
rather than seven is a race that the first timeout sets up for the others.

\texttt{ft.agree}'s loop computes \texttt{expected = \{c for c in range(comm.size) if c
not in get\_failed(comm)\}} on every iteration and completes as soon as
\texttt{expected} is a subset of the ranks that have voted. Rank~1 reaches its deadline
first. At that instant the \texttt{failures} table holds only rank~3, so its
\texttt{expected} is the seven survivors, it has seen one vote (its own), and its
\texttt{missing} list is the other six: \texttt{ft.agree\_timeout} with
\texttt{missing:\ [0, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7]}, preceded by six declarations. Rank~7 reaches its
deadline 1\,ms later, and by then those six declarations are in the table, so \emph{its}
\texttt{expected} has collapsed to \texttt{\{1\}} alone: \texttt{missing:\ [1]}, one
declaration, one \texttt{ft.agree\_timeout}. Those two events between them have now
declared all eight world ranks failed.

The remaining five ranks --- 0, 2, 4, 5 and 6 --- never reach their deadlines. On their
next poll, within five milliseconds, they recompute \texttt{expected} against a
\texttt{failures} table that now contains everybody, get the empty set, find that the
empty set is trivially a subset of what has voted, and take the success branch. Python's
\texttt{all()} over an empty generator is \texttt{True}, so all five return \texttt{True}
and emit \texttt{ft.agree} with \texttt{result:\ true}, \texttt{voters:\ []},
\texttt{excluded:\ [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]}. Five ranks agreed that a proposition held,
unanimously, among nobody.

\subsection{The root cause: a collective operation implemented as a per-rank increment}

None of the above should have happened, because all seven survivors called
\texttt{agree} with the same value at nearly the same instant. Querying the fabric shows
why they never saw each other:

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrl@{}}
\toprule
\texttt{cid} & \texttt{generation} & \texttt{epoch} & voters recorded in \texttt{coll\_parts} \\
\midrule
1  & 0 & 1 & 0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7 \quad(the barrier) \\
8  & 2 & 2 & 7 \\
9  & 3 & 2 & 0 \\
10 & 4 & 2 & 2 \\
11 & 5 & 2 & 1 \\
12 & 6 & 2 & 4 \\
13 & 7 & 2 & 5, 6 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\normalsize
\end{center}

Seven ranks, one \texttt{agree}, six collectives. Five ranks voted entirely alone and
ranks 5 and 6 happened to pair up. No \texttt{cid} could ever have reached a quorum of
seven, so the 60\,s block was inevitable from the moment the votes were cast.

The mechanism is one line. \texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place} ends with

\begin{center}\texttt{cur.execute("UPDATE comms SET generation = generation + 1 WHERE ctx=?", (comm.ctx,))}\end{center}

\noindent followed by \texttt{comm.refresh()}, which reads the generation back into the
caller's cache. Seven ranks called it, so the counter was incremented seven times and the
run's \texttt{comms} row ends at \texttt{generation = 7}. Each rank refreshed at a
different point in that sequence and cached a different value --- rank~7 read 2, rank~0
read 3, rank~2 read 4, rank~1 read 5, rank~4 read 6, ranks 5 and 6 both read 7. Then
\texttt{\_record\_collective} keyed the \texttt{agree} on
\texttt{(ctx, generation, epoch, op)}, and six distinct generations produced six distinct
\texttt{cid}s.

I would call this a real defect in AgentMPI rather than expected behaviour, for three
reasons. First, the increment is relative where every other generation write in the
codebase is absolute: the out-of-place \texttt{ft.shrink} elects a leader, has that leader
alone create the new context, publishes the result through a content-keyed \texttt{meta}
row so that non-leaders converge on the same \texttt{ctx}, and writes
\texttt{UPDATE comms SET generation=?} with the value \texttt{comm.generation + 1}. Run
seven times concurrently, that code converges; \texttt{shrink\_in\_place} does not.
Second, the operation is unavoidably collective. It is called from inside
\texttt{\_central\_barrier}, which every participant executes, so ``seven ranks call it at
once'' is the only way it is ever invoked, not an unusual usage. Third, the blast radius
is wider than the one symptom seen here: \texttt{Communicator.\_itag} embeds
\texttt{self.generation} in the internal tag of every point-to-point message a collective
sends, so ranks left holding different generations cannot match each other's messages
either. This run only exercised the fabric-keyed path; a message-passing collective after
an in-place shrink would fail the same way, and less legibly.

The fix is small and local --- compute the target generation once and write it absolutely,
as \texttt{shrink} already does, or make the increment conditional on the current value ---
but I would not change the code on the strength of this trace alone without also checking
whether any caller depends on the counter advancing once per participant.

\subsection{A second defect: agreement over an empty electorate}

The vacuous \texttt{True} is a separate bug from the generation split, and it would remain
after the split was fixed. \texttt{ft.agree}'s completion test is
\texttt{if expected <= set(voted)}, with \texttt{result = all(v for c, v in voted.items()
if c in expected)}. When \texttt{expected} is empty both are trivially satisfied, so the
function returns \texttt{True} without a single vote having been counted, and reports it
in the trace as \texttt{voters:\ []}. The same module already knows this state is
pathological and says so elsewhere: \texttt{\_agree\_survivors} raises
\texttt{AmpiProcFailed("no survivors")} when the survivor set empties out. \texttt{agree}
should do the same, and a caller receiving \texttt{True} from a consensus primitive is
entitled to assume somebody consented.

The consequence in this run is the contract violation visible in the timeline's colour
split. \texttt{ft.agree}'s docstring promises that ``either every surviving caller returns
the same value, or all of them raise''. Here two callers raised
\texttt{AmpiProcFailed} and five returned \texttt{True}, and the five were not a
superset or subset of any coherent survivor set --- by their own emitted payload they
excluded themselves. This is exactly the split-brain outcome that fault-tolerant
agreement exists to prevent, produced by the agreement primitive itself.

It is worth being clear about what the benchmark says about this, because it says the
wrong thing. \texttt{results/microbench/free-faults.json} records
\texttt{"agree\_consistent": true} for the shrink row. That field is computed as
\texttt{len(\{s.get("agreed") for s in survivors if s.get("agreed") is not None\}) <= 1},
and the two ranks that raised recorded \texttt{agreed = None} because
\texttt{bench\_faults} catches \texttt{ampi.AmpiError} and leaves the field unset. The
metric therefore filters out precisely the ranks whose disagreement it exists to detect,
and reports consistency across the five that survived to answer. The published row is
misleading and the trace is not; anyone reading the fault table in the paper should be
told that the shrink row's \texttt{agree\_consistent} is vacuous here.

\subsection{A third, quieter defect: in-place shrink does not shrink anything a collective reads}

Even had the generation counter behaved, the repair this policy performs is largely
nominal. \texttt{shrink\_in\_place} sets \texttt{comm\_members.state = 'failed'} for the
absentees, and the run's fabric confirms it: \texttt{crank 3} ends the run marked
\texttt{failed} and the other seven \texttt{active}. \texttt{Communicator.refresh}
duly reads that column into a \texttt{\_active} tuple. But \texttt{\_active} is
\emph{written and never read} --- it appears exactly once in \texttt{src/agentmpi},
at the assignment. \texttt{Communicator.size} is \texttt{len(self.\_members)}, which
counts failed members too, and \texttt{\_central\_barrier}'s completion test is
\texttt{len(arrived) >= comm.size}. So a second barrier on this communicator would wait
the full timeout for rank~3 all over again, which is precisely what the docstring's
``only excludes the dead from future collectives'' says it will not do. The one operation
that does exclude the dead, \texttt{agree}, gets there by a different route entirely: it
consults the \texttt{failures} table through \texttt{get\_failed}, not the member states.

The benchmark cannot catch this, because \texttt{rank\_main} runs exactly one barrier.
That makes the finding inferential from the source rather than measured from this trace,
and I flag it as such --- but the inference is short, and the trace does supply the two
halves of it: the member row is marked \texttt{failed}, and \texttt{comm.size} is still
reported as 8 in the \texttt{coll.barrier} payload emitted after the shrink.

\subsection{What \texttt{ft.agree} is for, and why it is needed here at all}

It is worth stating plainly, because the name invites the wrong reading:
\texttt{agree} repairs nothing. It is fault-tolerant consensus on a boolean over a group
whose membership is itself in doubt, and its output is a decision, not a communicator.
The \texttt{ft.agree} payload's two lists say exactly that --- \texttt{voters} is the set
whose consent was required and obtained, \texttt{excluded} is the set written off --- and
in a healthy run they partition the world.

The reason a decision is needed after a failure is that shrinking leaves every survivor
holding a \emph{local} answer to a \emph{global} question. Rank~5 knows that its own
barrier timed out and that it saw rank~3 absent; it does not know whether rank~6 saw the
same absentee set, whether rank~6 also shrank, or whether the phase whose completion is
now in question actually completed at rank~6. Without agreement two survivors can proceed
on incompatible premises --- one rebuilding a seven-member group and another a six-member
one, or one treating the phase as done and another retrying it --- and because the group
has already lost a member, an ordinary \texttt{allreduce} over logical AND cannot be used
to reconcile them: it would block forever on the dead rank. That is the gap
\texttt{agree} fills, and it is why \texttt{ft.shrink} calls
\texttt{\_agree\_survivors} before building anything. AgentMPI is explicit that this is
not a circumvention of FLP: the vote is routed through the fabric, which is a shared
fate-sharing point, and the docstring says so rather than claiming otherwise.

This run is therefore a demonstration of why the primitive matters, delivered backwards.
The seven survivors did diverge --- into six different views of the communicator's
generation --- and \texttt{agree} was the operation that would have caught it. It failed
to, because the divergence was in the very key it uses to find its peers.

\subsection{The flagged findings}

Two findings are generated for this run and both are expected rather than defects.

\emph{``\texttt{barrier/central} (label \texttt{phase}) was reached by 7 of 8 ranks, so it
never completed.''} This is the experiment. The benchmark kills rank~3 on purpose so that
the barrier cannot complete, and the policy under test is defined entirely by what happens
at that moment. A run of this benchmark in which the barrier \emph{did} complete would be
the defective one. The finding is also the reason the collective table shows \modelna{} in
the model column and \texttt{model\_checks\_total} is 0: \texttt{analysis.py} gates the
cost-model comparison on \texttt{n\_participants >= size}, correctly declining to score a
closed form against an invocation that did not happen.

\emph{``The coordination figures count time inside \emph{completed} collectives only\ldots
the reported share is a floor.''} Also expected, and on this run the floor is unusually
loose in a way the sibling runs make visible. The numerator behind \emph{coordination
share 17.6\%} is 106.367 rank-seconds, which is $7 \times 15.195$\,s: the barrier waits,
and nothing else. The 60\,s that all seven survivors then spent blocked inside
\texttt{ft.agree} --- 420 rank-seconds, four times the recorded figure --- contributes
nothing, because \texttt{ft.agree} is not a \texttt{coll.*} event and is not counted as a
collective at all. Worse, that unrecorded blocking is in the \emph{denominator}:
coordination share is defined against \texttt{world\_size} $\times$ wall time, which the
extra sixty seconds inflates from 121.7 to 603.4 rank-seconds. So this run reports
\textbf{17.6\%} coordination and its \texttt{proceed} sibling reports \textbf{87.4\%},
with an identical numerator, purely because this run coordinated for sixty seconds longer
in a way the metric cannot see. Reconstructed from the log, $7 \times (15.2 + 60.2) =
527.8$ rank-seconds of the 603.4 available were spent blocked in coordination, or 87\%.
The metric is correct as defined; the definition is not the quantity a reader of this run
wants, and \texttt{coordination\_is\_underreported: true} is the right disclosure.

\subsection{Against its siblings, and what a harness author should do}

Across the four policies the trace supports a clear ordering, and it is not the one the
names suggest.

\texttt{WAIT} and \texttt{RAISE} are indistinguishable from each other and diagnostically
dead. Both leave \texttt{algorithm} at its \texttt{dissemination} default --- only
\texttt{PROCEED}, \texttt{SHRINK} and \texttt{REVOKE} switch to \texttt{central} --- and
the dissemination barrier cannot name absentees, so neither run contains a single
\texttt{barrier.timeout}, \texttt{ft.*} or \texttt{coll.*} event. Their fabrics' failure
tables are empty. Both fail with \texttt{ERR\_TIMEOUT: receive timed out
[\ldots source=4 \ldots]}, naming a live rank rather than the dead one, and the benchmark
scores both \texttt{detected\_correctly:\ false}.

\texttt{PROCEED} detects correctly, in 15.195\,s, at a cost of one failure row and seven
events, and leaves the communicator untouched --- generation 0, all eight members
\texttt{active}. It buys knowledge and defers the repair.

\texttt{SHRINK} detects identically (15.196\,s) and then attempts the repair, and in this
trace the repair is what breaks. It costs an extra 60.2\,s of wall clock, seven false
failure declarations, an inconsistent agreement, and a communicator whose generation
counter is seven ahead of where any rank thinks it is.

\textbf{The recommendation is therefore \texttt{PROCEED} for phases whose input can be
partial, and \texttt{REVOKE} followed by an explicit out-of-place \texttt{ft.shrink} for
phases whose input cannot be --- and not the \texttt{SHRINK} barrier policy, until the
generation increment is fixed.} \texttt{REVOKE} is untested in this sweep, which is a
real gap in the evidence, but it is the only escalating policy whose implementation does
not route through \texttt{shrink\_in\_place}: it calls \texttt{ft.revoke}, which writes
an absolute \texttt{revoked=1} and is idempotent by construction, and then raises, leaving
the harness to call \texttt{ft.shrink} --- the sibling that does elect a leader and does
converge.

The counterexample that makes this concrete is elsewhere in the archive.
\texttt{real-tr-p16-full} is the only genuine failure among the 500 runs: sixteen ranks,
all sixteen in \texttt{failed\_ranks}, \texttt{ok: false}, 7{,}200\,s of wall clock,
\$0.0726 spent and no output. Six rank roles were never bound to a worker process, and the
ten that worked correctly died waiting inside a glossary \texttt{allreduce} that could
never fold. That run's harness imports none of \texttt{revoke}, \texttt{shrink} or
\texttt{agree}; its \texttt{failures} table has zero rows and its log has zero
\texttt{ft.declare\_failed} events. The present run's first fifteen seconds are precisely
the affordance it lacked --- name the absentee, record the failure, hand the harness a
list --- and its own analysis concludes that a shrink at $t\approx190$\,s over the ten
ranks that actually had executors would have finished the job in minutes. (Ten, not
twelve: twelve is the number of ranks that emitted a \texttt{coll.reduce} record, which is
a different set from the set that could do work.)

But the connection carries a caveat that this sweep is well placed to make, and it cuts
against the easy moral. \texttt{policy} is a parameter of \texttt{barrier} and of nothing
else. Reading the signatures in \texttt{src/agentmpi/algorithms.py}, \texttt{bcast},
\texttt{scatter}, \texttt{gather}, \texttt{allgather}, \texttt{reduce},
\texttt{allreduce} and \texttt{reduce\_scatter} take a \texttt{timeout} and no policy at
all. The collective that killed \texttt{real-tr-p16-full} was an \texttt{allreduce}, so
no choice among these four names would have saved it; the harness would have had to place
a policy-bearing barrier \emph{before} the allreduce, or the protocol would have to
extend the policy to the other collectives. That harness did use \texttt{PROCEED}, at a
barrier in phase 5, downstream of the phase-2 allreduce it never survived to reach. The
lesson this sweep contributes is not simply ``choose \texttt{SHRINK}'' but that a failure
policy only protects the operation it is attached to, and that in AgentMPI today exactly
one operation can carry one.

\section{Threats to this reading}

\textbf{The generation diagnosis rests on the fabric, not on the event log, and the event
log alone would not support it.} No emitted event carries a generation field:
\texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place}'s payload is \texttt{\{"excluded": [3]\}} and nothing more,
and \texttt{ft.agree}'s payload names voters and excluded but not the \texttt{cid} it
voted into. I recovered the six-\texttt{cid} split by querying
\texttt{runs/mb-free/faults-shrink/fabric.sqlite} directly, and the inference that each
rank's \emph{cached} generation differed --- as opposed to some other keying accident ---
comes from reading \texttt{\_record\_collective} and \texttt{shrink\_in\_place} together
with those six rows. It is a tight inference, and the row set (generations 2 through 7
with no 0 or 1, exactly seven voters spread over six \texttt{cid}s) admits no other
reading I can construct. But if the fabric were unavailable the trace would show only an
unexplained 60\,s stall, which is itself an argument for putting the generation into the
\texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place} and \texttt{ft.agree} payloads.

\textbf{This is $n=1$ on a race, and the particular numbers are schedule-dependent.} That
rank~1 timed out first, that it named six ranks and rank~7 named one, that ranks 5 and 6
landed on the same generation and everyone else did not --- all of that is one
interleaving of seven processes contending on a SQLite counter. A rerun would produce a
different partition of the seven votes across \texttt{cid}s and a different split between
raisers and vacuous returners. What would \emph{not} change is the structure: seven
increments of a shared counter and seven ranks caching it at seven different moments
cannot produce a single agreed key except by coincidence, and the failure is therefore a
property of the code rather than of this schedule. The counts 14 and 2 in the abstract are
this run's; the mechanism is not.

\textbf{There are no agents here and nothing in this run bears on agent behaviour.}
\texttt{executors: \{none: 8\}}, zero agent calls, zero tokens. The failure injected is a
rank that returns instantly, which models \texttt{FAIL\_STOP} and only
\texttt{FAIL\_STOP}. \texttt{ft.py}'s own taxonomy is explicit that the interesting class
for agent systems is \texttt{FAIL\_PLAUSIBLE} --- a rank that returns a confident wrong
answer --- and that no amount of timeout tuning detects it. Nothing in this sweep, and
nothing in the barrier policies at all, addresses that class. A reader taking these four
runs as the evidence for AgentMPI's fault-tolerance claims should note that they exercise
one row of a six-row table.

\textbf{The detection latency is a restatement of the deadline, not a measurement of a
detector.} \texttt{detect\_p50\_s} is 15.196\,s against a configured 15.0\,s, so the
figure is the timeout plus 196\,ms of poll granularity and fabric write latency. It says
nothing about how quickly AgentMPI \emph{can} notice a failure, because the only detector
exercised here is the barrier's own deadline. The lease-based detector in
\texttt{ft.health}, which is what \texttt{ft.py} presents as the failure detector, is
never called in this run: \texttt{\_agree\_survivors} would call it, but the harness uses
\texttt{agree} rather than \texttt{shrink}, and \texttt{agree} does not. A reader should
not generalise 15.2\,s into a detection-latency claim.

\textbf{Similarly, \texttt{shrink\_p50\_s: 0.0} in the published row measures nothing.}
The benchmark starts its shrink timer \emph{after} the barrier has returned, and by then
\texttt{shrink\_in\_place} has already run inside \texttt{\_central\_barrier}. What the
0.0\,s times is \texttt{comm.refresh()} plus a list comprehension. The actual in-place
shrink cost is bounded by the interval between each rank's \texttt{ft.declare\_failed} and
its \texttt{ft.shrink\_in\_place} event, which is under a millisecond for six of the seven
survivors and 1\,ms for the other --- the right order of magnitude, but recovered from the
log rather than from the benchmark's instrumentation.

\textbf{The third defect is read from the source, not observed.} That
\texttt{Communicator.\_active} is never consumed, and that a second barrier would
therefore hang on rank~3 again, follows from grepping \texttt{src/agentmpi} and from
\texttt{comm.size} counting all members. This run never issues a second collective on the
shrunken communicator, so the trace neither confirms nor refutes it. It is the one claim
here that a targeted experiment --- two barriers, one victim --- would settle in fifteen
seconds, and until someone runs it the claim should be read as a source reading.

\textbf{Finally, the harness catches its own errors, and that shapes every run-level
number.} \texttt{rank\_main} wraps the barrier in \texttt{except ampi.AmpiError} and the
agree in a second one, so no exception ever reaches \texttt{ampi.launch}. That is why
\texttt{job.finish} says \texttt{ok: true} and \texttt{failed\_ranks: []} for a run
containing two \texttt{AmpiProcFailed} raises and eight failure rows, why
\texttt{metrics.json}'s \texttt{failed\_ranks} is empty, and why \texttt{degraded} is
\texttt{true} only via the incomplete-collective clause. Every judgement above about what
went wrong comes from the event log and the fabric; the job-level accounting saw a clean
run and would have reported one even if all seven survivors had raised.

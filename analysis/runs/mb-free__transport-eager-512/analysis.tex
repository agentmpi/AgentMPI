\begin{abstract}
This is the middle of the eager column in a ten-cell transport sweep --- two modes
crossed with five payload sizes, all at $p = 8$ and all agent-free. Eight ranks form
a ring, each sending one 512-word payload to its successor and receiving from its
predecessor, with the mode pinned to \texttt{Mode.EAGER}. It completed in 0.03\,s
over 34 events and admitted 674 tokens into every receiver's context, 2.1\% of the
32{,}768-token budget and 16.5\% of the 4{,}096-token unexpected-message credit. Its
value is as the second point on a line: against the 128-word cell's 168 tokens, the
context charge is $4.01\times$ for $4\times$ the payload, so eager's cost to the
receiver is linear in payload with no fixed component. That linearity is what makes
the eager ceiling predictable, and it is what lets this sweep say where the ceiling
must fall without having measured every size --- 4{,}096 tokens of credit divided by
1.316 tokens per word puts it at 3{,}113 words, between this cell and the next
one up.
\end{abstract}

\section{Reading the timeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} is eight lanes of instantaneous marks with no bars in any
of them. That is a property of the benchmark rather than an observation about it: the
executor is \texttt{none} on all eight ranks, there were zero agent calls, and no
\texttt{task.*} event was emitted, so there is no interval for a bar to span. Read
the headline table's ``idle fraction 100.0\%'' and ``achieved parallelism
0.00$\times$'' accordingly --- they mean no rank ever held a task, not that ranks
were stalled. Each lane carries two grey lifecycle diamonds and two green message
ticks. The ranks arrive over a 21\,ms window against a 32\,ms total span, so
two-thirds of this run's wall clock is the launcher bringing eight ranks up against
one SQLite writer, and the ring itself occupies the remainder.

Within that remainder nothing waits on a peer. The median receive latency is 7\,ms
and the maximum 10\,ms, and neither is protocol delay. Rank 7 accounts for one of the
three 10\,ms figures directly: it initialised 24\,ms in and claimed rank 6's message
in the same millisecond it arrived, so its latency is simply how long the message sat
waiting for a receiver that did not exist yet. The others are contention, not
waiting --- every send inserts a row and every receive claims one inside an exclusive
SQLite write transaction, and eight ranks are doing that concurrently against a
single file. Every rank finalises within a millisecond of its receive. This is what
an eager exchange looks like when
it is well inside its budget --- the send returns as soon as the message row is
committed, and \texttt{\_await\_eager\_credit} finds zero outstanding queued tokens
at the destination because the ring gives each receiver exactly one inbound sender.
Figure~\ref{fig:comm} carries the structure the timeline cannot: a single

\section{Interpretation}

By construction: the world size, the ring mapping, one send and one receive per rank,
and the mode. One construction detail is worth stating because it drives the whole
sweep --- every \texttt{rank.init} records \texttt{eager\_limit: 1000000000}, so the
runtime's automatic mode selection is disabled. Left to itself,
\texttt{\_choose\_mode} would send a 674-token payload eager anyway (the default
limit is 2{,}048 tokens), but at 8{,}192 and 32{,}768 words it would silently convert
to rendezvous, and the sweep needs it not to. The failures in the eager column are
consequently deliberate: the harness forced a mode the runtime would have declined
to choose.

What emerged is the linearity, and it is worth being precise about it because the
sweep's central claim depends on extrapolating it. The payload is 512 repetitions of
\texttt{"word "}, 2{,}560 characters, priced by the default estimator at
$\lceil 2560/3.8 \rfloor = 674$ tokens. Against the 128-word cell's 168 that is
$4.01\times$ for $4\times$ the words; against the 2{,}048-word cell's 2{,}695 it is
$0.250\times$ for $0.25\times$ the words. The relation is $1.3158$ tokens per word
with no intercept, which is just $5/3.8$ --- the estimator's character rule applied
to a five-character word. So eager's context charge has no per-message overhead, and
a payload's admissibility is decided entirely by its own size. That is why the
ceiling is sharp rather than gradual: \texttt{\_await\_eager\_credit} compares
\texttt{n\_tokens} against the destination's \texttt{unexpected\_limit} of 4{,}096
and raises \texttt{AmpiContextOverflow} if the single payload exceeds it, and with a
linear price that condition flips at one specific payload size, 3{,}113 words. This
cell at 674 tokens sits at 16.5\% of credit; the next cell up, at 2{,}695 tokens,
sits at 66\%; the one after that, at 10{,}779 tokens, is refused. Two-thirds of the
distance to the cliff is covered between this cell and its immediate successor.

Set against its rendezvous twin, this cell also shows what the choice actually
trades. \texttt{mb-free\_\_transport-rendezvous-512} moves the same payload --- the
same content-addressed blob, the same digest --- in the same 34 events, and reports
zero admissions and zero context high water. The difference is not bytes on disk and
not messages on the wire; both runs write one 2.5\,KiB blob and send eight envelopes.
The difference is that here the receiver has the text and there it has a handle. At
2\% occupancy that is a bad trade to make reflexively, and the sweep is not arguing
for rendezvous at this size. The generated finding list is empty, correctly: there is
no anomaly in this cell.

\section{Threats to this reading}

The context numbers are admissions, not usage. With \texttt{executor: none} and zero
agent calls, \texttt{ContextAccount} recorded that 674 tokens entered a budget that
no model ever read, so this run establishes the protocol's accounting and says
nothing about agent behaviour. The token count itself is an estimate --- provenance
records \texttt{tokenizer\_exact: false} --- and the $5/3.8$ ratio that makes the
linearity so clean is an artefact of a character-based heuristic applied to a
pathological payload of one repeated word; a real BPE tokeniser would price this text
near 512 tokens, moving the sweep's ceiling in word terms by about 30\% while leaving
its existence untouched. The wall time is a single sample dominated by SQLite writes
and by a 21\,ms rank start-up spread that is itself two-thirds of the span, so the
fact that this cell took 32\,ms and the 2{,}048-word cell took 35\,ms supports no
claim at all. Finally, the linearity argument extrapolates to the failed cells from
three points, all of them the same synthetic string at different lengths; it would be
more convincing on payloads of varied shape, and the sweep does not have those.
Separately, the ring's one-inbound-message-per-receiver structure means the
\texttt{transport.credit\_stall} path --- the blocking back-pressure that
\texttt{\_await\_eager\_credit} exists to provide --- is never entered in any of these
ten runs, so the ceiling this sweep locates is a per-message precondition and not
the aggregate buffering limit the benchmark's docstring describes.

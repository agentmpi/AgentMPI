\begin{abstract}
Four agent ranks translated the eight-section book by the full stencil decomposition ---
\texttt{scatterv}, per-unit terminology extraction, a \texttt{UNION} \texttt{allreduce} over the
glossary, translation, a \texttt{halo\_exchange} on a four-node Cartesian line, and
\texttt{gather} --- in 308.44\,s with no failures, no contract violations and no retries. Speedup
over the 576.74\,s sequential baseline is 1.870$\times$ at efficiency 0.467. This is the middle
point of the strong-scaling series and the first one at which the topology is a topology rather
than a pair, so it is where two claims the campaign makes about the design become testable for
the first time, and both survive. The seam-reconciliation phase does real work here: three of the
six \texttt{revise} calls changed the text, and at the $u_5\,|\,u_6$ boundary both sides edited
toward each other coherently, which is the clearest evidence in the series that the halo exchange
is not decoration. And a neighbourhood collective is measurably cheaper than a barrier: the
exchange blocked its ranks for 64.25 rank-seconds where a global barrier at the same point would
have cost 79.91, a 19.6\% saving that is 0\% at $p=2$ and 43.3\% at $p=8$. The document's main
negative finding is where this point's efficiency actually goes. It is not the collectives. It is
the \texttt{pre-gather} barrier, which absorbed 174.40 rank-seconds --- 53.2\% of all idle time
and 14.1\% of the rank-seconds the run had available --- because the seam phase hands the two
interior ranks two revisions each and the two edge ranks one each, and a rank runs its revisions
in sequence. Four ranks is the only configuration in the series with that asymmetry, and it costs
this point about a fifth of its wall clock.
\end{abstract}

\section{Reading the timeline}

nothing alike. Before that the four lanes are dense and roughly in step: all four ranks are busy

for most of the terminology and translation phases. After it the picture collapses --- rank 0's
lane ends at 212.4\,s and rank 3's at 237.5\,s, while ranks 1 and 2 run bars until 308.0 and
300.3. The last 95.6 seconds of a 308-second run, 31\% of it, are two ranks and then one rank
working while the other two hold an open barrier. Everything interesting about this point is in
that asymmetry, and the rest of this section builds up to it.

\textbf{Decompose.} At $t=0.009$ rank 0 sends three eager messages of 1{,}881, 1{,}984 and
1{,}927 tokens and the \texttt{scatterv} is over in 6\,ms. That all three are eager is worth
one sentence, because it is a property of $p$: the ranks publish an \texttt{eager\_limit} of
2{,}048 tokens, a linear scatter's hop carries $1/p$ of the book, and at $p=4$ that is just under
the limit where at $p=2$ the single 3{,}911-token hop was just over it and went rendezvous. The
only rendezvous messages in this run are the three \texttt{gather} sends, which the harness

\section{Interpretation}

\subsection*{What is true by construction}

The phase structure is the harness's and the trace matches it instruction for instruction: 4
\texttt{coll.scatter}, 8 \texttt{coll.reduce}, 8 \texttt{coll.bcast}, 8 \texttt{coll.allreduce},
4 \texttt{topo.cart\_create}, 4 \texttt{coll.halo\_exchange}, 4 \texttt{coll.barrier}, 4
\texttt{coll.gather}, and 22 \texttt{agent.call}. The 22 calls are eight \texttt{terms}, eight
\texttt{translate} and six \texttt{revise}, and the six follow from the decomposition rather than
from any choice. With two units per rank, \texttt{build\_harness} hands neighbour context only to
a rank's first unit (from the left) and its last unit (from the right), so an interior rank
revises both of its units and an edge rank revises one: $1,2,2,1$.

Nine of the ten collective invocations sent exactly the number of messages their closed form
predicts, which Figure~\ref{fig:collectives} shows as matched pairs; the tenth is
\texttt{halo\_exchange}, discussed below.

One property is not by construction and matters for reading the series: $p=4$ is the first point
that takes the \texttt{binomial} code path. The selector in \texttt{algorithms.reduce} and
\texttt{algorithms.bcast} is \texttt{"flat" if p <= 3 else "binomial"}, so the glossary at $p=2$
was a one-round flat exchange and here it is a two-round tree. The scaling curve is not
algorithmically homogeneous across its four points, and the switch happens between the second and
third of them.

\subsection*{Where the efficiency went: a barrier paying for an unbalanced seam phase}

This is the finding the run makes best. Efficiency at this point is 0.467, down from 0.703 at
$p=2$, and the idle budget above says the loss is not in the message-passing collectives. The
glossary \texttt{allreduce} costs 25.92 rank-seconds, 2.1\% of what the run had available. The
\texttt{scatterv} and \texttt{gather} together cost 86\,ms. The barrier costs 174.40, and the
barrier is not a coordination cost in any interesting sense --- it is the shape of the work
arriving at it.

The shape is this. Model work per rank is 172.38, 272.93, 275.90 and 185.00 seconds, an imbalance
of 1.218. Splitting that by phase separates two different causes:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
  rank & units & words & paragraphs & terms+translate (s) & revisions & revise (s) \\
\midrule
  0 & $u_0,u_1$ & 1{,}324 & 26 & 132.49 & 1 & 39.90 \\
  1 & $u_2,u_3$ & 1{,}238 & 34 & 156.30 & 2 & 116.62 \\
  2 & $u_4,u_5$ & 1{,}241 & 69 & 171.14 & 2 & 104.76 \\
  3 & $u_6,u_7$ & 1{,}306 & 20 & 134.89 & 1 & 50.11 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Through terminology and translation the imbalance is 1.151 and it tracks paragraph count: rank 2
holds 69 of the book's 149 paragraphs and is the slowest, rank 3 holds 20 and is nearly the
fastest, and word count --- which is what \texttt{split\_units} balances --- points the wrong way,
since rank 0 has the most words and the second-lowest time. The revision phase then doubles the
spread, and it does so for a reason that has nothing to do with unit size: ranks 1 and 2 have two
revisions and ranks 0 and 3 have one, and a rank executes its \texttt{revise} calls in a Python
\texttt{for} loop over \texttt{mine}, one after another. Rank 1 spends 116.62 seconds on
revisions where rank 0 spends 39.90.

The consequence is the 174.40 rank-second barrier, and the counterfactual is easy to state.
Revision work totals 311.39 rank-seconds; spread evenly over four ranks that is 77.85 each, and
the phase's critical path would be about 78 seconds plus dispatch instead of the 116.6 rank 1
actually took. The run would have finished near 270\,s rather than 308\,s, for a speedup of
about 2.14 instead of 1.870. Roughly a fifth of this point's shortfall against $p=2$'s efficiency
is one scheduling decision inside the harness.

Four ranks is the only configuration in the series where this bites. At $p=2$ each rank has one
boundary and issues one revision; at $p=8$ each rank holds a single unit and every rank issues
exactly one; only at $p=4$ (and at any other $p$ with more than one unit per rank and more than
two ranks) does the interior/edge distinction produce a $1,2,2,1$ fan-out. That is a property of
the harness, not of AgentMPI, and it is worth reporting as such: the seam phase's work is
distributed by adjacency, and adjacency is not uniform on a line.

\subsection*{The neighbourhood collective earns its keep, and this is where it starts to}

The design argument for a \texttt{halo\_exchange} over a barrier is that a rank should be released
when its neighbours arrive rather than when the slowest rank in the world arrives. At $p=2$ that
argument cannot be made, because a rank's only neighbour is the whole rest of the world. At $p=4$
it can be made and measured.

The four ranks entered the exchange at 153.14, 156.19, 170.74 and 186.66\,s. A global barrier at
that point would have held each of them until 186.66 and cost $79.91$ rank-seconds. The exchange
cost 64.25, so the topology saved 15.66 rank-seconds, 19.6\% of the barrier's cost. The saving is
entirely rank 0's: its only binding neighbour is rank 1, which arrived at 170.74, so it left the
exchange 15.8\,s before the straggler arrived. Ranks 1 and 3 both wait on rank 2 either way, and
rank 2 waits on nobody.

Running the same comparison across the series gives $-0.5\%$ at $p=2$, $19.6\%$ at $p=4$ and
$43.3\%$ at $p=8$ (34.72 rank-seconds against a 61.27 barrier). The negative value at $p=2$ is
real and not a rounding artefact: a two-rank halo costs marginally \emph{more} than a barrier
because it exchanges two messages to achieve the same rendezvous. So the benefit of the
neighbourhood collective is zero at the bottom of the curve, first becomes positive here, and is
worth almost half the barrier at $p=8$. This run is the point at which the design choice starts
paying, and it is the smallest configuration in which the claim can be tested at all.

\subsection*{The seam mechanism did something here, and the reporting still does not show it}

Three of the six revisions altered the text. Pulling the \texttt{translate:u\emph{n}} and
\texttt{revise:u\emph{n}} results out of \texttt{runs/real-tr-p4-full/blobs} and diffing the
\texttt{translation} field gives 22 characters moved out of 6{,}959: \texttt{revise:u4} moved 4,
\texttt{revise:u5} moved 15, \texttt{revise:u6} moved 3, and \texttt{revise:u1},
\texttt{revise:u2} and \texttt{revise:u3} returned their input byte for byte.

The three edits are not noise, and one pair of them is the best evidence in the campaign that the
exchange does what it was designed to do. All three fall on the last or first paragraph of a
section, which is where the mechanism is aimed. At the $u_5\,|\,u_6$ boundary both sides edited,
and they edited toward each other. Rank 2 rewrote unit 5's closing clause from ``laughed heartily
for a while'' to ``laughed on and on for several minutes''; rank 3 rewrote unit 6's opening verb
of speech and its connective so that the choking laughter it describes reads as continuing rather
than beginning. Each had seen only the other's pre-revision text --- the two calls ran
concurrently, rank 2's from 190.9 to 246.8\,s and rank 3's from 187.4 to 237.5 --- and their own
recorded rationales say so explicitly: rank 3's first \texttt{changes} entry is that the new
wording ``directly continues the laughter at the end of the previous section, so this section does
not read like the opening of a new chapter''. At the $u_3\,|\,u_4$ boundary rank 2 inserted a
four-character connective into unit 4's opening so that the speech picks up the gesture that
closed unit 3. The remaining seam, $u_1\,|\,u_2$, drew no edit from either side. Two of three
reconciled seams were touched.

The \texttt{changes} field remains untrustworthy. The harness records
\texttt{n\_boundary\_changes} of 3, 8, 4 and 3 for the four ranks, eighteen in total, against
three revisions that actually moved a character. Reading the lists shows why: the entries are
descriptions of verification --- ``checked the glossary terms shared with the preceding section
and made no further alteration'' --- because the \texttt{Revision} contract asks for ``a short
list of the boundary or terminology fixes you made'' and the agents answered with the items they
inspected. \texttt{n\_boundary\_changes} therefore counts self-reported inspections, and nothing
in the pipeline diffs the artefacts. Across the series the reported and real counts are 7 against
0 at $p=2$, 18 against 3 here, and 30 against 8 at $p=8$.

What the phase costs is 311.39 rank-seconds of model work plus 64.25 of halo blocking, 375.64 in
total, 30.4\% of the rank-seconds available and work the sequential baseline never performs at
all. Per reconciled seam that is 125.2 rank-seconds here against 157.1 at $p=2$ and 55.9 at
$p=8$, and the number of seams it can reach is 1, 3 and 7 of the book's seven. The four seams
interior to a rank's own allocation --- $u_0\,|\,u_1$, $u_2\,|\,u_3$, $u_4\,|\,u_5$,
$u_6\,|\,u_7$ here --- were produced by separate agent calls with no shared context and are
exactly as raw as an inter-rank seam, and the mechanism never looks at them.

None of this is visible in the quality metrics: coverage 1.000, consistency 1.000,
\texttt{n\_divergent\_entities} 0, rendering verification 1.000 and paragraph fidelity 1.000 at
this point and at every sibling, including \texttt{real-tr-p8-nohalo}, which has no seam
mechanism at all. The campaign has no instrument that would notice whether the 375.64
rank-seconds bought anything.

\subsection*{\texttt{coordination\_share} is short by exactly the halo}

\texttt{metrics.json} reports \texttt{coordination\_share: 0.1625} and
\texttt{coordination\_span\_share: 0.3476}, with \texttt{coordination\_is\_underreported: false}.
The first two are too low and the third should be true.

\texttt{Analysis.collective\_rank\_seconds} sums \texttt{rank\_wall\_s}, which is assembled from
the \texttt{wall\_s} field each \texttt{coll.*} event carries. The \texttt{coll.halo\_exchange}
events in this trace carry only \texttt{\{label, left, right\}}: no \texttt{algorithm}, no
\texttt{rounds}, no \texttt{messages\_sent}, no \texttt{wall\_s}. The metric therefore scores the
halo at zero. Recomputing blocking directly from event timestamps gives 264.66 rank-seconds
against the reported 200.44, and the shortfall of 64.22 is the halo blocking to within 30\,ms.
Corrected, coordination share is 21.5\% rather than 16.3\% and the span share is 45.6\% rather
than 34.8\%. The same correction applied across the series gives 10.0\%, 21.5\% and 19.0\% at
$p=2,4,8$ against the reported 4.1\%, 16.3\% and 16.4\% --- so the corrected figure peaks here
rather than rising monotonically, which is the opposite of the shape the uncorrected numbers
suggest.

\texttt{coordination\_is\_underreported} exists to warn a reader that the blocking figure is
incomplete, but its implementation is \texttt{bool(self.incomplete\_collectives) or self.ok is
False}. It catches the $p=16$ failure mode, where ranks die inside a collective and never record
it. It cannot catch this one, where the collective completed and recorded itself but omitted its
duration. I judge this a real defect in two parts: the instrumentation gap in
\texttt{topology.halo\_exchange}, which the sibling analysis of \texttt{real-tr-p8-full} reports
as closed by commit \texttt{ee21d79} after these traces were taken, and the underreporting flag,
which as written would still read \texttt{false} on any run whose blocking is invisible for a
reason other than failure. Fixing the first does not fix the second for traces already recorded,
and every trace in this campaign predates it.

The generated warning that \texttt{halo\_exchange/?} reports 0 messages against 6 logged is the
visible tip of the same gap and is correctly raised. The fabric logged six messages tagged
\texttt{\_ampi:halo:0:8:R} and \texttt{\_ampi:halo:0:8:L}, which is $2(p-1)$ for a non-periodic
line, and no closed form is registered for the operation, which is why the row shows \modelna\
and Figure~\ref{fig:collectives} omits it.

\subsection*{The calibration warning names the wrong coefficient}

The findings warn that $\alpha$ and $\beta$ here are a median fallback rather than a fit,
``because the regression returned a negative slope ($\beta=0.1682$\,s/token)''. The conclusion is
right and should be heeded --- the published $\alpha=48.517$\,s and $\beta=0.071984$\,s/token are
robust medians, not a model, and no latency prediction should be drawn from them --- but the
reason is not what happened.

Refitting the 22 invocations reproduces the regression exactly: $\beta=+0.168202$\,s/token, a
positive slope of about six output tokens per second, with $\alpha=-13.66$\,s and $R^2=0.624$.
The guard in \texttt{cost.calibrate} is \texttt{if beta > 0 and alpha > 0}, so what failed was the
intercept. The finding text in \texttt{scripts/analyze\_run.py} hard-codes the phrase ``negative
slope'' for every \texttt{median\_fallback} case and then explains it with a queueing story: the
broker wait entering the measured latency but not the token count. That story would indeed
produce a negative slope, and it is the diagnosis \texttt{cost.py}'s docstring records from the
semantic-glossary run, but it does not apply here. Broker queueing is not inside
\texttt{latency\_s} in this run: the 2.6\,s dispatch wait lies between \texttt{enqueue} and
\texttt{claim}, and the reported latency begins at the claim. What the data say is that these
calls are generation at a near-constant rate with no measurable fixed cost, so the honest model
has $\alpha\approx0$ and $\beta\approx1/6$, and least squares over a 210--533 token output range
puts the intercept slightly below zero.

I count this a real if small defect, in two parts. The message misnames the rejected coefficient,
on this run and on $p=1$ and $p=2$. More substantively, the $\alpha>0$ condition rejects a
physically sensible zero-intercept fit, and it does so in a way that inverts the quality ordering
of this series: $p=1$, $p=2$ and $p=4$ are rejected with $R^2$ of 0.697, 0.612 and 0.624, while
$p=8$ is accepted as a least-squares fit with $R^2=0.250$. The only published regression in the
series is the worst of the four.

\subsection*{Six strays in a world of four}

The findings flag ranks 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 14 as registering without participating. The fabric's
\texttt{ranks} table ends with ten rows for \texttt{world\_size = 4}, six of them in state
\texttt{running} with leases renewed to thirty minutes past a job that has already emitted
\texttt{job.finish}. All six appear twice, at incarnations 1 and 2, in two bursts: within the
first four seconds of the run, and again between $t=242$ and $t=249$\,s.

The campaign's explanation --- a shared worker pool, and a worker registering against the wrong
job --- fits, and this run pins down the pool's shape. The stray index set here is
$\{4,\ldots,8,14\}$; \texttt{real-tr-p2-full}'s is $\{2,\ldots,8,14\}$ and
\texttt{real-tr-p8-full}'s is $\{8,14\}$. In each case it is exactly the pool's index set minus
the world, and the pool held indices $0\ldots8$ and 14. Whether AgentMPI accepting these
registrations is a defect is settled in the sibling analysis of \texttt{real-tr-p8-full}, which
traces it to \texttt{RankRuntime.register} performing an unconditional \texttt{INSERT} without
consulting either the \texttt{world\_size} meta key or \texttt{comm\_members}; I agree and will
not repeat the argument. What this run adds is containment evidence at a third world size:
\texttt{comm\_members} holds four rows, the binomial trees addressed only ranks 0--3, the Cartesian
topology was built over four coordinates, the barrier's \texttt{absent} list is empty, and none of
the six strays sent or received a byte. The blast radius was nil, and the reason it was nil is
again that the misdirected indices happened to fall outside the world rather than inside it.

The 31 reattachments the findings report are a different thing and are expected: 25 of them are
the four working ranks turning over a fresh worker process per agent call, which is how this
executor runs, and 6 are the strays' second registration. Rank 2 reaches incarnation 8 because it
made six agent calls.

\subsection*{What this point contributes to the series}

Wall times are 576.74, 410.11, 308.44 and 166.78\,s at $p=1,2,4,8$, so this point's speedup is
$576.74/308.44 = 1.870\times$ at efficiency 0.467 and a Karp--Flatt serial fraction of 0.380 ---
the middle value of a column that reads 0.422, 0.380, 0.188. A Karp--Flatt that falls with $p$ is
the signature of a fixed cost amortising, not of a constant serial section, so the paper's USL
fit of $\sigma=0.220$, $\kappa=0.0000$ describes none of the three points; its residuals have the
sign pattern $-,-,+$ and it over-predicts this point's speedup by 0.54.

Efficiency factorises exactly as $E(p)=(T_1/W(1))\cdot(W(1)/W(p))\cdot E_{\mathrm{par}}(p)$, with
$W(p)$ the model rank-seconds and $E_{\mathrm{par}}$ the achieved parallel efficiency in the
headline. Here that is $1.1131\times0.5718\times0.7345=0.4675$ against the table's 0.47. The
middle term is the work amplification, and it is the largest single contributor: $W(4)=906.20$
rank-seconds against the baseline's $518.16$, a factor of 1.749. Of that, 1.148 is the sixteen
shared calls simply taking longer --- terminology 171.42 rank-seconds against 156.76 and
translation 423.38 against 361.40, on byte-identical terminology prompts --- and the remaining
1.524 is the six extra \texttt{revise} calls. Dividing the amplification out of the whole series gives work-normalised
speedups of 1.000, 1.820, 3.270 and 6.895 and a USL fit of $\sigma=0.025$, $\kappa=0.0000$,
$R^2=0.989$, against the wall-clock fit's 0.873. Most of the reported serial fraction is the
parallel runs doing more work, not waiting longer.

Against its neighbours, this point is the worst of the three parallel configurations by
efficiency and the best by information content. It is the first with a real tree, the first with
a real neighbourhood, the first at which the halo beats a barrier, the first at which the seam
revisions change anything, and the only one whose seam phase is unbalanced. If one point in the
series has to be read closely, this is a better candidate than the endpoints.

\section{Threats to this reading}

\textbf{The counterfactual barrier and the counterfactual balanced revision phase are
arithmetic, not experiments.} That the exchange blocked its ranks for 64.25 rank-seconds and that
the four ranks entered it 33.5\,s apart are measurements. That a barrier would have cost 79.91 is
a calculation which assumes the rest of the schedule is unchanged, and it is not obviously true:
holding rank 0 for a further 15.8\,s would have delayed its \texttt{revise:u1} enqueue, which
might have found a worker faster or slower. The same caveat applies with more force to the claim
that an evenly split revision phase would have finished the run near 270\,s. Both are
order-of-magnitude statements about where the time is, not predictions.

\textbf{The seam edits are three edits, and I am reading Chinese I cannot fully evaluate.} That
\texttt{revise:u5} and \texttt{revise:u6} changed the text is a measurement, and the specific
strings are quoted from the blobs. That the two changes are ``coherent'' --- that they revise
toward one continuous action across the seam --- is my reading of the two paragraphs, and it is
the kind of judgement the trace cannot support. It is also a sample of one seam. The opposite
reading, that the three edits are incidental rewording and the three no-ops are the honest
answer, is not excluded by anything here.

\textbf{Attributing time to the interval that ends it is well defined but is not attributing
cause.} ``Rank 0 was blocked in the barrier for 95.99\,s'' is a measurement; ``rank 0 was blocked
because rank 1 had two revisions to run'' is an inference, well supported by rank 1's
\texttt{revise:u3} completing 0.15\,s before the barrier released, but an inference. The 57.9
rank-seconds of broker dispatch in the same table are not protocol at all and should not be read
as coordination cost, and the 15.5 rank-seconds attributed to the glossary broadcast are mostly
rank 3 waiting for term sheets that had not been written yet.

\textbf{This is a broker-mediated run and the log cannot see inside a worker.}
\texttt{metrics.json} reports \texttt{executors: \{broker: 4, worker: 37\}}, and the outputs are
real Chinese prose with a target-script ratio of 0.857, so this is not a surrogate executor. But
all 22 recorded latencies lie within 30\,ms of a multiple of 0.5\,s, which is what a scripted
executor would also produce; I read it as the broker's \texttt{poll\_s = 0.5} completion poll
because that is its value in the source and no observed observation lag exceeds it.

\textbf{Two of eight output sections are byte-identical to two other runs', the pool was shared,
and the usual explanation does not hold up.} The campaign ran in descending order of $p$ --- $p=8$
at 06:54, this run at 07:03, $p=2$ at 07:10, $p=1$ at 07:19 --- against one persistent worker
pool. Sections 0 and 1 of this run are byte-identical to \texttt{real-tr-p2-full}'s and
\texttt{real-tr-p1-full}'s, and section 0 is also identical to \texttt{real-tr-p8-full}'s, whose
own unrevised translation of it differs and which reached the shared text only after
\texttt{revise:u0} moved seven characters. The campaign's usual verdict on such coincidences is
convergence: a binding 208-term glossary, a pinned paragraph count and an agreed register leave a
near-deterministic decoder little room. That is testable here, and it fails. \texttt{prompt\_terms}
does not take the glossary, so a \texttt{terms:u\emph{n}} prompt is a pure function of the unit
and all four runs issue byte-identical terms prompts --- 8 of 8 digests match. If the executor
were near-deterministic, all 32 results would agree unit by unit; only 6 of the 48 cross-run pairs
do. \texttt{terms:u4} carries prompt digest \texttt{ea002f41} everywhere and returns 36, 38, 38
and 34 terms in 913, 956, 956 and 872 bytes at $p=1,2,4,8$. Identical input does not give
identical output here. What the identities track is placement: over the four runs' 48 unit-pairs
of final output, all 11 pairs in which the unit sat on rank 0 in both runs are byte-identical and
none of the other 37 are. That is what reuse confined to one pool slot looks like. It is not
proof --- a contiguous split puts the lowest-numbered units on rank 0 in every configuration, so
``same rank'' and ``early unit'' cannot be separated within this series, and the shared answers
were generated rather than served, since this run's \texttt{translate:u0} reports 46.02\,s for its
403 output tokens. My reading is dependence between runs rather than convergence. It leaves the
timings, message counts and collective structure in this document untouched, but it means the
quality column of the scaling table is not four independent draws.

\textbf{This point uses a code path $p=2$ does not.} Reduce and broadcast run \texttt{binomial}
here and \texttt{flat} at $p=2$, because the selector is \texttt{p <= 3}. Nothing in this document
depends on the distinction --- at $p=4$ the two would send the same three messages in two rounds
versus one --- but any statement of the form ``the glossary allreduce costs $X$ as a function of
$p$'' drawn across all four points is fitting across an implementation change.
